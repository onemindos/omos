**28 U.S. Code § 2007 - Imprisonment for Debt** addresses the
prohibition on imprisonment for debt in the United States. The provision
states:

**28 U.S. Code § 2007** -- **Imprisonment for Debt**

*\"No person shall be imprisoned for debt in any court of the United
States.\"*

This statute explicitly prohibits the practice of imprisoning
individuals for failing to pay debts in the federal judicial system. The
primary purpose of this law is to ensure that individuals cannot be
jailed solely for being unable to pay financial obligations, which could
include civil debts, such as unpaid loans, credit card bills, or even
child support obligations, under certain conditions.

1.  **Imprisonment for Debt Is Prohibited by the United States
    Constitution**

The **United States Constitution** specifically prohibits the
imprisonment of individuals for debts. The **Fourth Amendment** of the
U.S. Constitution guarantees the right against unreasonable seizures,
while the **Eighth Amendment** prohibits excessive fines and cruel and
unusual punishment, including imprisonment for debt. As the **Supreme
Court** stated in **Bearden v. Georgia, 461 U.S. 660 (1983)**, the state
cannot imprison a person for failing to pay child support unless it can
prove that the individual has the **ability to pay** but willfully chose
not to. In **Bearden**, the Court emphasized that the government must
not punish a person for their inability to pay but must instead assess
whether the individual has willfully failed to comply with child support
orders.

**Bearden v. Georgia, 461 U.S. 660 (1983)** held that imprisonment for
debt is unconstitutional unless there is a determination that the
individual had the **ability to pay** and **willfully refused** to do
so. The Court explained that individuals should not be punished
for **lack of ability to pay** and that due process is required before
incarceration.

2.  **Contempt Orders for Child Support Are Not Grounds for
    Incarceration in the Absence of Willful Non-Payment**

The **Supreme Court** has held that, while child support orders are
enforceable by contempt powers, they cannot be used as a means of
penalizing individuals for their **inability to pay**. The Court
in **Turner v. Rogers, 564 U.S. 431 (2011)** reinforced this principle.
The Court ruled that individuals facing imprisonment for contempt for
child support obligations must be given adequate **due process**,
including the opportunity to demonstrate **inability to pay**. Failure
to provide such due process constitutes a violation of constitutional
rights.

**Turner v. Rogers, 564 U.S. 431 (2011)** held that in civil contempt
cases for child support, the court must first ensure that the
noncustodial parent had the ability to pay the support, and if the
parent cannot, then **imprisonment is not a valid option**. The Court
noted that civil contempt cannot be used as a tool to enforce the
payment of support in cases where the individual does not have the
financial means to comply.

3.  **Contempt and Imprisonment for Non-Payment of Child Support Violate
    the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)**

The **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act** (FDCPA), codified at **15
U.S.C. § 1692 et seq.**, prohibits certain actions by debt collectors.
Although the FDCPA primarily applies to private debt collection
agencies, the principles of the law can be extended to **state
actors** under the **Supremacy Clause**. The enforcement of child
support obligations through imprisonment without the opportunity to
demonstrate inability to pay or otherwise defend against the charge
constitutes an unfair practice under the principles of the FDCPA, which
prohibits the **coercion** of debtors and ensures protections
against **unreasonable and extreme enforcement practices**.

In the case of **Pettit v. Pettit**, 626 N.E.2d 444, the **Indiana
Supreme Court** ruled that a support obligation **reduced** to a **money
judgment** no longer functions as a support obligation but is instead
treated as a **debt**, and such a debt cannot constitutionally support
imprisonment under Indiana law, or under federal law as well.

Specifically, the court held:

-   **\"A support obligation, once reduced to a money judgment, is
    transformed into a debt, and a debt, by its nature, cannot support
    imprisonment.\"**

-   This ruling further reinforced that **imprisonment for non-payment
    of support obligations** is **unconstitutional** when that
    obligation has been **reduced** to a **money judgment**, thus
    transforming it into a **debt**.

*The only debt for which imprisonment is constitutional is debt based on
fraud. Ind. Const. art. I, § 22. Debt based upon any other ground cannot
support imprisonment. It cannot constitutionally be said that a debt
based upon a support obligation can support imprisonment. It has, of
course, properly been held that a support obligation is not a
debt, [Davis v. State (1985), Ind. App., 481 N.E.2d
434,](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=10206622639680894333&q=pettit+v+Pettit&hl=en&as_sdt=2006) and
can support imprisonment. **However, when a support obligation is
\"reduced\" to a money judgment in the form of A shall recover X number
of dollars from B, the support obligation which is \"reduced\" is
transformed into a money judgment, a debt, and the support obligation no
longer exists.** The process for enforcement of a judgment or a decree
for the payment of money is set forth in Ind.Trial Rule 69, and includes
execution and proceedings supplemental. Imprisonment is not part of the
process. If A has X number of dollars and does not satisfy the judgment
against him in favor of B, he may not be imprisoned for contempt of
court for simply holding onto his money, because: 1) the Indiana
constitution forbids it, Ind. Const. art. I, § 22; and 2) a simple money
judgment does not contain a court-imposed obligation, the violation of
which could constitute a contempt of court.*

**Objection, Demand to Set Aside and Dismiss Void Judgment with
Prejudice Due to Coercion, Duress, Extrinsic Fraud, Misrepresentation,
and Violation of Constitutional Principles**

Objection! Come now, I am writing to formally demand the immediate
set-aside of the child support order and related paternity
acknowledgment currently being enforced against me. I assert that the
agreement to acknowledge paternity and submit to child support
obligations was procured under coercion, duress, fraud, and
misrepresentation, which renders the judgment void under applicable law.

**1. Coercion, Duress, and Fraud**\
The circumstances surrounding my submission to the child support
process, and the subsequent agreements I was compelled to make, violated
my constitutional rights under the U.S. Constitution, specifically the
right to due process and protection against coerced or involuntary
contracts.

The Supreme Court in ***Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616 (1886)**
and ***United States v. Throckmorton*, 98 U.S. 61 (1878)** held that
agreements entered into under coercion or duress are inherently void. I
was threatened with severe penalties, including the suspension of my
driver\'s license, income withholding from my employment, and even
incarceration, if I did not provide my private financial information and
consent to forced paternity testing. This conduct constitutes unlawful
coercion and renders any subsequent agreements void.

Additionally, I was misled by my attorney and by child support services
regarding the full consequences of signing the paternity acknowledgment
and agreeing to child support obligations. The information I was
provided was insufficient and inaccurate, depriving me of the
opportunity to make an informed decision, which is a violation of my
rights under ***42 U.S.C. § 1983*** (deprivation of rights under color
of law).

**2. Violation of Separation of Powers**\
The actions taken by the child support system also violate the principle
of separation of powers under the U.S. Constitution. Administrative
agencies have no authority to make judicial determinations regarding
child support without proper judicial oversight. The improper delegation
of judicial powers to the executive branch violates the separation of
powers doctrine and makes any resulting child support orders void.

As noted by the Supreme Court in ***Stanton v. Stanton*, 421 U.S. 7
(1975)**, the judiciary must play a central role in protecting
fundamental rights, such as the right to contest paternity or challenge
child support obligations. The failure of the courts to provide
oversight in this case constitutes a denial of due process and is
inconsistent with constitutional protections.

**3. Lack of Due Process**\
My constitutional right to due process was further violated because I
was not given a meaningful opportunity to present evidence or challenge
the paternity acknowledgment or the child support obligations. In
accordance with ***Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339
U.S. 306 (1950)**, due process requires that individuals be given notice
and an opportunity to be heard before being deprived of their rights.
The failure to provide such notice or opportunity in this matter renders
the judgment void.

**4. Absence of a Voluntary and Informed Agreement**\
A valid contract or agreement must be entered into voluntarily and with
informed consent. The paternity acknowledgment and child support
obligations imposed upon me were neither voluntary nor informed. The
extreme pressure and lack of accurate legal advice deprived me of my
ability to make a free and conscious decision. As established
in ***Horne v. Flores*, 557 U.S. 433 (2009)**, agreements entered into
under unlawful conditions are unenforceable.

**5. Misrepresentation by Attorney**

My attorney, who was entrusted to act in my best interest,
misrepresented the consequences of signing documents acknowledging
paternity and agreeing to child support obligations. This
misrepresentation deprived me of the opportunity to make an informed
decision. The attorney failed to adequately advise me of my rights and
the full scope of the legal consequences of entering into these
agreements. This is further evidence that the child support order was
procured through misrepresentation and coercion.

As held in ***Vitek v. Jones*, 445 U.S. 480 (1980)**, due process
protections require that an individual be provided sufficient legal
representation and a full understanding of their legal rights when
entering into agreements that affect their fundamental rights. The
absence of such protection here further demonstrates that the judgment
should be set aside.

**6. General Appearance Violates Constitutional Rights**

The requirement for me to make a "general appearance" under duress
constitutes a violation of my rights under the Fourteenth Amendment to
the United States Constitution. As established in ***Reynolds v. IAAF*,
23 F.3d 1110 (1994)**, actions that deprive individuals of due process
protections, including the forced appearance under threat of punitive
action, are unconstitutional and cannot form the basis of a lawful
judgment.

**7. Void Judgments Are Void Past, Present, and Future**

Void judgments, such as the one entered in this case, the latter is
still void with all the same attributes of a judgment void on its face.
It\'s a nullity - **[past, present, and future]{.ul}**. All acts
performed under it and all claims flowing out of it are void. This
principle was upheld in ***County of San Diego v. Gorham*, 186
Cal.App.4th 1215 (2010)**, which confirmed that judgments obtained in
violation of due process are void and without legal effect. As the
judgment against me was procured through unlawful means, I am entitled
to challenge it at any time.

**8. Conflict of Interest**

Under ***42 U.S.C. § 658A***, ***Incentive Payments to States*** -
states receive financial incentives for establishing paternity and
enforcing child support orders. This financial interest creates an
unconstitutional conflict that undermines the impartiality required in
adjudicating matters of child support. As the Supreme Court held
in ***Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927)**, a financial interest in the
outcome of a case constitutes a violation of due process and renders
decisions made under such conflicts void. The Child Support Services
Department\'s vested financial interest in this case presents an
impermissible conflict of interest.

The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be deprived
of **life, liberty, or property** without **due process of law**. This
includes both the right to be heard and the right to a **fair trial**.

**9. Violation of 28 U.S. Code § 1691 - Seal and Teste of Process**

**28 U.S. Code § 1691** mandates that all writs and process issuing from
a court of the United States must be under the seal of the court and
signed by the clerk. This statutory requirement was addressed
in ***SCANBE MANUFACTURING COMPANY v. Tryon, 400 F.2d 598 (9th Cir.
1968),*** where the Ninth Circuit held that the failure to comply with
this requirement renders the judgment void. Specifically, in **SCANBE
MANUFACTURING COMPANY**, the court determined that if the writs and
process were not issued under the proper seal and test, the entire
judgment could not stand, as it lacked the necessary procedural
validity.

In my case, the child support order and related paternity acknowledgment
were imposed without proper compliance with **28 U.S. Code § 1691**. The
writs and processes in question were either not issued under the seal of
the court or lacked the proper signatures from the clerk, making the
judgment void from the outset.

**10. Paternity Was Never Established**

The **establishment of paternity** is a key requirement for initiating
a **child support case** in the United States. Both federal law and case
law provide clear guidelines about this requirement. Below are the
relevant sections of the **United States Code (U.S.
Code)** and **Supreme Court case law** that mandate paternity
establishment as a prerequisite for opening a child support case:

**United States Code:**

1.  **42 U.S.C. § 654** - **State Plan Requirements**\
    This section establishes requirements for state child support
    programs to qualify for federal funding. It mandates that states
    must have procedures in place for **establishing paternity** before
    child support obligations are assigned or enforced. Specifically,
    the state must:

    -   **Establish paternity** as a prerequisite to establishing a
        child support obligation (42 U.S.C. § 654(4)(A)).

    -   This includes conducting hearings to determine paternity and,
        where necessary, requiring **genetic testing**.

**42 U.S.C. § 654(4)(A)** provides that a **state child support
plan** must have procedures for the establishment of paternity in all
cases where paternity is in question.

2.  **45 C.F.R. § 303.5** - **Voluntary Paternity Acknowledgment and
    Procedures**\
    This regulation, tied to federal child support enforcement,
    establishes requirements for state agencies involved in the
    voluntary paternity establishment program. It mandates that
    paternity must be legally established before child support orders
    can be issued.

    -   **45 C.F.R. § 303.5** outlines the process by which paternity
        must be **voluntarily acknowledged** or adjudicated by the court
        before a child support case can proceed. This includes the
        necessity of filing paternity acknowledgment with the state
        registry and following specific procedural requirements to
        inform both parties of their rights and responsibilities.

3.  **42 U.S.C. § 666** - **Procedures for Establishing and Enforcing
    Support**\
    Under this section, the federal government requires that states
    establish procedures for **establishing paternity** and enforcing
    child support orders, including the use of **genetic testing** where
    paternity is disputed. These federal procedures apply to all cases
    where a support obligation is being established or enforced,
    including those where paternity is in question.

    -   **42 U.S.C. § 666(a)(5)** requires that states have laws
        providing for the use of genetic testing when paternity is
        contested.

-   **Supreme Court Caselaw**

1.  ***Stanley v. Illinois, 405 U.S. 645 (1972)***\
    In this case, the U.S. Supreme Court held that the state could not
    automatically remove children from the biological father\'s custody
    without providing the father with due process, including
    a **determination of paternity**. The Court emphasized that a father
    has constitutionally protected rights to his children once paternity
    has been legally established.

-   **Stanley v. Illinois** confirmed that a biological father's rights
    and responsibilities, including child support obligations, must
    be **legally established through paternity** before any support or
    custody issues can be determined.

2.  **Michael H. v. Gerald D., 491 U.S. 110 (1989)**\
    This case reinforced the importance of **establishing
    paternity** for child support cases. The U.S. Supreme Court held
    that a man who is not biologically related to a child, but who is
    married to the mother at the time of the child\'s birth, does not
    automatically have paternity rights or obligations unless there has
    been an **establishment of paternity** according to state law.

-   While this case was about the **presumption of paternity** for
    married fathers, it emphasizes the critical point that paternity
    must be legally acknowledged or established in a formal process to
    determine child support responsibilities.

**11. Howlett v. Rose - Federal Law is Enforceable in State Courts**

The Supreme Court in ***Howlett v. Rose, 496 U.S. 356 (1990),***
established that **federal law is enforceable in state courts**. The
Court emphasized that laws passed under the Constitution are as binding
in state courts as those passed by the state legislature. This principle
of federal supremacy, found in **Article VI, Clause 2 of the U.S.
Constitution** (the Supremacy Clause), mandates that state actors,
including state courts and child support services, are bound to uphold
federal law.

The child support order issued against me, along with the procedural
violations identified above, constitutes a clear violation of **federal
law**, and state courts and agencies are obligated to recognize and
enforce these protections. The failure to adhere to **28 U.S. Code §
1691**, coupled with violations of due process rights, demands that the
judgment be set aside.

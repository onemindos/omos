**1. Violation of Due Process Under the 14th Amendment**

The **14th Amendment** to the United States Constitution guarantees that
no state shall deprive any person of **life, liberty, or
property** without **due process of law**. The suspension of my driver's
license for non-payment of child support, without any consideration of
my ability to pay, violates my fundamental rights to **due
process** and **equal protection under the law**. The **U.S. Supreme
Court** has consistently held that government actions affecting
fundamental rights, such as the right to travel, must be subjected to
strict scrutiny and provide due process protections.

In the case of ***Schmerber v. California*, 384 U.S. 757 (1966)**, the
Court reaffirmed that procedural safeguards must exist when the
government seeks to intrude upon fundamental rights. By suspending my
license without allowing me an opportunity to demonstrate my ability to
pay or to seek relief from the suspension, my rights under the **14th
Amendment** have been violated.

**2. Violation of the Full Faith and Credit Clause**

Under **Article IV, Section 1 of the U.S. Constitution**, known as
the **Full Faith and Credit Clause**, states are required to recognize
the judicial proceedings of other states. This includes
the **enforcement of child support orders**. However, it is essential
that such enforcement does not violate the **due process** guarantees
set forth in the **U.S. Constitution**. As demonstrated by the **Supreme
Court** in ***United States v. Windsor*, 570 U.S. 744 (2013)**, any
action that deprives an individual of their rights---whether state or
federal---without due process or just cause is impermissible.

**3. Failure to Follow Required Procedures for License Suspension**

**42 U.S. Code § 666(a)(3)** mandates that state agencies, including
child support enforcement programs, must follow specific procedures in
suspending a parent's driver\'s license for non-payment of child
support. These procedures must include providing notice of the
suspension and an opportunity for the individual to present evidence of
their inability to pay. The **Department of Motor Vehicles** has failed
to demonstrate that such procedures were followed in my case, thus
violating both federal law and my constitutional rights.

Furthermore, under **42 U.S. Code § 654(4)**, states are required to
create and enforce child support orders in a way that **adheres to
federal guidelines** while respecting individuals\' **due process
rights**. The blanket suspension of my license without such procedural
safeguards is therefore not only unconstitutional, but also in violation
of federal law.

**4. Court Precedent Supporting the Restoration of Driving Privileges**

In the case of ***Stinnie v. Holcomb*, 355 F. Supp. 3d 514 (W.D. Va.
2018)**, the court held that **the suspension of driver\'s licenses for
non-payment of child support** without consideration of the
parent's **ability to pay** was unconstitutional. The court found that
such suspensions violated the **14th Amendment's due process clause**,
which guarantees an individual's right to be heard before being
subjected to significant legal penalties.

The **U.S. Supreme Court** also held in ***Tate v. Short*, 401 U.S. 395
(1971)** that states may not impose **punitive sanctions** (such as the
suspension of driving privileges) **without first providing due
process**. This case is particularly relevant because it involved the
deprivation of liberty for an inability to pay, similar to my situation
with the child support enforcement.

In the case of ***Johnson v. Jessup*, 381 F. Supp. 3d 619 - Dist. Court,
MD North Carolina (2019)**, the held that - A finding by this court that
the DMV cannot constitutionally revoke Plaintiffs\' driver\'s licenses
for failure to pay a court-ordered fine or cost without first
determining their ability to pay would not imply that the state court
should not have imposed the fine or cost in the first place.

In the case of ***Wright v. FAMILY SUPPORT DIVISION OF MISSOURI
DEPARTMENT OF SOCIAL SERVICES, Dist. Court, ED Missouri Department of
Social Services (2020),*** the courts held that - Because none of the
available procedures allow Plaintiffs to be heard on their failure to
pay child support and later license suspension, the procedures may not
provide a meaningful opportunity to contest the license suspension.

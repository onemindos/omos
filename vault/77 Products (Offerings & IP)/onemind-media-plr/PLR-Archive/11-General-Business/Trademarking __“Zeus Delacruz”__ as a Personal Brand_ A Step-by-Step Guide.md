Trademarking “Zeus Delacruz” as a Personal
Brand: A Step-by-Step Guide
1. Can You Trademark a Personal Name (and Which Name to Use)?
Yes – personal names can be trademarked in the U.S. if they’re used to identify your goods or services in
commerce. Many famous brands are just people’s names. Under U.S. law, a full name (first and last name
together) is generally considered distinctive enough to function as a trademark without special proof 1 .
This is in contrast to a surname alone, which usually requires proof of secondary meaning (i.e. that
consumers associate that name with your brand) 2 . In other words, “Zeus Delacruz” (first + last name)
can inherently serve as a brand name 1 , provided no one else in your field has a confusingly similar name
trademarked 3 .


When deciding whether to trademark “Zeus Delacruz” or the longer “Zeus Legacy Delacruz,” consider
how you present yourself professionally. Both forms can be protected, but it’s often best to trademark the
name you consistently use in branding. For example, if clients and audiences know you simply as Zeus
Delacruz, that shorter name might be your primary trademark. On the other hand, if “Zeus Legacy
Delacruz” is the name you use on books, websites, or marketing materials, you might choose that for your
trademark. The shorter name is usually easier for branding (and less prone to typos), but the full name
including “Legacy” could be more unique. You won’t lose rights by going with the shorter version – a
trademark on Zeus Delacruz would likely protect against others using Zeus L. Delacruz in your industry due to
similarity. If in doubt, you can file separate applications for each version, but this doubles the cost. Most
personal brands pick one version and use it consistently for all business and marketing.


Important: If your trademark includes the name of a living person (which it does – you), the USPTO
requires that person’s written consent to register the name 4 . This rule exists to protect individual rights
of privacy and identity. In practice, if you’re the one applying for your own name, you’ll simply need to affirm
that and possibly provide a short consent statement (the online form typically asks, “Does this mark identify
a living individual?” – you’d answer yes and type your consent). Because it’s your name, consent is usually
presumed in the application process 5 . Just be prepared to sign a statement (or check a box) confirming “I
consent to the use and registration of my name Zeus Delacruz as a trademark.” Without that consent,
the USPTO will refuse registration 6 . Also, ensure your name trademark won’t falsely suggest a
connection with someone else famous – e.g. trademarking “Michael Jordan” if that’s not you would be
rejected 3 . In your case, Zeus Delacruz is unique to you, so this shouldn’t be an issue.


Bottom line: You can trademark your personal name. Decide whether to use the shorter or full version
based on branding consistency – either “Zeus Delacruz” or “Zeus Legacy Delacruz” can be protected. Just
remember that whichever version you choose, you should use exactly that name as your brand on your
materials to build trademark rights. (You can still use your full name in bios or legal documents, but the
trademark protects the specific phrasing you register.)

2. How to Register Your Name with the USPTO
Trademarking your name involves filing an application with the United States Patent and Trademark Office
(USPTO). Below are the key steps to register “Zeus Delacruz” (or your chosen variant) as a trademark:


    1. Prepare and Research Your Name: Before filing, do a trademark search to make sure no one else
       has a similar name registered in the financial or coaching services fields. The USPTO will reject an
       application if an existing trademark is identical or confusingly similar to yours in a related industry
         7 . Search the USPTO’s TESS database for “Zeus Delacruz” and similar terms. Look for any marks

       that sound or look like your name, especially in classes related to finance or coaching. If you find a
       conflict (e.g. “Zeus Financial Coaching, LLC” trademarked), you might need to tweak your branding or
       consult an attorney. If the coast is clear, you’re good to proceed. (USPTO’s own guidance suggests
       checking for similar trademarks before you apply 8 .)


    2. Choose the Goods/Services (Scope of Protection): In your application, you must specify the goods
       and services you will offer under the name. For a personal brand in financial services and
       coaching, this means describing your services in the proper legal terms. For example, you might list
       services such as “Financial consulting and advisory services” (which falls under International Class 36
       for financial matters) and “Personal coaching services in the field of personal finance and professional
       development” (likely Class 41 for education and training) 9 . The USPTO has a Trademark ID
       Manual of pre-approved wording – using those can simplify your filing 10 . You could find entries
       like “Financial consulting” or “Life coaching services” and add detail about the field. Using the ID
      Manual terms ensures your application meets the USPTO’s requirements (and avoids extra fees for
      custom descriptions) 10 11 . Be thorough in covering the services you plan to offer (coaching,
      webinars, online courses, financial planning, etc.), but don’t list services you won’t actually
      provide, as you’ll eventually need to show proof of use for each.


    3. Decide on the Application Format: You’ll need to choose the format of your trademark on the
       application. For a personal name, the simplest and strongest choice is a standard character mark,
       which protects the word itself (“Zeus Delacruz”) in any style or font 12 . This means your name is
       protected regardless of how it’s stylized. The other option – a special form – is if you had a specific
       logo or signature stylization of your name, but that limits protection to that exact design 12 . Unless
       you have a distinct logo for your name, select “Standard Characters” and enter the name exactly as
       you use it (e.g. ZEUS DELACRUZ ). Tip: Use all caps in the application for a standard character mark
      (the USPTO formats it that way, even though it covers upper/lower case usage).


    4. Start the Online Application (TEAS): Go to the USPTO’s Trademark Electronic Application System
       (TEAS) via the USPTO Trademark Center online 13 . You’ll need to log in with a USPTO.gov account
       (you can create one on the spot). Choose “Apply to register a trademark.” The system will ask if
       you want TEAS Plus or TEAS Standard. (As of early 2025, the USPTO is shifting to a single application
       form with a base fee, but essentially it’s similar to the old TEAS Standard process 14 15 .)
       Historically, TEAS Plus had a lower fee but required using pre-approved goods/services language
       and extra upfront info, whereas TEAS Standard cost a bit more but gave flexibility 16 . Under the
       new rules effective Jan 2025, there’s a base application fee of \$350 per class for all electronic
       applications 15 . If you use all correct IDs and info, you just pay that base fee. (If you stray and use
       custom descriptions or leave info out, the USPTO may impose small surcharges of \$100-200 later
         11 , so it’s best to do it right the first time.) In short, be prepared to pay around \$350 per class of

  could be about \$700 total in USPTO fees. Have a credit/debit card ready for the payment at the end
  of the form.


5. Fill in Owner and Correspondence Info: You (or your business entity) will be listed as the owner of
   the trademark. In the application, enter the owner’s name. This can be your personal name (since
   it’s your personal brand) or a company you own if you prefer the company to hold the trademark.
   Many individuals starting out just list themselves as an Individual owner 17 . Provide your mailing
   address and an email that you will check (the USPTO will send official correspondence via email).
   Note that your address will become public record, so some people use a business or P.O. box address
   for privacy. Double-check the spelling of everything here.


6. Identify the Mark (Name) and Its Significance: Enter the name exactly as your trademark (e.g.
    Zeus Delacruz or Zeus Legacy Delacruz ). The form might ask if this wording has any
  meaning or if it’s a name. You should disclose that “Zeus Delacruz” is the name of a living individual
  (yourself). The USPTO will require a statement of consent from that individual 4 . Since you are that
  person, you can write a simple consent: “Zeus Delacruz is the name of a living individual whose consent
  is of record.” Usually, typing your name as the signatory on the form and checking the consent box
  fulfills this requirement 5 . If there’s a section to upload a written consent, you can upload a short
  signed letter stating you consent to your name’s use. This step ensures no one else can register your
  personal name without permission 4 .


7. Specify Your Services (Goods/Services Description): As prepared in step 2, input the description of
   your services. For example, you might add two entries if you have two classes: one for Class 36 –
   “Financial consulting and advisory services” and one for Class 41 – “Professional coaching services in the
   field of personal finance and business success”. If you used the ID Manual, you can often select a pre-
   written description that matches what you do (e.g., “Financial consulting” is a standard term). The
   TEAS form allows you to search the ID Manual right inside it. Selecting the USPTO pre-approved
   descriptions not only helps ensure accuracy but also avoids a new 2025 rule that charges \$200 extra
   per class for using a custom free-form description 11 . Keep your descriptions clear and specific to
   financial and coaching services so the scope of your trademark is well-defined. (Remember, once
   you submit the application, you cannot broaden the scope of services later 18 . You can drop items, but
   you can’t add new categories, so include everything relevant now.)


8. Choose the Filing Basis (Use vs. Intent to Use): The USPTO needs to know if you’re already using
   the name in commerce or if you intend to use it soon. If you have already been using “Zeus
   Delacruz” as a brand (e.g. you’ve been offering coaching under that name, have a website or social
   media promoting your services under that name, etc.), you’ll likely file under “Section 1(a) – Use in
   Commerce.” This means you’ll have to provide a specimen (example) showing the name in use (see
   next step) and the date of first use. If you haven’t started offering services under the name yet,
   choose “Section 1(b) – Intent to Use.” An intent-to-use (ITU) application lets you secure a priority
   date now and actually submit proof of use later, up to a few years later. (After the application is
   examined and approved, you’d file a Statement of Use with specimens and a fee to complete
   registration 19 20 .) Most likely, if you’re launching your brand now, you might be in intent-to-use
   phase. That’s fine – just note that you cannot finalize the trademark registration until you do start
   using the name and show proof. Intent-to-use does incur an additional \$100 per class fee at the
   time you file your Statement of Use, so budget for that down the road.

    9. Upload Your Specimen (Proof of Use): This step applies only if filing under “Use in
       Commerce” (1a). A specimen is basically an example of your name being used commercially. For
       services, an acceptable specimen is often a screenshot of a website or social media page advertising
       your coaching or financial services under the name, a brochure or flyer, or an advertisement. It
       should show the name Zeus Delacruz in a manner customers would perceive as a brand for those
       services (for instance, on your website’s header: “Zeus Delacruz – Financial Coach”). If you have a
       booking page or a client agreement with the branding, that could work too. You’ll upload an image
       (JPEG/PDF) and possibly a brief description like “Screenshot of applicant’s website showing the mark
       in connection with offering of financial coaching services.” Ensure the specimen clearly links the
       name to the service (a standalone name by itself isn’t enough – it should be tagging the service). If
       you’re filing intent-to-use, you can skip specimen for now 19 ; you’ll provide it later when you
       actually have one.


   10. Sign and Submit the Application: Almost done! You’ll be asked to electronically sign the
       application. If you’re filing yourself, you as the owner can sign. This is a declaration that everything is
       true and that to your knowledge you have the right to the trademark. Once signed, you proceed to
       the payment screen. Pay the required fee (e.g. \$350 × number of classes, so likely \$700 if two
       classes). After submission, you should receive an immediate confirmation and a serial number for
       your application.


   11. Track and Follow Up: With the serial number, you can check the status of your application via the
       USPTO’s TSDR (Trademark Status and Document Retrieval) system or simply use the USPTO status
       search. The USPTO will also email the filing receipt to you. Now, prepare to be patient: it typically
       takes about 3 months for a USPTO examining attorney to review a new application 21 . If everything
       is in order, you won’t hear much until then. The examiner will look for any issues (conflicts, improper
       descriptions, etc.). You might receive an Office Action letter if something needs correction or
       clarification. Common issues for personal name marks might include: a request for the consent form
       (if not provided), clarification of services wording, or a refusal if they found a similar name. Don’t
       panic if you get an Office Action – it’s fairly normal; you’ll have 3 months to respond (or 6 months
       with an extension, though extensions now require a fee). If no significant issues arise, the examiner
       will approve the mark for publication.


   12. Publication and Registration: When approved, your trademark is published in the USPTO’s Official
       Gazette for a 30-day period 22 . This gives others a chance to oppose if they believe they’d be
       harmed by your registration (oppositions are relatively rare, especially for personal name marks in
       niche industries – likely not a concern unless someone with a similar name in finance objects). If no
       one opposes (or you overcome an opposition), the USPTO will move to register your mark. If you
       filed under use in commerce, registration happens fairly quickly after publication (certificates are
       issued a few weeks later). If you filed under intent-to-use, the USPTO will issue a Notice of
       Allowance after publication, and you then must submit your Statement of Use (with specimens) and
       fee to get the registration certificate. You’ll have 6 months from the Notice of Allowance to file proof
       of use, and you can extend that if needed (for a fee) up to a total of 3 years. Once your trademark is
       fully registered, congratulations – you can now use the ® symbol next to your name for the services
       covered, and you have nationwide protection in the U.S.


Throughout this process, make use of official USPTO resources. The USPTO’s Trademark Basics and
Process Overview guides are helpful for first-timers, and the online forms have info icons explaining each

3. Requirements for Trademarking Your Name in Financial Services
& Coaching
Trademarking a personal name in the financial and coaching industry follows the same legal process as
any trademark, but there are a few industry-relevant tips to keep in mind:


     • Choose the Right Classes and Descriptions: Financial services and coaching are distinct categories
       – make sure your application covers both if you need it. As noted, International Class 36 is for
       financial, banking, insurance, and monetary services (this would cover things like investment advice,
       financial planning, wealth coaching, etc.), and Class 41 is for education and training services
       (covering personal coaching, seminars, online courses, workshops, etc.) 9 . Review your exact
       offerings. For example, if you plan to do one-on-one financial coaching and also publish educational
       content or courses, Class 41 is important. If you provide specific financial advisory or consulting (like
       helping clients with budgeting or investments), Class 36 is appropriate. It’s better to cover both
       classes if your brand will span both areas. You cannot simply list “financial coaching” and assume it
       covers everything – you should break it down (the USPTO may even require you to clarify if
       something is education vs financial service). Using multiple classes will increase your filing fee, but it
       ensures your name is protected in all relevant facets of your business.


     • Avoid Descriptive or Generic Terms: Ensure that your name itself isn’t descriptive of the services.
       This isn’t an issue with “Zeus Delacruz” – it’s a personal name, not a generic term. But be cautious if
       you add any taglines or try to include a title. For instance, “Zeus Delacruz Coaching” could be
       acceptable, but “Zeus Delacruz Financial Advisor” as a mark might face issues because “Financial
       Advisor” is a generic term for the service. Generally, the USPTO will let you trademark your name
       plus a descriptive term, but they might require a disclaimer for the descriptive part (meaning you
       don’t have exclusive rights to the words “Financial Advisor”). If you were to include something like
       that, be prepared for a disclaimer requirement. Often, just the name alone is stronger and cleaner as
       a trademark.


     • Show Use in the Right Context: When it comes time to provide a specimen of use, make sure it
       aligns with the services in these industries. For financial services, a specimen could be a snapshot
       of your business’s website offering financial advice or a promotional brochure where “Zeus
       Delacruz®” is branded as the provider of financial consulting. For coaching, it might be a screenshot
       of a page advertising your coaching programs or a social media business page that lists your
       services. The key is that your name is being used in a brand capacity – e.g., “Zeus Delacruz –
       Certified Life Coach and Financial Consultant” on the site header, or on a banner of a speaking event.
       If you only ever use your name in a purely personal context (like just an author byline or a resume
       listing), that won’t count. You need to use the name outwardly in commerce – on marketing
       materials, service offerings, etc., which is usually natural when building a personal brand.

     • Regulatory Considerations (Not Trademark-Specific): While not directly related to trademark
       registration, remember that financial services is a regulated field. Ensure that using your personal
       name as a brand complies with any industry regulations or licensing rules. For example, if you are
       offering investment advisory services, you might need to be registered or follow certain guidelines
       when marketing yourself. Using a personal name is fine, but avoid any misleading titles in your
       branding (trademarking “Zeus Delacruz Wealth Management” might imply you are a registered
       investment adviser or firm – make sure that’s accurate if you use such terms). From a pure
       trademark perspective, the USPTO doesn’t police compliance with finance laws, but they do refuse
       marks that are blatantly misleading or descriptive (e.g. you couldn’t trademark “Zeus Delacruz CPA” if
       you’re not actually a CPA, because that’s a professional designation). So, stick to truthful use of your
       credentials in the specimens.


     • Acquire Distinctiveness if Needed: If your name were very common (like “John Smith”) in a given
       industry, sometimes it can be harder to trademark without proof that consumers recognize that
       name as you specifically. In your case, Zeus Delacruz is quite unique. It’s unlikely the USPTO will deem
       it “primarily just a surname” or too common. If “Delacruz” were very common, at worst they could
       initially view it as a surname, but since you are using the full name with “Zeus,” you benefit from the
       rule that first name + last name is inherently distinctive enough 1 . Thus, you shouldn’t need to
       show any special evidence of distinctiveness at the start. Over time, as you use the mark, it will gain
       even more recognition. Just be aware that personal name marks in general build strength through
       use – the more you promote Zeus Delacruz as a brand, the stronger your case if you ever need to
       enforce it.


     • Consent and Accuracy: Because the mark is your name, ensure everything matches up. The USPTO
       may ask in an Office Action, “Does this mark identify a particular living individual?” You’ll respond yes,
       that’s you, and that you’ve given consent. It’s a formality, but make sure to handle it. Also, sign your
       name consistently – e.g., if your legal name has “Legacy” and you chose to trademark the shorter
       version, you might sign the consent as Zeus Legacy Delacruz (full legal name) stating consent to use
       Zeus Delacruz. Little consistency details help avoid any confusion during examination.


     • Consider a Service Mark vs. Trademark: Technically, because you are offering services (not a
       physical product), your name will function as a service mark (which is just a subtype of trademark
       for services). There’s no extra step for this – on the application you’ll specify services and the USPTO
       automatically treats it as a service mark. Just a terminology note: you might see “TM” vs “SM” – you
       can use “SM” (service mark) if you want before registration, but “TM” is often used universally. After
       registration, you’ll use the ® symbol regardless. The point is, protect your name for services, which
       you are doing by clearly listing your coaching and financial consulting in the application.


In summary, trademarking your name in finance and coaching means accurately defining your services
in those fields, using your name in a branding capacity for those services, and meeting the same
requirements (distinctiveness, non-confusion) that any mark would. The process and rules are
fundamentally the same as any trademark – just applied to your personal brand context. As long as you
follow the process from Section 2 and use the name in your business, you’ll satisfy the requirements. Many
financial coaches and advisors trademark their names to build a unique brand (think of Dave Ramsey®,
Tony Robbins®, etc., who operate in personal finance or self-development – those are personal names that
have become protected brand names). You are on the right track doing the same with Zeus Delacruz.

4. Securing Your Brand Name on Social Media Platforms
Once you have (or even before you finalize) your trademark, it’s crucial to claim your name on all major
social media platforms. This is an important part of building and protecting your personal brand. Here are
the steps and best practices to do so:


     • Grab the Username Early: On each major platform – Instagram, Twitter (X), Facebook, TikTok,
       LinkedIn, YouTube, and any others relevant to your audience (e.g., Pinterest, Threads, Medium) –
       create an account or page using your brand name exactly or as close to it as possible. Ideally, your
       handle should be @ZeusDelacruz (or a variant like @ZeusLegacyDelacruz if you went with the full
       name). Even if you’re not ready to actively use a platform, reserve the name now. Social media handles
       are first-come, first-served, and you don’t want an impostor or someone with a similar name taking
       yours 23 24 . It’s free to register these accounts, and securing them prevents confusion later.


     • Use a Consistent Branding Across Platforms: Try to use the same username and profile name on
       all platforms for consistency. This makes it easier for people to find you and recognize your brand. If
       ZeusDelacruz is taken somewhere, consider a simple variant that still identifies you – for example,
       @ZeusDelacruzOfficial or @RealZeusDelacruz or adding an initial like @ZeusLDelacruz. (Avoid
       random numbers or underscores if possible, as they look less professional.) Consistency helps build
       your brand recognition.


     • Utilize Social Media Claiming Tools: There are online tools (like Namechk, KnowEm, etc.) that let
      you search a bunch of platforms at once for username availability. These can save time – you enter
      “ZeusDelacruz” and see which sites have it available. However, even manually checking each major
      site is worth the effort for something this important. Once you know what’s free, register them
      immediately.


     • Create Complete Profiles: When you sign up on each platform, fill out the profile info to match your
       brand. Use your name “Zeus Delacruz” as the account name (even if the handle has a slight
       variation). Use a consistent profile photo or logo (could be your portrait or a brand logo if you have
       one). In the bio, you can mention your role (e.g., Financial Coach and Advisor) so people know it’s you.
       Even if you don’t plan to post content on some platforms right away, having a placeholder with a link
       to your main website or “Coming soon” is better than leaving it blank. This also stakes out your
       presence.


     • Domain Name and Website: Along with social media, don’t forget to secure your domain name.
       Try to get zeusdelacruz.com (and maybe other variants like .net or .coach if relevant).
      Owning the domain that matches your brand name is important for professionalism and protection.
      Even if you just set up a simple landing page, it prevents others from using that domain. (Domains
      aren’t trademarks by themselves, but not having your domain can be a marketing disadvantage.)


     • Monitor and Protect Your Handles: After claiming your accounts, keep track of them. Even if you’re
       not actively using a platform, log in occasionally or keep the email/phone updated so you don’t lose
       the account to inactivity. Use strong, unique passwords and enable two-factor authentication to
       prevent hacking – your personal name accounts are part of your brand identity and should be
       secured like assets.

Remember, social media handles are valuable brand assets in today’s world. By claiming them early, you
prevent impostors or competitors from diluting your brand or misleading your audience 24 27 . Even if

your “future self” will be glad you did it 28 . It’s much easier to reserve a name now than to try to win it back
later. So, as soon as possible, lock down “Zeus Delacruz” everywhere you can online.

5. Legal Considerations, Fees, and Ongoing Obligations for
Maintaining a Personal Name Trademark
Obtaining the trademark is only the first step – you also need to maintain and protect it over the long
term. Here’s what to keep in mind regarding legal duties, costs, and obligations after you’ve filed and
registered your personal name trademark:


     • Initial Costs and Professional Help: The USPTO filing fees are a primary cost. As discussed, expect
       around \$350 per class (at minimum) for the application 15 . In your case, if covering two classes,
       that’s about \$700 in government fees. If you hired a trademark attorney or a service to assist, factor
       in their fees as well (these can range widely, perhaps \$800 to \$1500 or more for a straightforward
       single-class application, but this is optional). The USPTO fees are nonrefundable even if your
       application is refused, so doing it correctly is important. Budget for the possibility of an additional \
       $100 per class later if you filed intent-to-use (for the Statement of Use). Also, if any Office Action
       requires changes, you might incur minor costs (e.g., if you need an extension of time or to appeal a
       refusal, there are fees, but hopefully it won’t come to that).


     • Trademark Ownership and Structure: Decide whether you personally or an LLC/company will
       own the trademark. Many individuals start with personal ownership and later assign the trademark
       to an LLC once their business is formally established. There’s a small fee to record an assignment if
       you transfer it down the line. Owning it personally is fine if this is your personal brand business. Just
       know that whichever name/address you listed as owner is public. If you move or change name, you
      should update the USPTO records (via an address change or assignment record) to keep it current,
      though that’s a straightforward online filing.


     • Using the Trademark Properly: Use it or lose it. U.S. trademarks must be continuously used in
       commerce. After registration, you must continue to use “Zeus Delacruz” in connection with the
       services you listed. Non-use for too long (usually 3 years of non-use with intent not to resume) is
       grounds for cancellation for abandonment. So keep promoting your brand and selling your services
       under that name. If you ever stop offering coaching or financial services under that brand, you could
       lose rights. (If you pivot to other services, you might need a new application for those – trademarks
       protect the specific goods/services listed.) Always use the mark in a consistent manner – minor
       variations are okay (like with or without a middle name in casual use), but the core brand name
       should appear as registered on your materials. After your mark is registered, you should start using
       the ® symbol next to it whenever feasible (on your website footers, business cards, etc.) to put the
       public on notice of your registration. Before registration is granted, you can use ™ (e.g., Zeus
       Delacruz™) to signal you claim rights. While not mandatory, using these symbols is a good practice.


     • Enforcing Your Rights: The USPTO gives you the registration, but enforcement is up to you 29 .
       This means you should monitor the marketplace for any potential infringements – e.g. if someone
       else starts offering similar services under a confusingly similar name. The good news is, with a
       unique name like yours, infringement is less likely, but stay vigilant. You can set up Google Alerts for
       “Zeus Delacruz” or use trademark watch services to catch new applications that might conflict. If you
       do find someone using your name (or a very close variant) in the financial or coaching space without
       permission, you may need to send a cease and desist letter or take legal action if necessary. Having
       a federal trademark makes such enforcement much easier. Also, watch for any new trademark filings




                                                      9
 in the USPTO database that might conflict – during the publication stage of others’ marks, you could
 oppose them based on your prior rights. Many brand owners also register their name with the
 USTPO’s Trademark Clearinghouse or other services if they worry about domains (especially if you
 ever consider international trademarks). This level might be beyond initial scope, but just note that
 trademark ownership comes with the responsibility to “police” your mark. If you passively allow
 others to use a similar name for too long, you risk your mark becoming weakened.


• Renewal and Maintenance Fees: Unlike copyrights or patents, trademarks can last indefinitely as
  long as you keep up with maintenance. In the U.S., you have to periodically file documents and pay
  fees to maintain your registration. The key deadlines are:


• 5-year Maintenance (Section 8 Declaration): Between the 5th and 6th year after your registration
  date, you must file a Section 8 Declaration of Use (or Excusable Non-use) 30 . This filing is basically
  you swearing that “Yes, I’m still using this trademark on the services” and providing a current
  specimen of use. There is a fee per class for this (currently \$225 per class, rising to \$325 in 2025)
   31 . If you miss this, your registration will be canceled. The window opens at 5 years and you have

  until the 6th-year anniversary to do it (there’s also a 6-month grace period with an extra fee if you
  miss the window) 32 .
       ◦ Section 15 (Incontestability): At the same time as the 5-year filing, you can (optional) file a
           Section 15 Declaration of Incontestability, if you have 5 years of continuous use. This,
           when granted, strengthens your mark by limiting certain defenses against it. There’s a fee
           (currently \$200, $250 in 2025) for Section 15 31 , and you usually file it together with the
           Section 8 (there’s a combined form). This is highly recommended for a brand you plan to
           keep, as it gives you “incontestable” status – basically others can no longer claim your mark
           isn’t distinctive or challenge it on some grounds after that point.
• 10-year Renewal (Section 9 + another Section 8): Between the 9th and 10th year after
  registration, you must file a combined Section 8 + Section 9 filing 33 . Section 9 is the actual
  Renewal Application – it extends your registration for another 10-year period. Section 8 again
  reconfirms use. The fee for the 10-year renewal is currently \$300 per class (increasing to \$325) 31 ,
  plus the Section 8 fee again (so figure a few hundred dollars per class in total). After that, every 10
  years from the registration date, the same renewal (8+9) is required each decade 34 . These are
  essentially lifetime maintenance filings.

• The USPTO usually sends courtesy email reminders for these filings if your contact email is up to
  date 35 , but you are responsible for tracking it. Mark your calendar far in advance. Many trademark
  owners set reminders or use their attorneys’ docket systems for this. Missing a maintenance
  deadline can completely invalidate your registration.


• Continued Use and Specimens: With each maintenance filing, you need to submit a specimen
  showing current use of the mark on the services 36 37 . This means at year 5 and year 10 (and
  each renewal) you’ll want to have recent evidence that “Zeus Delacruz” is still being used as your
  brand – e.g., an updated screenshot of your active coaching website or a current advertisement. If
  you’ve expanded or changed your services, ensure you still use the name for at least one of the
  services in each class that you initially registered. If you stopped offering something listed, you must
  delete it from the registration at renewal. It’s better to delete a service you no longer use than to
  claim use and not have proof – the USPTO can cancel your whole mark if you’re caught not using it
  for claimed services. They even do random audits on some percentage of renewals 38 . So keep your
  registration accurate; you can amend it to remove services you dropped via a Section 7 request



                                                10
 anytime 39 40 . In short, only renew for the services you are actively using the name with, and
 provide a specimen for each class.


• Costs of Maintenance: As noted, the fees for maintenance are a few hundred dollars each time. To
  give a rough idea: the 5-year (Sec.8) filing is \$225 per class currently 41 (will be \$325), the 10-year
  (Sec.8+9) combined is \$525 per class currently (300+225, will become 325+325 = \$650) if filed on
  time. These can change, so always check the USPTO fee schedule when your deadlines approach.
  Compared to the initial effort of registering, maintaining is infrequent and not too expensive, but it’s
  absolutely essential.


• Legal Use of ® and TM: After your mark registers, start using the ® symbol in a superset of ways:
  on your website header, business card, social media bio, etc., where the name is mentioned in a
  brand sense. This gives notice that the name is registered and deters others from arguing they didn’t
  know. Before registration, you could use “™” (for instance, Zeus Delacruz™) to signal you consider it a
  trademark. While not required by law, using these symbols is a good habit. Note: do not use ® until
  the mark is officially registered (after the certificate is issued). Using ® too early (during the
  application phase) is technically improper and could cause issues.


• Geographic Scope and Expansion: A U.S. trademark protects your name within the United States.
  If you plan to offer services internationally or build a global brand, consider registering your name in
  other key countries or regions (for example, Canada, EU, UK, Australia, etc.). This can often be done
  after you get the U.S. registration (some countries allow you to use the U.S. registration as a basis).
  There are international treaties (Madrid Protocol) that allow a somewhat streamlined process to
  extend protection overseas. This is a more advanced step – if your coaching business goes global,
  you may want to consult an attorney about foreign trademarks. But at minimum, registering in the
  U.S. gives you a strong base and priority date.


• Beware of Scams: After your application or registration becomes public, you might start receiving
  official-looking mails or emails that are actually scams (soliciting fees for fake registry listings, etc.).
  The USPTO warns about these. Generally, any correspondence about your trademark will come from
  the USPTO (uspto.gov domain) or your attorney, not from random third parties. If you get mail
  asking for additional fees to “secure your trademark in a private database” – ignore or verify with
  your attorney. There are many trademark scam letters that target new filers.


• Protecting the Brand in Marketing: From a marketing perspective, now that you’re making “Zeus
  Delacruz” a protected brand, use it prominently and consistently. Build goodwill under that name.
  You can mention “Zeus Delacruz® is a registered trademark of [Your Name/Company]” in small
  print on your site if you want to be extra clear. This kind of notice isn’t legally required, but it can
  further establish your claim. If you license your brand (say you allow someone to use your name or
  partner with another coach under your brand), make sure to do it with proper agreements, so you
  maintain control over how your name is used – but that’s only if you ever go that route.


• Backup Plan – What If USPTO Refuses? In the unlikely event your application doesn’t go through
  (for example, if an examiner surprisingly found conflict or considered the name too surname-y), you
  often have the chance to argue your case or amend the application. Worst case, you might have to
  move the mark to the Supplemental Register (used for certain names/descriptive marks) initially
  and then later get to the Principal Register after proving distinctiveness. This scenario is not




                                                  11
       expected for your unique name, but I mention it so you know a refusal isn’t necessarily the end. You
       could also refile with a slight variation or logo if needed. An attorney can help if any complex refusal
       arises. Again, this is just a consideration; expect success if properly filed.


     • Continuing Business Obligations: Keep in mind, owning a trademark doesn’t exempt you from
       needing proper business licenses or following industry laws (e.g., if you’re doing financial advising,
       ensure compliance with SEC/state regulations). The trademark is about your brand name, not a
       business license. So maintain your business in good standing (LLC if you have one, etc.), as
       sometimes if a business dissolves and no one’s using the mark, that could complicate things. But as
       long as you continue the business, the mark lives with you.


In conclusion, maintaining a personal name trademark like “Zeus Delacruz” means continuing to use the
name in commerce, keeping up with USPTO filing deadlines, and safeguarding your brand’s integrity.
Budget for periodic fees (the 5-year and 10-year maintenance) as part of your business expenses. The
benefit is that your trademark can last forever – unlike a patent or even your initial business registration, it
doesn’t expire as long as you renew it and use it. This can become a very valuable asset: your name, as a
brand, could even be licensed or expanded into products (if one day you write a book or have a product
line, you might trademark the name on those too). Always use the trademark in a consistent, quality-
associated way – the law requires you to not let it become misleading or generic. But given it’s your
personal name, the main risk (genericide) isn’t really a concern – Zeus Delacruz won’t turn into a common
noun.


By following these guidelines – registering properly, locking down your name on social media, and staying
on top of legal formalities – you will solidify “Zeus Delacruz” as a strong personal brand in the financial and
coaching industries. Good luck with your brand, and welcome to the world of trademark owners! With your
name protected, you can confidently build your business and reputation around it for years to come.

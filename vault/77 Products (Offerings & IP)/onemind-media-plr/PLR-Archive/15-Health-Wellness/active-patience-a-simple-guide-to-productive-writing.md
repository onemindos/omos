                                      Introduction
“Many a false step was made by standing still.”
   - Fortune Cookie

     Are you struggling and frustrated with writing your book? Do you constantly start
writing projects only to never finish them? Are you disappointed in yourself when you
don't finish on time—or ever?
     If this sounds familiar, know that you are not alone. This book shows you how and
why you should use Active Patience with all your creative endeavours and never be
disappointed again. Change your approach and you will overcome procrastination and all
the other creative battles.
     I have started many projects only to put them aside and not look at them again for
years. I have taken career breaks and sabbaticals and still not finished one creative
project. I have disrespected and betrayed my natural ability and genuine passion for
fifteen years. I locked myself up in a cubicle job for years with secret dreams of having
one day a creative career. I have started blogs and abandoned them while still paying for
the hosting in the hope that I would one day go back to them. I have saved writing
competitions, intending to enter them, and not followed through on story ideas. I have
thought about writing more than I have written.
     I have experienced shame, anxiety, depression, failure and devastation at not
achieving what I wanted to achieve. I have existed as a zombie and lived only in my
dreams.
     When I turned twenty-eight in my hometown of Melbourne, I felt like an absolute
failure. The whole writing thing wasn't working out for me. With a stop-start, over-
analytical mind, I had little to show for all my suffering. I wasn't producing. I wanted to. I
thought about it constantly. The more I quit, abandoned or put things on hold, the more
impatient I got with myself and my work. But the more impatient I got the more I
remained stuck and stationary.
     What I'm proposing is to stop the cycle and stop the madness.
     The difference between now and all those years ago is I learnt a valuable skill. A
different way to approach my work. All the greatest artists, inventors, scientists, ancient
philosophers and architects knew it. If you want to overcome any obstacle and become
accomplished whatever your goal, you are going to need to acquire and practice the art of
Active Patience.
     I now work regularly on all my projects. I have finished projects. I'm proud, content,
work hard and love it. I don't worry about what other people think and I don't worry
about what I'm thinking.
     If someone like me with all my flaws and wasted years can acquire such a skill and
mindset, I believe anybody can. This guide is my journey to Active Patience.
                                 Instant Gratification
“As we get past our superficial material wants and instant gratification we connect to a
deeper part of ourselves, as well as to others, and the universe.”
    - Judith Wright, Author and Educator

     First lets stop the blame game. You are not a failure. You are not too intrinsically
flawed to achieve what you want. You have been programmed for instant gratification
and it is everywhere. We worship it. We love it. Who wouldn't? You can send a tweet in
a blink of an eye, faster than you can roll up your yoga mat. Emails are sent
instantaneously across borders and oceans. When we need money we make the transfer
on our phones while waiting in line. We can record our favourite TV shows and watch
them whenever we feel like it. No more waiting until 8:30pm on a Monday to watch the
next episode of Game of Thrones. We can discover the answer to pretty much any
question we have anywhere in the world with one touch of our smart phone. We want
what we want and we want it now.
     All this instant gratification has spread to every facet of our lives. If we can have so
much of our life instantly, then why can't we transform our lives instantly? We are told
we can be anything we want to be. We can increase our income in thirty days. Lose ten
pounds in ten days. Write a book in twenty days. We are told we must have certain things
before a certain age. If we are not married by the time we are thirty we think, when did
everything start to go downhill? I haven't got that big break and I'm twenty-five. I must
be on the wrong path. The YouTube video of me singing didn't go viral? Where's my
recording contract?
     I have been guilty of thinking I must be on the wrong path purely because it was
taking a long time!
     When things weren't instant I would think something must be very wrong. It’s like
when you try and open Word on your computer and it seems to be taking more time than
usual. At once you think something is wrong with the computer!
     Machines and technology have sped up our lives but we forget we are mere mortals.
We try to write or create like machines. Expecting to produce results in an impossible
time frame or when we wish it would be completed. When we miss the deadline it’s not
no wonder we feel like disappointments or failures.
     What happens when that diet and exercise program gets derailed? Your thirty days
are up and results are zero. We give up and move to the next guru. We get back on the
hamster wheel and the cycle continues. We wallow in self-hatred. Nobody ever told us it
would take ten years to find our passion in life—they said one year. Nobody ever said it
would take one year to lose thirty pounds—they said two months. We have been
programmed to believe if something takes a long time then we have failed. Nobody ever
said it would take six years to write your first novel but it did and you feel ashamed and
embarrassed to tell people it took you so long.
     There is no doubt communication is going to get faster. Expectations will get higher.
So how do we go from a fast-paced life to a project where our mindset needs to change to
the long game? Delayed gratification is the key to your dreams. Active Patience is the
energy behind the hand that puts the key in the door. Active Patience is the long game. Its
what you need when you decide to write a book. It might take six months or six years but
it takes as long as it’s going to take. It’s what you need when you overhaul your
unhealthy habits. It’s energy, slowly burning, propelling you forward.
                        The Myth of the Overnight Success
“My dad told me, ‘It takes fifteen years to be an overnight success’, and it took me
seventeen and a half years.”
    - Adrian Brody (Actor and Academy Award Winner)

     The reality we don’t want to believe is that there is no overnight success. The truth
is, successful writers have been working their whole lives to get where they are.
     Below are a few examples of what it takes to be a so-called ‘overnight success’:
        • Apple iPod: It took three years for the iPod to become an overnight success. The
        first iPod was released in 2001. Within a year Apple had revised it, but it wasn’t
        until the fourth version came out in 2004 that sales took off.
        • Twitter: Biz Stone had been creating blogging, mobile phone and social media
        products for eight years before founding Twitter. He said, “Timing, perseverance,
        and ten years of trying will eventually make you look like an overnight success.”
        • Pinterest: the CEO recently said they had “catastrophically small numbers” in
        their first year, and that if he had listened to popular start-up advice, he probably
        would have quit.
        • Tiger Woods: Although he was the youngest golfer to win the Masters
        Tournament, he'd been practising golf his whole life. He was a child prodigy who
        started learning at the age of two.

     Delve’s video essay, ‘Why da Vinci was no genius’, explains that Leonardo was a
loser for a long time. The essay goes on to say that we have a distorted view of creativity
and success. You could say he was the most exaggerated overnight success ever to have
lived. When da Vinci got his first big break and painted the Last Supper, he was forty-six.
That's middle-aged to us, but back then, it would have seemed like old age. When he was
thirty the only work he could get was drawing dead people, while Michelangelo was
painting the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel.
     The essay points out that the way we think of success today has changed. ‘Here there
is a common thread of great achievers but it’s the opposite of how we think today.’ The
common thread is patience. Everybody today has desire, ambition, talent and skills, but
the real test seems to be, do we have the patience?
     We have it much easier now than any other time in history. We have a platform on
which to show our work to potentially millions of people. Our creations have the
potential to go viral and touch people on a global scale.
     What if you found out you would finally master the skill of writing only when you
were seventy-five? Would you still do it? Leonardo would, and so would Vincent van
Gogh, because what’s the alternative? The regrets most people have in their old age are
for the things they never did.
     Active Patience knows it’s in for the long haul. Success is not overnight. There are
no overnight flights here. You can choose to sit in the waiting lounge and be a spectator,
or you can get on the plane. Long haul flights only. The longer the flight the more exotic
the destination. They are the only ones worth taking.
                                    Procrastination
“A year from now you may wish you had started today.”
    - Karen Lamb, Author

     Endless books and blog posts have been written on procrastination and everyone has
a theory and a solution. My dad's theory is that it’s a form of laziness. Whatever the
theory or solution is, procrastination will tie you into a knot. You’re actively seeking
distractions and avoidance instead of actively working on your goal patiently.
     My own procrastination has stopped me producing hundreds of thousands of words,
finishing numerous books, starting businesses and in general moving on with life. I have
reached for the chocolate too many times and spent money on things I didn’t need just to
avoid my writing. In fact, starting seems to be the hardest part of writing. The race has
started but you can’t get over that first hurdle, so you decide to have an energy drink,
which does nothing but delay you.
     I once was a personal assistant. I had oodles of free time to do whatever I wanted. I
was there for nearly two years and had practically no supervision. I thought to myself,
this is the perfect job for someone who wants to write. Mind you, I was still in the early
stages of writing; but I used to think to myself, what an opportunity I have here. So what
did I do with this opportunity? I blew it. I didn't write for a whole year. I surfed the net.
Ate lots of muffins and enjoyed the water views from my office while drinking lattes. I
discovered heaps of writing blogs during these days. Most I still read today. But they did
not get me moving. I would make up little excuses. Anything to avoid working on my
project. My favourite was the one where I believed it would be too messy having one file
at home and one at work. I could have kept it on a USB and worked from that file, but I
kept coming up with excuses to not write. I felt constantly defeated by my unhappiness at
work. I wanted to keep things simple. Minimise the casualties. Then I started to fear my
supervisor would discover me. Being a writer was a deep, deep secret of mine and one I
hadn't told anyone about. I was so fearful that a senior manager would see what I was
doing that I let it become my greatest excuse for not writing.
     I surfed the net until the unhappiness became unbearable. I thought if I took a year
off to write, it would give my book the quantum leap it needed. Procrastination still had
me for at least another year and the battle continued.
     Today my procrastination manifests in the same way. Surfing the net is a big one and
reading a million blogs on writing and publishing. I have lost count of all the different
sites and blogs I have signed up for. I have a constant stream of newsletters and blog
updates in my inbox everyday. I think I have read ten different How To blog posts of the
same problems over and over again. In my mind they give me reassurance and that
feeling that I'm doing something writing-related.
     The reality is, the only person who should be let into the party is Writing, not
something else dressed up as Writing. This is not a fancy dress party, just as your life is
not a dress rehearsal. Sure, many blogs are extremely helpful, and I have read some posts
that have changed my life, but if you spend endless hours reading blogs, you know you
have a procrastination problem.
     Active Patience ignores the call of the Internet, the overflowing inbox and errands
that can be done later. Your dog doesn't like Active Patience because it puts your goals
before anything else. It knows losing a few hours reading blogs is not going to help you.
Active Patience has your back. It’s looking out for you. It knows the pain of regret and
disappointment if you don't get to work. It knows the big picture even if you don't. It can
see a mile down the road. It knows what you can become if you act on its advice.
                                       Impatience
“Adopt the pace of nature, her secret is patience.”
   - Ralph Waldo Emerson, Essayist, Lecturer and Poet.

     Instant gratification seems to be making us more and more impatient. I can get an
instructional video in seconds on how to create the most eye-catching eBook cover. I can
upload a book to Amazon in minutes. People talk about it all the time. 'I want to write a
book and put it on Amazon,' or 'I'm going to write an eBook and edit it myself.' They start
writing and it gets tough. The end product seems so instant. So easy. Writing the thing
should be easy too, right? I have fallen into this trap many times. I just want to upload the
damn thing. I want to see it out there in the world. Stuff spending years and years
learning the craft, writing the book, editing and writing draft forty-five. We want to
become members of an exclusive club without filling out the paperwork or looking at the
submission requirements.
     Impatience is an enemy. For me, instead of an army on the horizon that I
occasionally go into battle with, the enemy is within. Impatience with my writing is like
my personal bodyguard and confidant, guiding me and protecting me not from bad but
from good. They make me second-guess myself constantly by doubting myself, they want
things done like a Roman general but they also don't want me to get ANYTHING done.
They don't want a war but they don't want peace either.
     During these confusing times my impatience manifests in these ways:
        • Feverishly checking word counts and constantly changing my word count goals.
        • Writing paragraphs devoid of description and filled with dialogue, just to get
        that feeling of reaching the end.
        • Talking about 'The Book' with family and friends, when work on 'The Book'
        seldom happens.
        • Collecting cover designs I like, which leads on to the next point…
        • Creating lots of new cover designs in Canva.com, mostly of projects I haven't
        started, and creating ones that just look damn good.
        • Googling editors when the first draft is not yet completed.
        • Googling agents with no finished manuscript to offer.
        • Pressing the compile button in Scrivener to see something in manuscript format.
        • Analysing Scrivener statistics, including how many paperback pages my novel
        is.
        • Buying domains for possible blogs, brands and series (P.S. I own
        ActivePatience.com and many more).
        • Copy and pasting writing from old projects, changing it slightly and adding to
        new project, which creates that feeling by seeing the word count go up.

     Of course some of these things aren't bad. What is bad is if they are stopping you
from achieving what you want to achieve, if they keep you up at night, or make you want
to throw in the towel, or if they make you think something is wrong because it’s taking
too long. If your impatience makes you feel like a failure, if you want to beat yourself up
all the time, then maybe your current system is not working. Imagine the things you
could achieve if you kept going.
      Patience is essential to get you over the finishing line. We don't want to believe that
success can take an awfully long time to achieve. Sometimes it never comes. Do you
want to be that person who couldn't stick at their writing for a couple more years, saw
your idea become the latest hit ten years later? Impatience kills. It wants it now but you
can't do it now, so you think, why bother? Why give yourself one chance of success when
you can give yourself many? Impatience is really betrayal in disguise. Choose to be
patient and win the respect of your soul.
                                         Anxiety
“Anxiety is the hand maiden of creativity.”
   -T.S. Eliot

     Anxiety was my little secret for a long time. I never understood it. I have had car
accidents over anxiety, stuffed up oral presentations, said the wrong things many times
and hit that send button when I know I shouldn't. I was an anxious kid. I would scream at
the top of my lungs if I saw a black dot on the wall for fear there was a bug in my room.
As an adult, anxiety has stopped me from writing numerous projects which in turn has
caused me more anxiety. It’s a cycle that has seemed never-ending.
     For me the triggers are usually inaction. Physically, it manifests if I haven't done any
exercise. Anxiety builds up, since it has nowhere to escape. Mental anxiety comes from
inaction. The more you don't do, the more anxiety it creates.
     Why is anxiety so important, you ask? Anxiety is often the feeling before the
impulse of impatience. It can also come before you procrastinate. It makes you avoid,
turn a blind eye and turn your back on what you want to achieve. It always appears when
you least expect it. It’s that dreaded feeling that bubbles up from the deepest cavity of
your gut to infect your body and mind and spirit.
     Anxiety shows itself in the following ways:
        • It stops me from finishing projects. Anxiety makes me fear the end of anything.
        • I fear being uncomfortable if I confront my writing. Avoidance seems easier.
        • It makes me think that maybe I'm on the wrong path if I'm feeling anxious in the
        first place.
        • I question my self-worth
        • I turn to distractions like chocolate, fast food, shopping and cheap shopping
        centre massages.
        • I talk about projects when I know I shouldn't. A build up of anxiety for me can
        sometimes burst out of my mouth when I should be writing words on the page.
        The problem is, once you tell people you are writing a book, they ask when I’m
        going to be finished, which adds more to your anxiety.
        • Writing endless ideas in notebooks complete with mind maps, arrows and
        clouds. Anxiety seems to push the idea out and ease frustration, but it stops the
        exploration once it gets too uncomfortable to confront things again.
        • It makes me list endless reminders and ideas which I then don’t act on.

     Healthy habits that become second nature are essential for Active Patience. Energy
in. Energy out. Ideas, writing and all of our creative energy are slowly and regularly
released. Exercise keeps us calm, cool and collected, it releases endorphins and it builds
our store of energy. Anxiety clouds the mind. Combatting anxiety opens the path to
achieving a writing life you can be proud of.
                                     Perfectionism
“Perfectionism is the voice of the oppressor, the enemy of the people. It will keep you
cramped and insane your whole life, and it is the main obstacle between you and a shitty
first draft.”
      - Anne LaMotte Author of Bird by Bird: Some Instructions on Writing and Life

      Perfectionism is often the deadliest enemy for writers—and anyone else who wants
to achieve any goal in their life. It’s a soldier who tortures and kills you slowly. We
torture ourselves with perfectionistic dreams of great achievement while slowly dying
inside of failure.
      As a writer, the need to be perfect seems to be more prevalent. You put yourself out
there in the big bad Internet world, to be scrutinised, critiqued, ignored or dismissed. You
put your heart and soul on the line. It’s natural to want everything to be perfect, but
perfection is unattainable. It’s a goal that can't be reached because whatever you achieve
is never good enough. It’s never enough that we just had a go. It’s not good enough to
write a hundred words. It’s not good enough that you felt too tired after work to sit at the
computer.
      Recently I was preparing for a trip to Japan. I decided I was going to finish the first
draft of an e-book I really wanted to write. It wasn't going to be long, so I thought I could
finish the book in a week, send it to my editor, and when I got back I could publish it.
This was my fantasy. Every spare moment I had, I added to it. The day of my flight I was
still adding to it. Flustered, I finally gave in. I knew I was only going to be 70% finished,
and because I was in such a rush, dribble was coming out. I was trying to hit that word
count target without thinking about what I was writing or what I really wanted to say.
Once I was on that flight to Japan, I took my laptop in hopes that I would get some
inspiration on the trip. In reality, I was too busy to look at my laptop. Throughout my trip
I had a weight hanging over me. I hadn't reached my goal. I felt terrible that I had to send
this half-finished thing to my editor. The perfectionist in me wanted me to be
superwoman and I couldn't achieve it. Not only couldn't I finish the eBook but every day,
that little demon on my shoulder said, what’s wrong with you? Why can't you write 500
words a day? I wanted to have the perfect week of writing in order to have a perfectly
finished eBook. When the stars don't align, the guilt sets in that I couldn't live up to my
perfectionist self.
      My perfectionist brain self wants me to be like the following:
          • Be able to write anywhere, any time, on public transport, and at 5am, like Dan
          Brown.
          • Be published with my first novel.
          • Fluke an agent with one mega email.
          • Have a life, full-time job, travel, watch favourite TV shows and have the perfect
          body all while being prolific.
          • Write 3000 words a day every single day of the year, this would be 1,095,000!
          Perfect! Now get to work.
          • Read -two or three books a week, including all the classic self-improvement
       books of the last fifteen years and every one of Shakespeare’s plays.
       • Work out everyday at the gym and be a 'skinny' writer for when I have to do
       head shots and promote myself.
       • Never fall behind on my TV schedule with all my favourite reality shows and
       still write all the time.
       • Create the ultimate writing retreat and home office, although I know I can't
       afford to.
       • Buy more online programs, courses and workshops.
       • Become a writing machine and pump out multiple novels a year, plus non-
       fiction.
       • Journal every single morning, including weekends, when I'm tired or I’m
       running late.
       • Writing should always be a joy. If it’s not there must be something wrong.
       • Pave the way for the perfect writing success story or journey.
       • Test as many Wordpress templates as possible until the perfect one is found.
       • Blog logos, colour schemes, book covers, freelancers, examples. Need I say
       more?
       • Spend hours trawling through stock photography for imaginary blog posts.
       • I will convert all my family and friends to believe in writing.

     The thoughts that regularly come over me are the ones that say, if you can't create
perfect writing in the perfect environment in the perfect body, then don't bother. Failure
can be a payoff because we are building a case against ourselves. We’re gathering
evidence of all our faults and mistakes. Whenever we sit down and do something, our
heads say, remember that day when you failed again? This is the same situation all over
again.' The payoff of failure is inaction and a free pass to gorge on junk food or watch
hours of mind-numbing TV.
     Of course all of this is possible, depending on the time or day of the year, but
perfectionism wants me to do it all every single day. Perfectionism dictates it’s all or
nothing. If I fall short one day or don't create at all, my head gives me that free pass
because I didn't fit the ideal. Remember, it’s an all or nothing situation.
     Active Patience accepts the fact that perfection doesn't exist. It never looks over its
shoulder to check its surroundings or see what other people are doing. It never lets you
give up because you’re not that so-called ideal. It knows the work is consistent and
reliable regardless of whether you have that beach house or a quiet place to work.
     Everything is filled with imperfection. Know that every month or year your ideals
and dreams change. Your idea of perfect now won’t be the same in a year’s time.
Perfection itself is imperfect, flawed and inconsistent. By actively pursuing what you
love with an unshakeable belief in yourself and your abilities, you depend on nothing but
yourself. At the end of the day, you have everything you need to create exactly what you
want.
                                  Delusional Fantasies
“Belief and delusions are incestuous siblings.”
    - Aleksandar Hermon, The Lazarus Project

     Living in a delusional fantasy goes against everything Active Patience stands for. I
was living in one for a long time. The opposite of Active Patience is being stuck but not
the kind of stuck where you can't move at all. It’s more like being captured by a gigantic
spider. It wraps you up in a glue that you can wiggle in without getting anywhere.
     When I set out to write my book, I didn't think about what the process would be. As
the years went by, I developed a number off fantasies. My fantasies were:
        • I could take a year off and finally finish that novel. (I did this and didn't finish.)
        • The writing life consists of productive writing days, freedom and lots of coffee
        shops and lunch.
        • I will be my most productive when I have the perfect home office. Until then,
        piles of papers and cute stationery accumulate in a plastic storage container.
        • I can write 100,000 words in six months. No problem. Um.
        • I’d finish reading a novel and say, to myself ,'I could have written this!'
        • Writing is the solution to everything.
        • I can eventually escape the cubicle job. For good.
        • Being home with my dog will give me animal kudos in the universe. (When I
        was home for my writing year, the dog didn’t notice me much at all. She was only
        after snacks and slept most of the day.)
        • Self-publishing is the solution to everything.
        • I thought daydreaming about delusional fantasies all the time might make them
        come true.
        • I will have a super-glamorous book launch where all my family and friends will
        be proud.
        • I will be finished in six months and published within twelve months.

     The thing about delusional fantasies is that when you get what you thought you
wanted, the reality is nothing like the fantasy. We are setting ourselves up for
disappointment.
     Constantly being in a delusional fantasy means you are never in the moment. Your in
the time machine of your head. You live in either the past when you could have got it
right, or the future when you might get that thing that is missing from your life. Instead of
doing the work, you actively get nowhere. The active is in our heads and the patience is
in the waiting. Hope accumulates and again we actively get nowhere.
     Active Patience is about the real grit and hard work, however long it takes. Being
aware and alert while we work and not in our time machine. We don't hope for the best.
We bank on our actual efforts to take us to where we want to go.
                                          Regret
“We must all suffer one of two things: The pain of discipline or the pain of regret and
disappointment.“
    - Jim Rohn

     I envy people who say, 'I have no regrets’. Although I have no regrets about some
things, there are others I deeply regret. Before you get the imaginary violin out, they are
not sad or tragic. They are minor blips along the road. Miniature betrayals that could fill a
dollhouse. Every room an exact replica of another. Doorways blocked. Mediocre
furniture. Young and stupid echoes throughout the house.
     My 'life' regrets are not unique, and they are probably common to all creative people.
I was artistic as a child, until I changed from private school to public. I abandoned all the
artistic stuff I was interested in. To cut a long story short, I ignored my creative interests
until my late twenties. I studied Business at university and got a cubicle job in a bank.
For years, all the signs were there. My degree took me two years longer to get because I
knew I wasn't studying what I wanted to do. Year after year and job after job I knew in
my heart I wasn't meant for the corporate life. Still I continued. When I was younger I
underestimated the self-destruction that can occur if you don't follow that inner compass.
For years I was angry with myself and felt like I betrayed myself and punished myself for
it. At the same time I didn't think it was possible to do what you wanted to do. I thought it
was a part of life. It wasn't until my late twenties when my compass was pulling me in
another direction that I couldn't ignore it anymore. So here I am, trying. I don't know
what the future holds and I may go back to that cubicle, but those little regrets aren't
sucking me dry anymore.
     Those little suckers were as follows:
       Daily Regrets
       • Not reaching my word count
       • Not writing
       • Not writing blog posts
       • Not starting new blog/s
       • Not writing any short stories
       • Feeling tired in the morning
       • Feeling too tired to write morning pages
       • Missing a work out at the gym
       • Not doing yoga in the morning or a salutation to the sun.
       • Not reaching inbox Zero
       • Not catching up on all my fave blogs
       • Not listening to one full podcast every day and not re-listening to my fave
       episodes.
       • Not reading every day
       Monthly Regrets
       • Not writing 30,000 + words, though it seems easy when you do the math
       • Not finishing a novel/project
       • Not entering writing competitions
       • Putting off enrolment in creative writing courses or any online course
       • Putting off art workshops
       • Not reading at least four books in the month
       • Not rereading favourite writing books
       Yearly Regrets
       • All of the monthly regrets X 12

     When Active Patience finally clicked, I understood its purpose. Active Patience
prevents regret. It’s the effort or activity that matters and the patience that prevents and
protects against regret by giving birth to our long-term goals. The labour is long and hard
but we know the pain of not doing it will probably kill us. Why is it that when we sign up
for a degree that takes three or four years to complete we don't think twice, but when we
think of writing a novel that might take the same amount of time, we stress, avoid and
procrastinate. I think it’s because we know when we get that degree we will have that
piece of paper, which is a ticket to somewhere else. Working on a creative endeavour that
could take years to complete is fraught with danger. It’s risky. Its outcome is uncertain.
There is no piece of paper at the end of it, except an imaginary one written on our soul
that we can't see or put on our résumé. There is no job at the end of it. Instead, what
awaits you is lack of regret. Every character, word and syllable of effort has been
recorded. Duly noted. Activity registered. Patience verified. Lack of regret is a ticket to
your truth.
                                       The Basics
“Simplicity boils down to two steps: Identify the essential. Eliminate the rest. “
    - Leo Babauta, Author and Blogger of Zen Habits

    They say we go through a personality/character evolution every five years. Make this
book your mindset evolution. What is the alternative? Putting yourself through
disappointment and suffering for years to come. Make this book the next evolutionary
stage in your life. Choose to practise the art of Active Patience.
    Let’s dissect the words and break them down. Let’s get to the basics. What do the
words ‘Active and Patience’ mean, according to the Oxford English Dictionary?
       Active
       * Engaging or ready to engage in physically energetic pursuits
       * Characterised by busy or lively activity
       * (Of a person's mind or imagination) alert and lively
       * Participating or engaged in a particular sphere of activity:

     Origin: Middle English (in the sense 'preferring action to contemplation'): from Latin
activus, from act-'done', from the verb agere.
       Patience
       * The capacity to accept or tolerate delay, problems, or suffering without
       becoming annoyed or anxious:
       * Origin: Middle English: from Old French, from Latin patientia, from patient-
       'suffering', from the verb pati.

     The following pages contain ten habits to get you started cultivating Active Patience.
You will create your own along the way. Like any habit, they take time to incorporate
into your life.
                            Habit 1. Adjust Your Mindset
“For anything worth having one must pay the price; and the price is always work,
patience, love, self-sacrifice. No paper currency, no promises to pay, but the gold of real
service.”
     - John Burroughs, Naturalist and Essayist

     Adjusting your mindset is essential. Notice I didn't say ‘change’. Most people hate
change and I can count myself in that group. I absolutely hate it when someone (usually
family) says to me, 'You need to change’. For years I thought I needed to be fixed. If
‘change’ is a negative word for you, chances are it always will be. When someone says
you need to change, your natural reaction is to get defensive. I prefer to think that my
mindset needs adjustments or needs tweaks. Small tweaks can make all the difference.
Start by making the below adjustments or tweaks. These adjustments work with you and
don't conspire against you. Remember, we are actively trying to achieve our goals…
patiently.

Never lose sight of your Intention
     Why are you working on this project? Is an idea trying to come through you or have
you identified a trend and know you are the right person to bring that idea to life?
Whatever your motivation or intention, be clear and straightforward with yourself. When
the going gets tough come back to this intention.

Enlist the Experiment Mindset
    Think of your writing project as an experiment. Without experimentation, where
would we be? Success or failure is irrelevant. Your idea must first be tested. The words
must meet on the page. It must be executed. Ideas must be tested if only for your own
peace of mind. Nobody wants to always wonder if they could have written a book.

Learn your triggers for excitement
    One of the triggers that gets me excited is listening to my favourite podcasts. The
only reason I used to look forward to my train ride into work was so I could listen to Tim
Ferris, Joanna Penn, Valerie Koo and others. Their words of advice and interviews kept
me on track. They reminded me of what I really wanted in my life—and it wasn't to keep
my cubicle job in the city. You need to sustain yourself. Keep topping up the well.
Discover what makes you excited about projects or ideas. Make those triggers continually
flow.

Practise Gratitude
     Stop judging your present situation. Observe and celebrate what you have done
rather than where you have to get to. I don't know about you, but I’m always surprised
when I write. I think, where did that come from? We are always trying to get somewhere
other than where we are. Be grateful you have imagination and creative energy. Some
people can't find theirs or access it. It’s mysterious. It’s scary. Be grateful for it.
Outmanoeuvre Disappointment
    Focus on what you have achieved. You are worthy of putting your ideas on paper.
Your self-worth is not based on numbers or the past or what other people have said about
you. You are growing and nurturing your talents at all times. You may be in the early
stages of growth, but a big old giant tree is growing and making a forest one seed at a
time.

Stop Searching for Guarantees
     Is it risky to spend every spare moment writing a book for a couple of years with no
guarantee of success? You betcha. Nobody knows how long anything is going to take or
whether it will be successful. By actively and proactively persevering, we can weave our
threads slowly, building our stories one thread at a time. Jump and you will sprout wings.

Let The Work be the Reward
     In order for us to press on and never give up, you must let the work be the reward.
Not everything will be well received, not everything will get published. There will be
setbacks. We have heard it all before. It’s not the destination but the journey. You have
an idea. You obviously can write. Enjoy the process, and if you can't, find ways you can.
Have fun with your writing.
     Forget about grammar and spelling. Nobody sees your first draft. Besides, that’s
what editors are for. Marvel in the magic and mystery of scenes, dialogue and characters
appearing on the page.

Live by a Theme
     Live by a theme that you can actively and patiently work towards even with the
smallest effort. Have your goals and have a theme or many themes you will live by.
Themes like, This year is going to be my year of being creative everyday, will help keep
you on track and make you feel like you have achieved something even with the smallest
of contributions.
                              Habit 2. Let Go of the Ideal
“Everything that’s realistic has some sort of ugliness to it. Even a flower is ugly when it
wilts, a bird when it seeks its prey, the ocean when it becomes violent.”
     - Sharon Tate, Actress

     When I first realised I was writing a book I thought I could write it in six months.
Three years later, including one year of writing paralysis, I still wasn't finished. I felt like
a failure. I thought after three years I should have three books finished. I soon realised
this ideal or fantasy was causing me allot of pain, and the pain was getting greater as time
went on. I couldn't stop time but I could stop the pain. I had to let go of the ideal.
     Your ideal is the dream or crazy goal that's stuck in your head. Goals and dreams are
not bad, but remember, we are aiming for Patience and Actively working towards
realistic goals which will realise our dreams. At this time, you don’t know how long the
project will take to complete or whether it should go the distance. Aim for small goals
that get you closer to your dream.
     The ideal could be that you want to write a novel in one month, six months or a year.
You bought the book and have read how the author wrote a bestseller in three months, so
you figure you can do it in six months. Sure it’s possible, but to write consistently for six
months will take Active Patience. What if it takes longer? A year? Eighteen months?
What about two years? All kinds of feelings will be bubbling up by now. More ideas will
have appeared in your notebooks. Anxiety creeps in. Fear and perfectionism take over.
Judgement fills our guts. Then the ultimate disappointment—in yourself, your project,
your abilities. Do you really want to be the person who couldn't follow through? Who
couldn't stick it out for another six months or, god forbid, another year?
     Whatever ideal you have, it will conflict with reality. Guaranteed. Life happens.
Illness, stress, you name it. Let go right now. Instead of thinking of a timeframes, long-
term goals or deadlines, let go of all the numbers. How many chapters are you aiming
for? How many obligatory scenes will be needed? Love scenes, confrontations, opening
and ending scenes in each chapter” There are no rules. Forget about the perfect ideal
timeframe and let go of the need to know when that monster will be finished. Nobody
knows how long it will take. Nobody knows what life will throw at us. The muse might
throw us down a plot direction we never thought of. We are only writers and don't have
crystal balls.
                    Habit 3. Shut that inner critic up for good
“If you hear a voice within you say “You cannot paint” then by all means pain, and that
voice will be silenced.”
     - Vincent Van Gogh

    Your inner critic is that voice inside our heads that says the following:
      • It’s taking too long to write this novel.
      • See, I told you, You have no creative talent.
      • You are on the wrong path.
      • You were never meant to write.
      • You don't have the discipline.
      • Quit, already.

     The list goes on and on…
     The inner critic’s voice is like a movie reel. It plays over and over inside our heads.
It’s not only a voice. It’s a voice we have associated all our failures in life with. We put
the movie to the voice. We build up a case against ourselves. We search and find the
evidence. Remember when you failed that essay in English? The inner critic says, 'See, I
told you so. You were never meant to write! It was there in the beginning.' Our inner
critic knows we get easily discouraged. It is desperate for us to give up when it knows we
needed another six months. It tells us we failed when we didn't write that novel in three
months.
     We are not as bad as we think we are. People feel intrinsically flawed. Our minds
play tricks with us and so we confuse hard with complicated. When things get tough we
think it must be our character or personality flaw. ‘See, I knew there was something
wrong with me.’
     Stop internalising your projects. Stop associating your so-called failure with your
own personal flaws. Step back from the project because, just as we are not our thoughts,
we are not our books.
     Everyone can find evidence of failures in their life. When we embark on writing, or
creating anything for that matter, in order to cultivate active patience we need to
remember our achievements in life, focus on the task at hand and never give up.
     Start ignoring the inner critic and plough through no matter what. Active patience is
constantly moving forward no matter what. Active Patience waits for no one and never
stops to listen to the inner critic. No ideas? Great, keep going. Writing for ten years?
Great, keep going.
                             Habit 4. Work Consistently
“Ask, and it will be given you; search, and you will find; knock, and the door will be
opened for you.”
    (Luke 11:8-9).

     Many books have been written on how to work productively and consistently. You
need to know what works for you. Know your triggers for success and failure, experiment
and execute daily, weekly, monthly, whenever you can. If you want to write, the
consensus seems to be: make time to write. Do what you have to do. Create realistic
goals. Stop raising the bar so high you don't achieve it and fall down the self-loathing
spiral of despair.
     Working with Active Patience means we find pockets of time and start without a
rumbling of complaints in our heads. We focus on the task at hand. We are in the
moment. Our minds are not preoccupied with the past or future. We don't focus on what
we didn't finish last year or what we want to achieve by the end of this year. We get to
work. Actively working, we never think of the destination, rewards or guarantees.
     Every time I felt my writing was going nowhere and it all felt like an eternity,
anxiety would start to bubble up. Like a stew bubbling on a stove it slowly wilted my
motivation. To tame anxiety, use your work and focus back on the task at hand. Just as
you get more energy by doing more exercise, you get more energy to write by writing
more. So we are physically moving and mentally more alert as a result.
     This is where bum glue is needed. Our minds and fingers are active and so you must
find where they work best. This is where you shut the door of your study and ignore the
kids, or you sit in the corner of your favourite cafe with your laptop.
                            Habit 5. Think Zen, Do Zen
“The Energy of the mind is the essence of life”
   - Aristotle

     The long game is really a mental game. Our mental energy is directly related to our
physical energy. We read it all the time and so I won't bore you with the calories in and
calories out, or set out a diet plan. But I will say that you need to be as healthy as you
possibly can be. The more you exercise the more energy you have, the more energy you
have the more clearly you think. Active Patience is calm. Exercise reduces panic and
anxiety behaviour. This is the kind of behaviour that makes us feel we are in a hurry and
trying to do a million different things at once. We become paralysed, and beat ourselves
up for it.
     If you are sluggish and tired, I guarantee you are more likely to be depressed,
frustrated and prone to disappointment. You will be stuck in a never-ending cycle of self-
destruction, with nothing to show for it all. All those negative emotions are feeding off
each other and stopping you from achieving your goals. So do yourself a favour—get
physically and mentally fit. Do you really want to be huffing and puffing through your
marathon, or cruising through it? You decide.
     Remember, to embody Active Patience, we must be in it for the long term. We must
have a store of energy that we know how to fill, that will sustain us. We must be
constantly restocking shelves, and we must complete regular stock takes.
         • Start assessing your physical and mental energy levels.
         • How tired are you at the end of the day or on the weekends?
         • What are your eating habits?
         • How long can you focus and concentrate on your project for?
         • What are your anxiety levels like?
         • Do you give in easily to your negative thoughts?

     What gives you energy may not give me energy. Everybody is different. Some
people drain of energy easily, others don't. Find what works for you. Here are some
actions and ideas to get you mentally and physically fit:
        • Meditation
        • Yoga
        • Join a gym
        • Ride a bike
        • Listen to inspirational podcasts
        • Listen to your favourite music regularly
        • Record positive affirmations and listen to them regularly
        • Create a vision board to give you mental energy
        • Start a blog
        • Join a club full of like-minded people
       • Visit an art gallery by yourself

   Active Patience cultivates and lives best in the bodies and minds of physically and
mentally strong people.
               Habit 6. Train your brain for delayed gratification
“The gratification comes in the doing, not in the results.”
   - James Dean, Actor

     Practising delayed gratification is essential if you want to complete any long-term
project. It’s also the hardest skill to acquire. Then there is the problem of knowing when
to give up on a project. Sometimes, it’s necessary to give up. Some book ideas should be
blog posts. The more we practise Active Patience the more intuitive we get with our
projects. Remember, we’re in it for the long term. When we complete a project we start
the next one, never flinching or reacting. We calmly keep moving forward. We're
constantly increasing our energy and managing our stress levels, never worrying about
how long it’s taking or how long it’s going to take.
     Rewards are important. Let’s be honest. We all need them. There’s nothing wrong
with that. Leaving carrots along the road to success is perfectly acceptable. We are not
robots.
     First assess how long it takes for you cave in. Try timing how long it takes before
you check your word count. How long can you go without touching that ice cream or
chocolate bar you’ve been saving? How long can you go without checking your emails?
     Like a muscle, delayed gratification must be used regularly and built up. Start
challenging yourself and increase your times. Record them in an Excel spread sheet or
mentally make ante of them. Then start working out what rewards you will have in the
future if you complete your current project. Work out the rewards, then forget. This step
is essential. Record the reward, then forget it. Remember, we are actively moving
forward calmly to the finishing line. It’s imperative we not get too involved with our
rewards. Active Patience lives in us. Yes, we need a little help every now and then. But
everything we need is inside of us. We move the wheels, start the motor, put the car into
drive. Sometimes, dangling a carrot in front of us gets the car moving. That’s fine. But
we all know the force is really within us, as they say.
     Giving into immediate gratification and being able to withstand delayed gratification
can make all the difference between an amateur who is 'always starting to write a book'
and a professional who has written four books. Which one do you want to be?
                               Habit 7. Practise Patience
“Patience is not passive; on the contrary, it is active: it is concentrated strength.”
    - Edward G. Bulwer-Lytton, British politician,
    poet, critic and prolific novelist.

     We all have an idea of what patience looks like. You have been waiting for your
coffee for over ten minutes and you don't have a care in the world. The cash register
crashes and still you wait. These things happen, you tell yourself. Your meeting is soon,
but you know you are going to make it. You are calm. Looks pretty serene, if you ask me,
and probably a little unrealistic.
     Patience is seen as being passive. Everything will fall into our laps if we sit back and
hope. We are told that if we pray, the universe will give us what we want. We don't like it
when someone says the universe rewards action, so get cracking. Patience is seen as a
feeling that involves us pulling the reins in. It involves using our inner strength of self-
control and restraint. And then whatever we want will materialise out of thin air. We put
off our projects until tomorrow and the next day and the rest of our lives.
     As we know, Active Patience requires we systematically move closer to achieving
our goal. But how do we work on long-term goals when we can't wait in line for an extra
five minutes, or we get frustrated at the sight of a traffic jam? And then we wonder why
this novel is taking so damn long, or why we gave up on that other novel halfway
through.
     Practising Patience in its purest form is an obvious part of Active Patience. The aim
isn’t to become a yogi who never stresses about anything. The aim is to practise being
patient without having to think about it. It shouldn’t be hard or like you are suffering in
silence. Make it second nature.
     But first we need to look at its evil twin, impatience.
     We all know what it feels like to be impatient. You’re waiting in line. Someone is
taking forever to order something, and then they pay by card, which takes longer and we
get infuriated. We might stomp our feet, swear, whinge and complain.
     Impatience can often come from a fear of what we can accomplish. We are often so
afraid of what we can accomplish that we want to speed up time to get there faster, or we
want to abandon everything so we can move on to the next project. Why? Because we are
so damn impatient. We want it and we want tit now.
     Why is impatience so bad for us? Remember, we are trying to increase our energy
and impatience is a blood-sucker. It kills energy. We torture ourselves and it’s like a
domino effect. We can't get anything done and then we suffer more because we can't
achieve what we want. You can't create that book series you’ve had in your head for ten
years, or write the story of how your grandparents met in WWII. Instead, you create
WWIII inside yourself because the only person that suffers is you. Your work doesn't
suffer because it doesn't exist. You haven't given birth to it yet. It’s a figment of your
imagination.
     To tackle impatience and become more patient we must be in the moment when
impatience overtakes us and irrational thought starts to creep in. When you know you are
starting to have these thoughts and they become physically uncomfortable, try the
following.
       • Stop. Watch the mind.
       • Take note of the rambling.
       • Stop resisting and give in to the situation. Surrender.
       • Do a body scan and slowly relax from head to toe.
       • Watch your breathing. Is it shallow? Change it to deeper breaths.
       • Know that everything is going to be okay. Everything will work itself out
       eventually.

     Working on any long-term project like a book is a test. Testing your patience is part
of the game. Take it in your stride. Don't be so easily defeated.
     When we loosen up in the moment, our perception of time changes. We start to
realise we have all the time in the world.
                              Habit 8. Active Reflection
“Thinking is the hardest work there is, which is probably the reason why so few engage
in it.”
      -Henry Ford, Businessman

     Thinking is active. Reflection is active. It is mandatory to reflect before, during and
after a project is finished. Writing a novel does not require only writing. You will
eventually have to sit down and draw mind maps or brainstorm to work out characters,
plot holes and scenes. We think with our fingers but our minds do the talking. Remember
when your dad said, ‘Always sleep on an important decision’? Some ideas need to
marinate. You may not believe it but your mind is still tinkering away at your project at
all hours of the day.
                         Habit 9. The low-information diet
“Sometimes a pessimist is only an optimist with extra information.”
   -Idries Shah, Author

     Watching other people’s journeys can be liberating, fun, exciting, inspiring and
motivating. Harmless, right? Every author, artist and creative has a blog these days. I tend
to read a mixture of blogs, but most of them are bloggers at the top of their game. People
bleed on the page. They share their highs and lows. The world can see their earnings,
struggles, their successes and latest products. If you tend to be an impatient person like
me, someone who procrastinates a lot, then watching, reading and listening to other
people’s journeys can go from half an hour a day to hours. Inspiration turns to
infatuation. Infatuation turns to procrastination.
     The problem with looking at other people is it causes you to feel inadequate and
question why your journey is not like theirs. Why can't I create three or four books a
year? Why isn't anybody reading my blog? Why can't I stick to a blogging schedule?
How do I build an email list like so and so?
     When I'm reading a good blog post and I'm really enjoying it, I automatically feel
envious. I think to myself, how did they get all this information and package it up like
this? Where did this idea come from?
     Hints and tips are like finding buried treasure. It doesn't matter if you have heard the
same advice or it was said differently by another writer. You feel like you have won the
lottery. Each time you find a golden nugget you want to find more. Harmless, right?
     Too much advice makes us needy, constantly searching for more. We are like
treasure hunters. We're addicted and want to find more. We go on regular treasure hunts,
trawling through sites and blogs. Three unproductive hours go by and deep down you
know you blew it. Again. Again you wonder how those other writers do it. Then you
Google your favourite authors plus writing tip and waste another hour.
     I propose a solution. Go on an information diet. Cut back on all blogs or any other
source you use to avoid your work. Whatever the source is, cut back. You know what is
doing harm and what isn't. Put the writing and the project first. Make it a priority. What
you don't know will not touch you in any way. When we get wrapped up in other
people’s stories we forget our own story. You are the star of your own life, so act like the
most self-absorbed star you can be. Stop observing other stars and focus on your own
light.
                            Habit 10. Kill Perfectionism
“Perfectionism is self -abuse of the highest order”
    - Anne Wilson Schaef, Author

    Kill perfectionism before it kills you. Spy on yourself and start noticing how you
want things to be. Do you want the perfect writing room before you start writing? Are
you being stubborn and holding out until you get that writing room? Beating
perfectionism requires acceptance and debunking that belief system in your head. You
know you don’t need a writing room to write. The truth is, you have everything you need.
Below are examples of how to beat perfectionism:
       Health
       • You know deep down inside that you will never have the perfect body. There
       will always be something too big or too small.
       • Focus on health and physical energy. You will think more clearly and be more
       productive.
       Family & relationships
       • Accept that not everyone is going to get you and your passions and hobbies.
       • Surround yourself with some people who do get you.
       • Some people just aren't creative (hey, nobody’s perfect)
       Environment
       • You can't avoid all interruptions
       • Studios and cabins in the backyard are really, really expensive, not to mention
       cold in winter.
       • Decor comes and goes. Whatever your dream desk, vision board and office chair
       look like now, chances are you will change your mind in six months.
       • Don't bet on winning the lottery so that you can be really productive at your
       beach house.
       Career
       • If you are waiting for the perfect job and boss who will understand you, it's not
       going to happen. Even if it does, you won't be as productive as you think you will
       be. The reality is never like the fantasy.
       • If you dream of quitting your day job—having the discipline to work from home
       is a lot harder than having a day job and collecting your paycheck.

                                             Foreword


Kevin Rhodes has travelled a road most people are not called to travel. It has been a road of great
challenge, great discovery and great reward.
The great challenge has been physical, mental and emotional.
Kevin has had a rich and full life. His life included a gratifying law career, a stint as a theatrical
producer, a late life emerging author career as well as the deep satisfaction of being a husband
and father in a loving family.
Then Kevin was diagnosed with MS. His journey with MS is the story he tells in this book.
The challenge of confronting the debilitating disease of MS has allowed Kevin to discover inner
strengths, develop rehabilitative strategies and realize life truths about himself. His courage to
accept his reality and at the same time reject its limitations has given him the strength necessary
to be an active agent in his treatment and not just a passive observer of a progressive disease.
The challenge of simultaneously accepting and confronting his reality has propelled Kevin to a
world of astounding multi-hour physical workouts, constant research on his condition and deep
reflection on the meaning of his life.
His great discovery has been about himself.
By not flinching before a hard truth Kevin has discovered deep truths about himself and life. A
person really does not know how he or she will respond to a critical life challenge but now Kevin
does know. Life dealt him a hard hand and he has played it. Played it hard right back and played
it well. He has been up to the greatest challenge of his life.
His discovery has been that he has inner resources he never realized. He has a mind that can
focus on an intractable problem. He has a body that can accept the most arduous demands. He
has the will to do what needs to be done, no matter how painful or rigorous. He has a life
populated with loving, caring and supportive family and friends.
His great discovery is that he has the resources and the will to be up to his greatest challenge –
the challenge of his own continuing life.
His other great discovery has been that he has the courage to share his journey with us.
His journey has been filled with discovery about the meaning of life, the human capability when
faced with a damning diagnosis and his immense will to live life full out no matter what the
situation. Usually these ideas are exaggerated clichés but Kevin has discovered that they actually
are the necessary ingredients to a successful strategy of combating a debilitating disease.
His great reward has been the journey itself. By being an active agent in his own treatment Kevin
has discovered himself in a way that gave him insights into his own inner truths.
And that is what this book is about.
Kevin has looked in the mirror long and hard. He has suffered physical limitation. He has
experienced hours of self-directed rehabilitation regimes. He has accepted a difficult reality. And
he has endured.
But the poetry of his journey has been the life realizations he has discovered. Rather than just
punishment his disease has instead given him deep rewards. It sounds grandiose to say that he
has discovered the meaning of life but in many ways that is exactly what has happened for
Kevin.
And like John the Baptist emerging from the desert with truths to share, Kevin has truths to
share. His truths are hard won but that is what gives them validity.
Our great fortune is that Kevin combines the gifts of the philosopher with the gifts of the poet.
He has contemplated his situation and has gazed deep into his own soul. He has seen what it
takes to live with an impossible and difficult truth. He has seen what it takes to confront a truly
life-threatening disease. He has seen what it takes to persevere and at the same time retain a life
affirming joy.
Kevin’s book is profound for its honest truths and its personal revelations. Read it with an open
heart and mind as he shares with you his journey and what it has taught him. Kevin is too smart
to prescribe his journey as the set of answers for everyone but his journey has given him some
insights and wisdom on how to live a full and brave life when faced with impossible odds.
Kevin’s epiphanies have been hard earned and he offers them as guides for others in their own
struggles and life journey.
His story is his gift to us.

And still we went on. To live meant to have it – that one undeniable thing we were

                                           Prologue:
                                      One Morning in April


       April 2011
       I thought I might do my marathon the same weekend as the Colfax Marathon in May, so I
       looked up last year’s winning time for someone my age, and it was just under 3 hours.
       Right on target. But then on the last Sunday in April I woke up, and I just knew. Today
       was the day. I didn’t tell my wife when I left. I was afraid to jinx it, I guess.
       I did the first 10 miles faster than my target pace, but when they were over I had lost all
       feeling in my ankles and feet. I had to stop to stretch and massage and fill up my water
       bottle. I wanted to do the next 8 miles in a single stretch, but had to stop halfway for
       more water and more stretching and massaging. The bottom half of my legs were
       swollen, one of my hamstrings kept cramping, and the places where I had some fractures
       were aching. It didn’t look good.
       I finished the first 18 and sat for awhile, kicking my feet and trying to get the feeling
       back, wondering if I was done. And then there was Coach’s gentle voice. “Think you
       could get back on and go a little further?” My answer was “No!” And then I got back
       on.
       I’d hit the infamous Wall that marathoners talk about.
I was running my first marathon… on an elliptical machine. I’ll tell you why in a few. What you
need to know for now is that it changed my life forever, and not just because of what happened
that one time, but everything that led up to it, and has happened since.
It keeps changing my life, every single day.
I think it could change yours, too.
I wrote this book to understand what I learned and what I’ve become because of that morning in
April, and to tell you what might be possible for you, too, if you’re willing.
But I’m way ahead of myself, and of you. We need to seriously backtrack.
                                          Life Athletes


I wrote this book with life athletes in mind.
Life athletes live with fire and focus. You know when you meet one. You know if you are one.
“Athletes” might be literal or metaphorical. Runners or entrepreneurs, executives or artists…
what we do isn’t important; what matters is what we have in common.
Life athletes value the inner game. We play from the inside out, and we want to know how we’re
doing. Theory is interesting, but it needs to prove out. We’re experiential learners. What we
believe, how we behave, how we get things done… we put it all into practice, and to the test. If
it’s not working, we change it.
Life athletes are “internal locus of control” people: we have this urge to do something about it.
We believe what we do makes a difference to ourselves and others, and we hold ourselves
accountable.
We take the urge to improve and grow up a notch. We’re always in training, inside and out --
always sharpening our game. People call us “intense.” Maybe. Intense is just who we are, what
we do.
We may lead a team or be on one, but ultimately we play to an audience of one: ourselves.
When an idea takes us, we own it. We throw ourselves into it. We leave it all out there. We don’t
get motivated, we are motivated. We do the reps, get into the game, succeed, fail, grieve, get
over it, try again. It’s what we do, our habit, our calling card.
And yet…
There are times when all that drive and resiliency, motivation and initiative are too much of a
good thing. Sometimes we keep going when we really ought to call a timeout. But we don’t.
Challenges and the unexpected… that’s especially when we kick it in gear. We keep patching
our inner game in the heat of the action to the point where it’s worn out. and so are we. Now the
timeout isn’t optional. We need to step back, reconsider, gain new vision, create a new game --
one that works now, for who’ve we’ve become, not who we were.
If that’s you, then you’re in the right place, reading this book. You’ve created and refined your
inner game, probably several times. Maybe it made you a star. But now you need a new inner
game, and you’ve gone looking. That’s what drew you here. That’s what drew me here. I wrote
this book because my inner game wasn’t up to a new challenge in my life. Writing this book, I
found the inner game needed -- or really, it found me, as you’ll see.
This is my life athlete story. It’s yours, too, if you’re willing. That was the other reason I wrote
it: because I thought it could help you with your game, too. You’ll just need to own what’s in
this book, make it yours, make it work for you. But that shouldn’t be hard: you do that already. I
believe that, and belief is where this book began.
                           I Wrote This Book Because I Believe


We all do -- believe, I mean. Inevitably, inescapably, we believe. We can’t help it. And not just
that: we believe our believing can change things, for ourselves and for others. We all do that;
life athletes to an extreme.
What we believe lies close to the heart of the inner game -- perilously close: if we stop at what
we believe, we don’t get to the core, the source, the real power. I know that now; I didn’t know it
when I started writing this book.
I wrote this book because I needed an inner game that would help me do something impossible. I
didn’t believe it was impossible at first. That belief came later. When it did, it was apparent that
my inner game wasn’t up to doing the impossible. This book tells how I got myself into the
impossible game.
The impossible game I discovered is based on the concept of personal ethos. I Googled “ethos”
and here’s what came on top:
                 Ethos: the characteristic spirit of a culture, era, or community
                          as manifested in its beliefs and aspirations.
The definition speaks of collective ethos. This book is about personal ethos. I believe each of us
has our own “characteristic spirit manifested in our beliefs and aspirations,” and that we can
build a powerful inner practice on it. We can even tackle the impossible.
I believe I’m not alone, wanting to do something impossible. People are doing impossible things
everywhere these days. Impossible is a disruptive technology at work in the collective human
soul, inviting, tugging, prodding, challenging. Try an experiment: be aware of people doing
impossible things, and notice what you notice. You’ll find it everywhere. It’s part of the ethos of
our age: walls of possibility falling, being pushed back, disintegrating, vanishing.
I believe impossible is the inner game of our age, and we’re being invited to play. I believe the
invitation wants an individual response, and that getting in touch with our personal ethos is an
essential skill for getting into the impossible game.
I also believe the invitation is coming with a special urgency, and that there’s a lot at stake in our
individual response. That’s because of something else I believe: that we are living in apocalyptic
times -- no, not in the sense of a cataclysmic one-off grand finale event, but a shuddering and
grinding and shifting of the tectonic plates underlying what it means to be consciously human --
a paradigm shift of monster size.
Paradigm shifts happen when people adopt them. I believe there’s enormous risk in whether we
do or don’t adopt this one. If enough of us refuse the invitation to create a new, post-apocalyptic
world, we could bring about our own end -- not with a collective, world-ending catastrophe, but
with a series of individual ones carried out in unknowing collusion, causing us to wither, shrink,
lose vitality, until finally, as T.S. Eliot prophesied, the world ends not with a bang but a
whimper. Hence the special urgency that characterizes the invitation.
“Inner game” and “apocalypse” are metaphors. They work for me. This book uses the former;
my next will use the latter. There are others, of course, and each of us picks the metaphors,
myths, stories, and vocabulary that work for us. Our separate preferences aren’t the point; the
point is that people all around the planet are sensing that the human race is thinking, believing,
creating, and behaving differently these days, that there’s a giant shift in outlook and response
going on that’s bigger than our individual versions of it. Way bigger. I believe that.
My latest personal response to this huge paradigm shift of human consciousness has been to
write a book about a new inner game I know by the name “ethos.”
And somehow, you’ve been drawn here to read about it. Maybe you weren’t thinking “inner
game” before you got here, but something about the metaphor resonated, and here you are,
sensing with me the need for a new inner game for being human, now more than ever. We share,
on some profound level, not just the metaphor but an awareness of the larger truth that lies
behind it. That’s what we’re really looking for, and we’re committed to practicing it when we
find it.
I believe all that. Those beliefs are why I wrote this book.
                                             Game On


“All you can do is exercise.”

That’s what the neurologist said when he told me I had MS. I laughed out loud. I was already
exercising, in a big way. In fact, I was training to run a marathon. And not just run it, but win it.
“All you can do is exercise” was good news. You want exercise? Okay no problem. I got
exercise. Lots of it.
The specific diagnosis was “Primary Progressive Multiple Sclerosis.” About 10% of people with
MS get that version. The others get the remitting/relapsing kind that’s treatable with drugs that
flatten the cycles. Primary Progressive MS doesn’t cycle, and it’s not treatable. It’s degenerative.
Period. The neurologist prescribed exercise not as a cure, but as a way to try to slow down the
degeneration.
Thanks, Doc, but I’ve got other plans.
I didn’t start wanting to run and win marathons. I just wanted my legs and feet to work like they
used to. I had a couple accidents (skiing and bicycling) and thought I hadn’t rehabbed properly.
After several years of ineffective treatments, I decided to pursue recovery through a new exercise
regime. Then I got carried away and wanted to run and win marathons.
Turned out it wasn’t a rehab issue, it was MS. It showed up after surgery for the bike accident.
Apparently, that happens sometimes: MS lies dormant until trauma releases it. I didn’t know it
was MS until years later. In the meantime, I kept trying to rehab until I could barely walk.
(You’re wondering how I do marathon training if I can’t walk. I’ll tell you in a bit.)
After the diagnosis, I kept on exercising. The only thing that changed was the perceived degree
of difficulty. Rehab an injury? Okay, no problem. Heal MS? Hmmm.…
Lots of people with MS exercise, just like lots of people run marathons and some people win
them. Nothing unusual or remarkable about that -- nothing impossible. But for me, after a couple
years, MS finally made my marathon goal impossible. It took me a long time to realize and admit
that. For the longest time, I thought running and winning marathons was possible for me, despite
the MS. I don’t think so anymore. It’s impossible. I get that.
I still want to do it.
One other thing you need to know: I don’t want my marathon to be assisted -- e.g., in a
wheelchair, or by pedaling a bike with my arms, or with the aid of an exoskeleton or something
like that. Nothing against those things -- they’re amazing, they help people do amazing things,
but they just aren’t my thing.
I want to win a marathon on my feet.
Feet or bust.
26.2 miles, on really fast feet. That’s the goal.
Game on.
                               Impossible Means Impossible


We need to be clear that “impossible” means impossible.
Impossible is not some admirable challenge, quest, high calling -- something so great and
glorious and good but seemingly out of reach that we label it “impossible.” That’s just semantics.
Impossible is the real deal -- out there beyond out there, beyond the known scope of our
experience, beyond what we envision. It’s somewhere we can’t go, something the mere attempt
to think about sends us spinning into the inexplicable, unknowable, unforeseeable, unimaginable.
And yet…
Impossible is also something we’re passionately committed to even though it can never happen,
because we love and desire and need it more than life itself.
That’s what impossible means, at least in this book. We need to get that. It’s critical to
everything that follows .
                                Beyond Life Beyond Reason


This book is about me dealing with MS, but it’s more than that -- like my book Life Beyond
Reason wasn’t just about me leaving law practice to produce a show. Training to win marathons
when you have MS and leaving the law for multimedia theater are interesting, but the bigger
story is what goes on inside us when we try to live differently and do new things. To do that, we
need to be different. We do that by changing our inner game.
Life Beyond Reason’s inner game was the source code for my post-lawyering life. I still practice
and share it in blogs and workshops. It’s a strong game -- it took me to the other side of ego,
which is huge for personal transformation. But it wasn’t making my impossible marathon
happen. I’d been well launched, but needed a course correction.
As soon as I realized that, the ethos inner game showed up, beginning with random ideas and
word bursts that I wrote down, wondering what they were. There was a characteristic spirit about
them; if I remained in that spirit, there was more to write; if I left it, the writing vanished.
After awhile, I became aware that a new inner game was moving in, unpacking its things,
demanding attention. That’s the way it is with apocalyptic change: one world ends as a new one
rises in its place. And so it was with my shift to a new inner game.
                                      What You’ll Find Here
Running Past Our Limits is a collection of blog posts I wrote in late summer 2012, 2013, and
2014, and again in early 2015. They fill in the backstory, tell you about my marathon training
and MS, and illustrate my former inner game.
With the backstory in place, the journey to a new inner game begins with what I learned about
why my inner game was weak, and what it needed to get me into the impossible game. From
there, it takes a deep dive into the ethos inner game -- explores its essential awareness, key
insights and practices.
                                   What This Book Asks of You
The ethos inner game asks a lot of us. So does life. And so does this book.
But you expect that. You’re a life athlete. And if you’re not, you might become one.
This book is not an explanation, it’s an invitation. It asks you to bring your full self to your own
inner game, get immersed in the pursuit, get carried away with your own discoveries, make them
yours, build your own thoughts and practices, achieve your own mastery. I want there to be times
when reading feels like a jazz solo, when you’re jamming and grooving and riffing it, and
suddenly you realize that’s what you’re doing and you look around, wondering where you just
went, and if that was okay.
Yes it’s okay. More than okay. Go for it. I’ve done it several times myself, writing this.
I want the book to be your launching pad, and if you forget how you got launched, and who
cares anyway, then so much the better. I write my books that way because I live that way, and
this book is that way only more so. It has an edge to it -- that urgency and insistence I talked
about earlier. I felt that at the start, I feel it again now. Ethos does that to us: it shakes and moves
things in us and our world at our deepest levels, and we feel and experience and know the
shifting beyond our usual means of understanding. It has to be that way, otherwise we’ll just stay
stuck in what we already know, even if it’s not working, and won’t make the changes we need to
stay in the game.
A book like this is a journey -- full of discoveries, mostly self-discovery. That will be true of
both of us: I’ll be in a different place when it’s done than I am now; so will you. Both of us will
get to the end and realize how far we’ve come, how much our inner game has changed. How
about we both keep that in mind as we go along, and pause now and then to reflect and compare
where we are then to where we are now.
I have no doubt we’ll both be surprised at what we discover when we do that.

                                    Onto Something Big


Sometimes at my workshops, somebody will ask me to tell my story. I usually don’t. I think it
implies that my story is somehow more valuable than anybody else’s. We’re all in this make-
your-dreams-come-true thing together, and I want to keep it that way.
But this time I’m going to make an exception, because I’ve stumbled onto something so big and
amazing there’s no way anybody could think it’s just about me. It’s huge, and if all of us could
tap into this, we and our lives and our world would change – maybe as fast as overnight. Really,
It’s that good. Here’s the story:
I have trouble walking. Running is impossible. You name it, I’ve tried it: rehab, PT, lifting,
stretching, massage, acupuncture, chiropractic, energy therapy… No luck so far. But I love to
work out, and last September (2011) my daughter was home and wanted to lose some weight
she’d put on during a year in France. (Bon appétit!) So she’d go to Bally Total Fitness with me
and get on the elliptical machine, and I’d get on the one next to her. She’d go maybe an hour. I’d
go maybe 10 minutes.
She lost the weight she wanted to lose, and I got an idea: “I wonder if I could start running on
this machine, the way I used to before I got hurt?” I also wondered if maybe somewhere along
the way my body would decide to start working again like it did before.
Then I got a second, totally crazy, wild hare idea. “I wonder if I could do 12 miles in 60 minutes
on this thing?” Never mind that I have trouble walking to the end of the block. I have no idea
where the idea came from; it just did. But I do know that twelve 5-minute miles is fast.
So I started in, grinding out maybe a mile in 12 or 15 minutes. My ankles and feet don’t have
much feeling in them, and when I walk my hips and legs sort of stop working, so that I have to
drag myself along. I call it my Quasimodo walk. And so when I was done with my elliptical
sessions I could barely get off the machine. I’d look around, trying to make sure nobody noticed
as I staggered and wobbled over to a chair. I’d collect myself and then go staggering and
wobbling home (I have a cane, but hate to use it), afraid some cop was going to come by and
stop me for public drunkenness. (Seriously.)
I worked on pace and stamina throughout the fall, and discovered something really important:
when my feet and ankles are numb and my legs stop working, I can still make the elliptical
move. I don’t have to actually pick up my feet (which is a problem), all I have to do is glide.
(Hint: That’s important. Remember that for later.)
                                 Don’t Call the Ambulance


I started to get faster and go further. About the time winter was arriving, I did 5 miles in 66
minutes, then at Christmas I did 7 in 82. One day I decided to go as long as I could, which turned
out to be an hour and 45 minutes. I don’t remember how far I went. When I got home, my wife
was frantic. I’d been gone so long, she thought the people at Bally Total Fitness must have sent
me off in an ambulance.
One day when I was feeling really strong I decided to try to run a 5 minute mile, just to see if it
was physically possible. The machine shook and wobbled -- apparently I was pushing the limits
of more than just me. I labored, but I did it. So now I knew it was theoretically possible to run 12
miles in an hour.
Around Christmas two terrific things happened. One day I was gliding along with my eyes shut,
as I often did, when a pat on the back made me jump. It was our local Bally amazon. “Working
hard!” was all she said. A couple weeks later I was limping toward the door after a workout
(“Just limp,” my wife had advised, “You don’t look drunk that way.”), when a guy my age met
my eyes. “Man, you work out hard,” he said. “Yes, I do,” I replied.
You can go a long way on encouragement like that.
What about my goal of 12 miles in 60 minutes? Um, not so much. Yes, it was theoretically
possible, and I’d made great progress on both pace and distance, but that goal was shooting at the
moon. Around mid-January, I gave up on it. There were still too many days when my legs just
wouldn’t move anymore and I’d have to quit after a mile or so and go trudging home in despair.
Plus, as a healing modality, this hadn’t been my best idea. My feet and legs were getting worse,
not better, although it was hard to tell if that was because of my new exercise regime or not –
they’ve been getting worse for awhile. But despite that, I was feeling better – inside at least. My
heart and lungs had to be loving it – especially my heart, in more ways than one. So I kept on.
And then one day, maybe a month or two after I gave up on my goal, I got a new idea – another
totally crazy, wild hare idea: “I wonder if I could run a marathon on this thing?”
                                          Meet Coach


A curious thing started to happen as I entertained the idea of running a marathon on the
elliptical: I got a coach. Well, I already had one for other things, but now he got involved with
my running program.
I don’t think I’ve told you about Coach before, have I? In short, he’s that part of me that accepts
what I say I want at face value, and never doubts that I truly want it or can achieve it. He’s that
drive inside me that will not accept that something is impossible if I really, really want it, that
believes in me no matter what, and won’t let me quit or call myself a failure.
It’s nice to have a guy like that around - not that I wouldn’t like to strangle him sometimes. He’s
a disciplinarian, but always fair and never cruel. He’s also frequently exasperating, because he
never knows when to call it quits, never recognizes when I’ve reached my absolute, all-out limit.
He always thinks I’ve got more to give, and always requires it of me.
Maybe you’d like to meet him. You can. He’s imaginary, but that doesn’t mean he’s not real. I’m
convinced you’ve got a coach like mine inside of you. Yours will look and act differently than
mine, but he or she will do the same things for you that mine does for me. You might want to go
looking inside yourself to see if you can find one and get acquainted.
Anyway, once I started to think about doing a marathon, Coach started showing up for my
workouts. I’d finish a session on the machine and sit down to recover, and then I’d hear a voice
in my head. “Think you could do another mile?” Or, “Think you could do three more miles at 8
minutes?” Or whatever.
Usually, the questions came at a point in my workout when the answer was an emphatic, “No!”
And then he’d say, “Think you could do it anyway?” And so I would, and I’d hit whatever the
workout goal was, usually right on the nose. It was uncanny.
With Coach’s help, my pace and stamina went leaping forward. My wife quit worrying about
how long I was gone at my workouts. Along the way, I taught myself some quick recovery
techniques – breathing, stretching – that shortened my break times.
I also started visualizing. You hear about athletes doing that. If nothing else, it’s a good way to
pass the time when you’re on an elliptical for an hour – a whole lot better than watching the
energy-sucking crap on all the TV’s. I’d see myself running a marathon, being the shocking old
white dude who could keep up with those guys from Kenya and Ethiopia who can run impossibly
far impossibly fast.
I became an international marathon phenom. It was fun, and the best part was, I always won
every race.
                                             Race Day


Winter became spring, and I didn’t have a date for my marathon, but when I started running 12,
then 15, then 18 miles, I knew it must be getting close. I did have a time goal: three hours.
That’s just under seven minutes per mile. From what I can tell, three hours is a dividing line for
marathoners, the level where you’re starting to get serious. One day I ran 8 miles at that pace,
took a break and ran 7 more. “You could run all day at that pace,” I heard Coach say, and I knew
he was right.
Coach had started showing up not during my workouts, like he used to, but on the way over to
Bally. We’d discuss goals and plans for the day, and I’d almost always hit them on the nose, no
matter how aggressive they were. There were still days when I had to quit early, but fewer.
I thought I might do my marathon the same weekend as the Colfax Marathon in May, so I looked
up last year’s winning time for someone my age, and it was just under 3 hours. Right on target.
But then on the last Sunday in April I woke up, and I just knew. Today was the day. I didn’t tell
my wife when I left for Bally. I was afraid to jinx it, I guess.
I did the first 10 miles faster than my target pace, but when they were over I had lost all feeling
in my ankles and feet. I had to stop to stretch and massage and fill up my water bottle. I wanted
to do the next 8 miles in a single stretch, but had to stop halfway for more water and more
stretching and massaging. The bottom half of my legs were swollen, one of my hamstrings kept
cramping, and the places where I had some fractures were aching. It didn’t look good.
I finished the first 18 and sat for awhile, kicking my feet and trying to get the feeling back,
wondering if I was done. And then there was Coach’s gentle voice. “Think you could get back
on and go a little further?” My answer was “No!” And then I got back on.
I’d hit the infamous Wall that marathoners talk about. I lasted the next 2½ miles on sheer guts,
got off and staggered over to sit down. No way. Again the voice. “Think you could go further?” I
did a mile and a half, and got off again. I was done. It was over. I had no feeling below my
knees, my ankles and calves looked like Elephant Man. Again the voice. I did another 1.2 miles,
at one point looking down at the read-out on the machine to see that I hadn’t lost that much pace.
Amazingly, my three hour goal was still possible. Too bad finishing wasn’t.
It didn’t matter that Coach insists on believing that whatever I want to do is possible. He was
wrong this time. Impossible obviously had won. I was 23.2 miles into a marathon, and no matter
how many times Coach asked me to keep going, it wasn’t going to happen. Even if I could’ve
found the will, finishing was physically impossible. My body was in full rebellion. It wasn’t
going anywhere.
I sat there in total defeat, and all I could think was, “I’m 3 miles from the finish line! I just can’t
quit now!” In a fog of defeat, I got up and dragged myself back to the machine. I picked up my
first leg with both hands and put it on one of the pedals, then dragged the second foot up.
                                    “I’m So Proud of You!”


Three miles to go. I glided along, keeping my eyes on the distance and pace read-outs on the
machine. It felt like running through a swamp, but I kept telling myself to just glide, just keep
gliding. And amazingly, I was still on pace. I closed my eyes and visualized crowds of people
lining the streets, chanting, waving, cheering. I opened my eyes after a bit, and saw two things
that made me mentally stagger.
The first was that I’d run another mile. The second was my wife, standing there. She’d been off
walking, enjoying the park that morning. She’d taken a nap in the sun, and when she woke up,
she knew. She knew I was running the race I hadn’t dared to tell her about, because I’d been
afraid I might fail. And she knew she had to be there at the finish line.
She dashed over to Bally and told the guys at the front desk that no, I’m not a member, but that’s
my husband over there and he’s running a marathon and I need to be there when he finishes.
I don’t think I’ve ever seen a more welcome sight. She met my eyes when I opened them. “I’m
so proud of you,” she said. She started to cry. I started to cry. Ever try to finish a marathon when
you’re crying? My tears poured down my face with my sweat, which was already burning in my
eyes. “And Coach is going nuts,” she added.
You gotta understand: Coach is as buttoned up as they come. He’s a Tom Landry lookalike:
suit, tie, London Fog topcoat, felt fedora, the whole works. I closed my eyes, and there at the
edge of the crowd was Coach, waving his sport coat over his head and whooping it up. It was too
much, it made me start to cry again, so I opened my eyes.
“I’m so proud of you!” my wife said again. “You can do it!” I sent her off to get some water or
paper towels or something. I didn’t really want her to leave, but I had to quit crying and focus on
finishing. The last mile was my fastest of the day. I burst under the arch of balloons as the crowd
roared and Janet cried and Coach whooped.
And then it was over. I dropped off the machine and stood, unable to move. My time was
2:51:57. That’s counting only the time I spent actually moving on the machine, not the time I
spent trying to get feeling back in my feet and all the rest. If I’d counted all that, it probably
would have added another 20-25 minutes. I decided to cut myself some slack and not count the
extra.
“I just ran a marathon on the elliptical,” I told the guys at the front desk as I left. They didn’t
seem all that impressed. Oh well, I didn’t do it for them.
I have two more running exploits to tell you about, and then I’ll draw some conclusions, and this
series of blog posts will be over.
                                Gliding Into the New Normal


A week later, I ran another marathon. I didn’t plan to do it. I just woke up in a mood, checked in
with Coach, got the nod, and did it. My time was 2:35:58 – 16 minutes faster than the week
before. I missed the world record for a man my age by a minute and 35 seconds. Even if I had
counted the time off the machine, refilling my water bottle and working to get the feeling back in
my feet, I still would have come in under the original 3 hour goal I’d had for the week before.
Funny, the first time, when I shared my marathon story with family and friends, everybody got
all inspired. This time, it was no big deal. Oh, Kevin/Dad ran another marathon. I wonder what’s
for dinner.
A couple weeks later, I hit my goal of 12 miles in 60 minutes – the goal I’d set eight months ago
and given up on four months later. Again, when I woke up that day, I just knew it was time. I
checked in with Coach, and he was good with it. When I finished, I plopped down on the bench
in the locker room, drenched in sweat, internally celebrating. I took off my shoes, and then…
there was Coach’s gentle voice. “Think you could do five more?”
By now, I’ve learned that Coach only asks me to do what he knows I can do, even if I don’t
agree. We set a pace goal of 5:15 for the five miles. It was tough going, and I did them at 5:35 –
one of the first times in a long time I hadn’t hit a goal, but still worth feeling good about. Again I
headed to the locker room, and when I reached for the combination lock on my locker, the voice
came again. “Think you could do two more?” I didn’t bother to spin the lock. “I’ll do three,” I
said, and headed back out. My pace was 5:20.
I want to do my next elliptical machine marathon in 2:20:00. That’s a 5:20 pace. It will beat the
world record for my age by 14 minutes. Then I want to run one as fast as those guys from Africa.
That will be a 4:40 pace. And I want to celebrate my 60th birthday by running a real marathon
under 3 hours.
No, I don’t actually run. All I have to do is glide. And no, I don’t count the time when I have to
get off the machine to get water and tend to my physical limitations, which haven’t gotten any
better – not yet, anyway.
So what do we learn from all this? You’ve long since figured out that this series of blog posts
isn’t about running marathons on the elliptical machine. Instead, it’s about running past our
limits, whatever they are. It’s about doing things we know are impossible. Apparently we can
live in two realities at once. In one, there is no possible way we can do the thing we want. In the
other reality, we can, and the first reality’s negative opinion doesn’t count. The first time we do
it, we’re stunned and astonished, and so is everyone else. The second time, it’s just routine. What
used to be impossible has now become the new normal.
What’s the new normal you’d like to create for yourself? Like I said back at the start, if all of us
could tap into this idea of doing the impossible, we and our lives and our world would change,
maybe overnight.

                                      Diagnosis and Denial


Last summer I wrote a series on lessons learned from marathon training. I’m occasionally asked
for an update. Here it is, a year later.
You can read the whole story in the archives, but briefly, I started training a couple years ago as
a way to deal with what I thought was an unfinished injury rehab issue. I didn’t intend to become
a marathoner; I was having trouble walking, running was impossible, and I’d tried pretty much
everything else, so one day I got the idea of using the elliptical machines at Bally’s to reprogram
my body into moving again.
After a discouraging start, I eventually ran a marathon on the machine. My times quickly got
faster and faster, and before long I was into elite-level training and turning in world class
marathon times. No, I wasn’t actually running, I was using an elliptical machine, but still… a
marathon at 4:37 minutes per mile is booking it!
In the meantime, my new rehab theory wasn’t proving out. The faster and further I ran, the less I
could walk. It made no sense: on the machine I could do the impossible; in real life I could
barely do what’s possible for most people. I sought solace at the keyboard, and wrote that first
Running Past Our Limits series, which ended this way:
        You’ve long since figured out that this series of blog posts isn’t about running marathons
        on the elliptical machine. Instead, it’s about running past our limits, whatever they are.
        It’s about doing things we know are impossible. Apparently we can live in two realities at
        once. In one, there is no possible way we can do the thing we want. In the other reality,
        we can, and the first reality’s negative opinion doesn’t count. The first time we do it,
        we’re stunned and astonished, and so is everyone else. The second time, it’s just routine.
        What used to be impossible has now become the new normal.
        What’s the new normal you’d like to create for yourself? Like I said back at the start, if
        all of us could tap into this idea of doing the impossible, we and our lives and our world
        would change, maybe overnight.
Sounds good, but the trouble was, my world definitely was not changing overnight. My first new
normal goal was simply to walk again. It was joined by a second: to run a real marathon in
world class speed. The second goal was happening on the elliptical machine; the first wasn’t
happening at all.
With encouragement from family, I began four months of bouncing from one medical specialist
to another. Four MRI’s, two EMG’s, an endoscopy, a spinal tap, and a few dozen vials of blood
later, the “impossible” I was up against suddenly took on a whole new meaning.
                                             Denial


When it comes to achieving goals and making changes, the inner game matters. In fact, it might
be the only game that matters. Our thoughts and feelings create psychological and physiological
states inside us that help or hinder. We can either work with that to our benefit, or not.
I got a major review course in this last February, when three huge things happened to my
training: (1) I broke the “impossible” two-hour marathon mark, not once but twice; (2) during a
speed workout, I “ran” a 3:34 mile that would have set a new world mile run record; and (3) I
was diagnosed with MS.
Odd, isn’t it, how getting tagged with a couple letters like “MS” affects us? There’s an energy to
them; they instantly reshape our current reality and project out a future for us. And even though
February had been a record-breaking month, those two letters threatened to trash my whole
training regime.
So what did I do? I went straight into denial.
My first step was a “terms of engagement” email to friends and family. I told them the diagnosis
and then laid down some ground rules: don’t offer sympathy, don’t tell me about people you
know who have MS, don’t tell me about the drugs I can take, and don’t tell me to join a support
group. I’m sure it seemed rude, but those kinds of responses weren’t going to help me, and I
needed to head them off.
That done, I dove back into training. For the first few weeks, the diagnosis often came to mind
during my workouts – usually in the form of “OMG, I have MS!” The mere thought of it sucked
the energy right out of me. That just wasn’t going to work, so I created a patter to recite in
response. Instead of “I have MS,” I changed it to “A neurologist has concluded that certain
conditions in my body satisfy a diagnosis of MS.” My patter was factual, but the sting was gone.
Whenever the MS thought came up, I’d repeat my patter to myself until the need went away.
Maybe I “had” MS on some level, but I sure wasn’t going to let it have me.
No, that’s not Pollyannaish or positive thinking; it’s actually good neurology, and was founded
on medical advice. There’s plenty of research to show that “fake it till you make it” has real
oomph to it, plus my neurologist had said that, for a variety of reasons, drugs couldn’t help, and
the best treatment for me was exercise. I laughed out loud. “I can DO that!” I said. Remember,
the whole premise of my training was to fool my body into doing what it didn’t think it could do.
The only thing the diagnosis changed was that now I had a bigger fool to fool.
I eventually got further reinforcement for my denial approach, but not before a powerful new
training assistant showed up one day.
                                      Opportunity Knocks


Our greatest challenges are our greatest opportunities. We don’t often see them that way.
Instead we get fearful and frustrated, and shut down. If we can resist that response, we’ll find
that obstacles are like stored up energy that, once released, launch us into our most astonishing
breakthroughs. I believe and teach that, and my MS diagnosis became a case in point.
My working theory for all that gliding (fast!) on the elliptical machine was to (a) use my body to
show my mind that I was capable of a running-like movement, so that (b) my mind would
obligingly rewire my brain to send appropriate running signals to my body, and (c) once the
circuit was complete, I’d be running again. My MS diagnosis introduced a new wrinkle: my
physical training needed to do more than just change my mind and rewire my brain, it also
needed it to rewire the rest of my nervous system (where the MS symptoms had taken up
residence). Same plan as before, just a larger scope.
About that time, the gym where I work out installed four brand new Adaptive Motion
Trainer/Open Stride machines. They’re like ellipticals, with one HUGE difference: they actually
require a running stride. I.e., instead of just gliding along, I actually had to pick up my feet. Since
my secret for using the elliptical was that it let me get away with just gliding along, using a
running motion on the AMT/Open Stride would be a giant step ahead.
I didn’t see that at first; I just thought the AMT was a new kind of elliptical. I stepped on to try it
out and couldn’t make it move. No one else seemed to be having that problem, so I kept trying,
feeling stupider by the moment, and finally asked a personal trainer for help. Just do this, she
said, jogging easily along. I tried again. No luck. I closed my eyes, willing my legs to move not
just back and forth, but also up and down. Slowly they started. I was exhausted after ten minutes,
getting off the machine was a real problem, and walking the two blocks home even more so. All
the way, I wondered, like Boromir when the Balrog shows up in the Mines of Moria, “What new
devilry is this?”
Some online research revealed what an incredible gift I’d just been given. The AMT/Open Stride
would teach my body perfect running form, which would get patterned directly into my brain and
the rest of my nervous system, until one day I would transfer the running (not gliding!)
movement to the earth beneath my feet. Sweet! Once again, my greatest challenge had become
my greatest opportunity.
Or so I believed, but belief requires action: either the new machine was going to get me closer to
my goal or it wasn’t, and there was only one way to find out. I needed to go back to square one,
where I’d started 18 months earlier on the elliptical, with ten ponderously slow minutes. True to
form, in the months to come, those ten minutes repeated over and over released a whole new
energy into my training.
                                      Find Your Placebo


My new AMT/Open Stride training got a boost when I surprised myself by going to an MS
seminar and being glad I did. I’d gotten these invitations before and blew them off, but the topic
was “dealing with MS through diet and exercise,” and I wanted to sharpen my edge.
What I got for starters was a surprising sense of community. It was odd to watch other people do
the “get up from a chair” routine, and funny to hear a speaker talk about the characteristic MS
“foot slap” and “toe stub.” We all laughed when another speaker acknowledged that
EVERYONE views c-a-n-e as the “never-say-that-to-me-if-you-know-what’s-good-for-you”
four-letter word. And it was good to know I’m not the only one who’s afraid I’ll get stopped for
public drunkenness because of the way I walk sometimes. “Just use your cane,” someone said.
“People will just assume you’re handicapped and not bother you.”
We weren’t just friends in that room, we were teammates with a common goal: don’t give up.
One woman whose symptoms had obviously gotten the best of her – she said she was on the anti-
spaz drug, and was in one of those wheelchairs that holds you upright – won a door prize and
took a loonnggg time to motor herself up to the front. We didn’t care, we just cheered. Plus, I
saw “the look” on a couple faces. It’s my game face, the one I wear when I go to my workout. It
was pretty scary – both to see it and to recognize it as my own.
The highlight for me came when a medical researcher talked about the placebo effect: how
people get well not from “real” treatment, but because they believe they will. “Find your
placebo,” he advised. “if you think it will help you, it probably will.” I felt smug; I’d already
found mine. Is it crazy to believe that elliptical marathon training will get me across a finish line?
No crazier than people getting better taking placebos. I’ll take another bottle of those, please, and
I don’t care that they’re not covered by insurance.
I just finished a book called Mind Over Medicine: Scientific Proof That You Can Heal Yourself.
The writer is Lissa Rankin, an M.D. who went kicking and screaming into non-traditional
medicine after realizing that LOTS of people in placebo control groups get better. She realized
that the typical attitude of “Isn’t that cute, those people got better taking sugar pills” misses what
she ended up viewing as the mother lode.
Plus, she goes on to say, it’s not just what we believe, it’s also about making room in our lives
for purpose and meaning, working with passion and living with joy, getting connected and
making room for creative expression, and changing our circumstances when they turn toxic on
us. All these things can heal both our bodies and our lives.
How about it? What’s your placebo for whatever is ailing you?
                                   Hold ‘em or Fold ‘em?


Each week, my training culminates in an aggressive weekend run. My latest was epic: a
marathon time just 30 seconds over the “impossible” two hour mark; 28 total miles at a 4:36
pace; plus another slow (5:18 pace) four miles tacked on at the end, just because. My body is
now doing on the AMT/Open Stride machine what it was doing on the elliptical machine last
February.
Compare that to the day before, when my family went to Copper Mountain to celebrate my
daughter’s birthday. We rode the American Eagle chairlift up, and they hiked from there to the
12,441’ summit while I rode back down and shuffled around base village with my cane.
When do we stop believing, stop doing the “fake it till you make it” shuffle, stop taking the
placebo, and just give up?
It’s not just a question for me and my marathon training; it’s a decision all of us inevitably face
every time we try to create something new and big and exciting – hang our shingle, change jobs,
launch a business, start an artistic project…. Hope, vision, and inspiration are strong fuel, and we
run on them a long ways, but one day we hit one too many walls and run out of emotional gas,
not to mention money. Now what? Do we accept our fate and go back where we came from?
And what about this awful mess we’ve made of our lives in the meantime?
Wouldn’t it be nice if we could learn when to hold ‘em and when to fold ‘em before things get
ugly?
Jonathan Fields, a lawyer turned serial entrepreneur, author, producer, blogger, and coach, wrote
a book about this: Uncertainty: Turning Fear and Doubt into Fuel for Brilliance. It’s the best
I’ve read on the subject, and can’t recommend it highly enough. Besides his usual insights, he
serves up scores of internal and external strategies for deciding when to hit the eject button or
not. Three of his self-examination questions caught my eye over the weekend:
       In light of the information and experiences you’ve had along the journey to date, does
       [your] original motive still hold true?
       Are you still equally or even more determined to make it happen?
        And given what you now know, do you believe you can make it happen?
Yes, yes, and yes.
The creative journey always takes us places we didn’t expect. It’s by turns unnerving,
threatening, unreasonable, unsafe, and just plain weird. And – what’s completely exasperating
about it – creativity never gets stuck or quits; it just runs on like the Energizer Bunny. Which
means if anybody’s going to wake up and end the dream, it’s up to us.
Guess I’m not ready to do that yet. Instead, I’m looking into new ways to take my training to a
whole new level. How about you?
                                              Mercy


I sometimes lose track of why I’m training. It’s not about going farther and faster on a machine,
or even about actually running a marathon. It’s about reclaiming full health, in body and soul.
That’s the real goal; everything else is just modality.
Every creative endeavor – business, artistic, athletic, or otherwise – has times when it shifts and
drifts, goes where we didn’t plan, sometimes in alluring new directions. Then what? Do we
reaffirm the original goal and stick with the plan, or follow the new direction to a new goal?
People sometimes suggest experimental drugs they’ve heard about, or theorize about mechanical
gizmos that might help. Or they suggest biking or swimming instead of running. Nothing wrong
with their ideas in theory (even if they would require a whole new training regime!), and if they
held out a clear path to healing, I’d consider them. But so far they haven’t. Instead, they’ve only
offered a form of dependency that full healing would make unnecessary. I don’t want a drug or a
device to move me; I want to do the work myself.
Maybe it looks like I’m crazy or stubborn, but something inside tells me that, if I adopted a new
goal and modality, my soul would suffer. I’d give my power away; my heartfelt project would
become someone else’s. Never mind that their terms might produce success as they define it, I’d
hurt my soul if I went there. Sorry, I just can’t.
It’s a tough issue. Ultimately, I find guidance in the concept of mercy. I don’t mean shallow
“mercy rules” like everybody who shows up gets a ribbon whether they win or lose, or if the
score gets too lopsided you don’t have to finish the game. Those things are false mercy – bogus
consolation prizes that don’t satisfy. Cheap mercy leaves a bad taste – not just in our mouths but
in our spirits. We lost badly and we know it. A ribbon won’t change that.
Genuine mercy offers a balm to our pain. Whether we stay in the game or not, mercy invites us
to create and keep creating the same way we did at first, only now our vision is clearer, we’re in
touch with our essence and value and purpose like we weren’t before. Whether we stay the same
course or try a new one isn’t the point; that we can go either way with our souls intact is what
matters.
Maybe mercy reveals that we didn’t really want what we said we wanted after all, that we didn’t
fully know our hearts’ desires when we started, and now the pressures and obstacles have
revealed a deeper and more abiding truth about what we’re after. Once we know that, there’s no
point staying with what would become pointless suffering. We’re not quitting, we’re accepting
an invitation to a more deeply satisfying outcome. Quitting and mercy feel different, and we
know the difference. Quitting mocks us because we’ve painted ourselves into a corner; mercy
blows the roof off and shows us the stars. Quitting says it’s over; mercy signals a beginning.
Quitting fills us with regret; mercy stuns us with relief.
We reach the place of mercy when we pass the point of no return. Quitting can’t undo what
we’ve done, it can only make us regret we ever got this far. Mercy can’t undo it either, but it
doesn’t want to. It doesn’t call us back to what we so desperately wanted to leave behind, it
invites us forward to what we really wanted in the first place. When we’re in the place of mercy,
it’s not time to quit, it’s time to begin.
That’s where I’ll end this update. All the denial, finding my placebo, the gift of a new training
machine, the hold ‘em or fold ‘em crisis, and all the rest come down to just this one amazing,
liberating truth: mercy, inviting me forward to new health, which is still what I’m after.
Stay tuned.

                    A Day (and Week) in the Life of an MS Athlete


Someone told me about MS Fitness Challenge. Great organization; you should check them out.
The founder David Lyons is in showbiz, so they get a lot of celebrity endorsements. David did a
terrific interview of me for his blog. I just looked at it again, and it gave me the idea for this post.
All MS athletes share symptoms that would stop us if we let them. The trick is to make them
irrelevant. Well-meaning people at the gym sometimes ask how I’m feeling, whether I’m having
a good or bad day. I’ve learned to pause and explain that I don’t think in those terms. They aren’t
helpful. If I paid attention to how I feel, I’d never show up, and I sure wouldn’t keep going when
it gets tough.
And it does get tough. You do this, you suffer. Not whining, just sayin’.
The MS symptoms create practical challenges beyond the workout itself, too -- like getting off
the machine when I’m done. I can barely walk; it takes all my focus to cross about 20 feet to sit
down. I drag myself along and hope nobody is noticing, and one time I took a facer. Lately I’ve
been wondering if I should just bring my cane. Same thing with walking down the stairs to leave
the building. These days I mostly take the elevator.
I work out 5 days a week on average, with goals for every workout and for the week. I alternate
strength, stamina, and speed workouts, and something I call “heart elasticity” training. (Did one
of those yesterday. Two hours all out. Looked like I was standing under a rain spout, which is
something for a guy with MS, because one of things you lose is your body’s ability to cool itself.
Guess my training has reversed that.)
The differences in workouts are a matter of metrics: varying machine settings such as stride
height, length, and resistance, and watching how they interact with body metrics such as stride
frequency, target heartbeat, breathing pattern, even how much I sweat. On stamina runs I can
zone out, but the other workouts require constant attention, changing the machine settings and
monitoring body metrics.
Daily workouts are a couple hours, weekends take longer, but training is really 24/7. It’s a
whole, integrated practice embracing details like how much I sleep and when and what I eat.
Mostly, there’s an action/recovery cycle to be observed. Cut corners, you suffer. Like I said….
The weekly cycle culminates in a major weekend workout. This past Sunday I went three hours,
in one-hour segments. The first was for speed, the second (my fastest, as is usually the case) for
combined speed and strength, and the third for stamina. Around 2:50:00 I hit the point where my
right foot spazzes out so entirely it turns out like a ballet dancer’s. I’ve learned to pull it back
straight by sheer focus, and can keep it there for 20 minutes; on Sunday I only had to deal with it
for the final 10.
The Open Stride machine is stingy about mileage: best I can tell, it credits just under half what a
regular elliptical machine gives you. Sunday it gave me 15.35 begrudging miles -- not much to
show for 3 hours and 2,000 calories. Curiously, when I go that hard that long, my feet and legs
often work better than they do after a shorter workout. That was the case yesterday.
So I took the stairs instead of the elevator.

                             A Random Idea Gets Scientific


In the “What’s Your Impossible?” video I told you about a couple posts ago, a friend of mine
(his name is “Angel Vigil” -- no kidding) describes my workout routine as “his own self-
invented alternative treatment to keep the nerves and his muscles and everything working as long
as he can, and fight the progressive, debilitating nature of MS.”
Angel didn’t know it, but he was summarizing two neurological concepts that underlie what I’m
doing. The first is neuroplasticity: our brains rewire themselves when we learn. The second is
myelination: if we use those new neural pathways often, our brains coat them with a substance
called myelin, which acts as a learning and skill development supercharger.
Developmental molecular biologist John Medina describes neuroplasticity this way in his book
Brain Rules:
       Eric Kandel is the scientist mostly responsible for figuring out the cellular basis of [the
       process of how the brain learns]. For it, he shared the Nobel Prize in 2000… Kandel
       showed that when people learn something, the wiring in their brain changes. He
       demonstrated that acquiring even simple pieces of information involves the physical
       alteration of the structure of the neurons participating in the process. Taken broadly, these
       physical changes result in the functional organization and reorganization of the brain.
       This is astonishing. The brain is constantly learning things, so the brain is constantly
       rewiring itself.
Myelination got a huge popular boost from Daniel Coyle’s 2009 book The Talent Code. As the
Amazon book blurb says:
       Drawing on cutting-edge neurology and firsthand research gathered on journeys to nine
       of the world’s talent hotbeds—from the baseball fields of the Caribbean to a classical-
       music academy in upstate New York—Coyle identifies the three key elements that will
       allow you to develop your gifts and optimize your performance in sports, art, music,
       math, or just about anything.
       These three elements work together within your brain to form myelin, a microscopic
       neural substance that adds vast amounts of speed and accuracy to your movements and
       thoughts. Scientists have discovered that myelin might just be the holy grail: the
       foundation of all forms of greatness, from Michelangelo’s to Michael Jordan’s. The good
       news about myelin is that it isn’t fixed at birth; to the contrary, it grows, and like
       anything that grows, it can be cultivated and nourished.
Put neuroplasticity and myelination together, and you’ve got brain flubber. Both are at work in
our brains all the time, whether we know it or not. My approach is to harness them consciously
and intentionally. Technically, what I’m after is re-myelination -- when damaged neural
pathways get rebuilt. I need that because MS has a destructive effect on -- you guessed it --
myelin. Therefore, in neuroscience terms, my exercise routine is an attempt to use neuroplasticity
and re-myelination to restore my MS-damaged neuro-pathways.
Just like Angel said.
I didn’t know any of this three years ago when I began my “self-invented alternative treatment.”
I just had an idea. Turns out I got the idea about the time neuroscientists were hot on the myelin
trail. Apparently I somehow got on somebody’s memo routing list.
Now that I do know about these things, I have a new goal in life: to become a lab rat.
More on that next time.
                                   Science and Snake Oil


A friend shared a TED talk with me about how a rat with a severed spinal cord had its nervous
system regenerated so it could run again. The researchers used a combination of chemical and
electrical stimulation to get the rat’s legs moving involuntarily, but that wasn’t enough. So they
created a robotic harness that allowed the rat to move if and when and where it wanted. After
that, the rat’s spinal cord grew new connections. The scientists created an opportunity for
healing, but the rat’s desire to move took over and made it a reality.
No wonder some scientists built a statue to honor lab rats. God bless that rat. It’s my hero; I want
to be just like it. We all know you can’t move again after your spinal cord is severed. The rat
proved us wrong. We also know you can’t win a marathon if your MS makes it hard to walk.
How about if I be the lab rat who proves us wrong?
The rat needed scientific help, and so do I. Seriously, now: if by any stretch you know someone
who is seriously researching MS and exercise, would you please email me with an introduction?
Let me be honest: I don’t want ideas, like have I contacted the National MS Society. If you can
think of it, I probably have already. What I’m looking for is a real relationship handoff to a real
person. If you can do that, then many, many thanks.
Once we’ve got a research lab on board, we need a committed rat. No problem there. The rat
introduces the X Factor: how does the desire to move generate healing? The scientists don’t
know, and the rat’s not talking. If it could, it would probably sell us snake oil, and we’d probably
buy.
Last year I wrote about the “placebo effect”: believing yourself into an altered state in which the
healing you want actually happens, even though it’s not supposed to, there’s no rational reason
why it should, and you’re actually putting your faith in a sugar pill.
I know one guy who got rid of his MS by visualizing miniature beavers chewing up his brain
scarring (“Multiple Sclerosis” means “multiple scarring”). I’m not making that up. If that’s not a
placebo I don’t know what is. I’ve haven’t tried his approach, but I’m not above a daily practice
that includes mind tricks, even though I honestly believe some of what I do is pure snake oil.
Is it still a placebo if you don’t think it will work? The authors of a book on personal growth
called The Tools answer that question by telling this “famous story about Niels Bohr, the great
Danish physicist and father of quantum physics”:
       A young physicist visited him in his home and saw a horseshoe hanging on the wall over
       the hearth. “Surely, professor, you don’t believe that a horseshoe will bring you good
       luck,” the young physicist exclaimed. “Of course not!” Bohr replied. “But I’ve heard that
       you don’t have to believe in it for it to work.”
And so we’ve got science, and we’ve got snake oil. Should work.

                   My Short And Unspectacular Life As A Lab Rat

where the people are who are doing relevant work, write them emails. These are busy people -- I
almost never hear back, except for a a couple responses from obviously overworked research

Busted.

You’d be healthy, too, if you trained like I do. No, I didn’t actually say that. Well, maybe I did. I

maybe we’ll have something to talk about. We’ll see.

follow. I can learn from the experts but don’t need their pat on the back, don’t need to fill my

will make an exception for me in their research because I’m so exceptional.

for.

                                 Learning to Walk Again (1)


I blogged before about attending a program on MS, diet, and exercise. They mentioned a device
called a “WalkAide” that uses electrical stimulation to counter “MS foot drop.” You strap on the
WalkAide below your knee, and each step sends a shock along the outside of the shin bone
telling your muscles to pick your foot up. Kind of like your mom used to, only more effective.
“It’s expensive,” they said. So much for that idea.
During a workout last summer, I wondered if you could stimulate muscles with electricity when
the nerves aren’t working. I remembered the WalkAide, found it online and emailed the
manufacturer. They emailed back: “There’s a clinic in Denver where you can get a free
evaluation.”
That was the Hanger Clinic in Englewoood. They have a poster with a surfer dude holding a
surfboard under a prosthetic arm, standing on two prosthetic legs. (He’s easy to pick out in this
poster.) The slogan underneath is “Empowering Human Potential.”
Obviously I was in the right place.
Kyle Sherk is serious, professional, efficient. We talked, he strapped on a WalkAide, I took a
stroll down the hallway. The shocks along my shin bone knocked me off balance. Step, shock,
stagger… step, shock, stagger… down the hall.
Kyle made adjustments, I walked. It’s common with MS to lose nerve function in one leg before
the other. For me, that’s the right. The WalkAide has two electrodes. Twist the cuff a little in this
direction, the foot jumps up and to the right. That way, to the left and in. It was funny and a little
creepy, watching my foot jumping around as Kyle made adjustments. I laughed, Kyle laughed at
me laughing.
Next task was getting qualified through insurance -- in my case Medicaid. Three months later I
got three letters the same day, all with different dates, all saying the same thing: Medicaid would
pay for the WalkAide.
More fitting, more walking, more adjusting, as I learned not to be zapped off balance. The
WalkAide records every step. At each visit, Kyle plugged it into his PC to get a usage tally.
Workout days produced major spikes. The first time he saw one, his eyes got big. “What were
you doing on Sunday?!” I’d told him about my training -- maybe he’d forgotten, or maybe like a
lot of people it didn’t really register, it seemed so impossible. I reminded him. His eyes got
bigger.
At first, I didn’t wear it for workouts. I didn’t want my body to need it, and I didn’t want anyone
to say I had an unfair advantage. I didn’t need to worry: it was quickly apparent that you
couldn’t just strap this thing on and suddenly start running marathons. Its biggest impact was on
recovery: after a workout it was easier to get off the machine and over to a nearby chair, which
had become an issue.
We finished the Medicaid testing protocol and I went to pick it up my permanent unit on
Christmas Eve. Kyle was upset -- UPS hadn’t delivered yet -- no WalkAide in my stocking. Then
his cell phone buzzed, and he dashed out. Santa got there after all, with the coolest electronics
gift I’ve ever gotten.
But there was more. Another amazing gift had already been shaping up.
                                Learning to Walk Again (2)


A few weeks with the WalkAide, and one day I asked Kyle if he could recommend a rehab
person to teach me to walk again. Kyle doesn’t smile widely, just sort of slightly, slyly. He
smiled. “Let me ask you,” he said, “On what level do you want to recover?”
He already knew the answer. He’d seen enough massive activity spikes from my workout days. I
raised my hand as far over my head as I could, which is saying a lot -- I have long arms, really
long arms. He chuckled. “I thought so,” he said. “But I just had to ask.”
He left and came back with a brochure for the Walking Free Amputee Rehabilitation Program at
the Functional Performance Center in Littleton. The rehab I needed is called “gait training.”
They use it for amputees like the Hanger Clinic surfer dude. I had the equivalent of an artificial
limb on my lower right leg… why not?
The brochure said Tracy Rauk would be my trainer. “She’s the best,” Kyle said. I emailed the
Center. Katie from the front desk emailed back to schedule an appointment. I replied with a
question: “Do you take Medicaid?” She replied no, but they would work with me on payments.
Sorry, I replied, that wouldn’t work for me now.
So much for gait training.
Several weeks later, Katie called me one morning. “We had a staff meeting today and Tracy said
to get you in here. She says we’re going to qualify for Medicaid so we can treat you.”
Really? You’d do that, just for me?
Yes.
We started a couple weeks before Christmas. The second session I just had to ask. “What if you
don’t qualify with Medicaid? It’s the government -- it could take awhile. Do we need to wait
before we go further?”
“If we don’t, this is all pro bono,” Tracy replied. Issue resolved. Let’s get back to work.
If you saw the movie The Intouchables, you’ll remember the scene where the guy in the
wheelchair says this about his unconventional new caregiver, “That's what I want. No mercy. He
often hands me the phone, you know why? He just forgets. So you're right. He doesn't really feel
any empathy for me.”
That’s the way it was with Tracy. She showed me no mercy, with no empathy for what I couldn’t
do. She’d set up an exercise and I’d think, no way. Then I’d do it. It was that way the first couple
sessions, then I decided that had to end. It was time for a new attitude: whatever she asked, I
would think I could do it, and no more of this internal doubting. I was doing what she asked
anyway, so why protest?
It worked.
Recently, she laid out an obstacle course for me: wide steps, things to step over, thera-bands
around my ankles. I paused, as I always do, looked it over, took a deep breath before starting.
Tracy laughed. “That’s what I like about working with you,” she said. “You stand there and
you’re like, DOMINATE!”
Yeah, pretty much. That’s me. If you’re going to do it, rule it.
And if the WalkAide and the gait-training weren’t enough, it gets even better. I got a third
training gift right after New Year’s.
                                    Learning to Eat Again


I’ve long been skeptical of fad diets. Now I’m on one.
My daughter Darcy works at Vitamin Cottage Natural Grocers. As I was beginning my gait
training with the WalkAide, she told me about a book at her store -- something about
overcoming progressive MS with diet.
It had to be Terry Wahls, M.D. I’d watched her TEDx talk last summer. She was a former Pan
Am Games athlete whose MS eventually put her in a zero-gravity wheelchair. 85% of people
with MS start with the relapsing-remitting version: their symptoms flare up and back off; drugs
even out the cycles. In time, most of them move to “secondary progressive MS”: fewer cycles,
but the symptoms progressively worsen.
That’s what Dr. Wahls had. She began a massive research effort that changed her life and her
medical practice. She’s out of the wheelchair now, walking and on a mission.
Her book is The Wahls Protocol: How I Beat Progressive MS Using Paleo Principles and
Functional Medicine. My diagnosis is “primary progressive MS,” which I like to call “elite-level
MS.” It doesn’t cycle, just goes downhill -- at least, it’s supposed to. If it worked for her, why
not me?
I read the book. My wife read it, Darcy did, too. Then I read it again. We talked about it a lot. Dr.
Wahls’ diet is basically paleo, but she presents it in three increasingly radical stages. We’ve
always had a healthy diet, but this… veggies (no problem), protein (no problem adding more
meat, but that’s a lot of protein everyday!), and fat, lots of fat (are you kidding me?!). Plus, at the
highest level, no grains or starches, and almost no fruit (what?!?!).
We discussed and debated, then a surprise motivator got me off the dime. After a workout one
day I had a sharp pain on my right side. Over the next couple days there was another point of
pain, then another and another. Then I had a dream.
My ten-year self and I were at the county fair, in line for a carnival ride, which turned out to be a
huge Tupperware full of glorious sugary concoctions -- cakes, cupcakes, cookies, candies…. I
woke up and my right side was burning. The mirror revealed a nasty red swoosh from my spine
to my sternum. If you’ve had it, you know what it was: shingles.
Shingles is a herpes virus. It LOVES sugar. Our six-month trial of Dr. Wahls’ diet had just
begun, and my personal version would be even more extreme than hers: no sugar at all, plus no
arginine-rich foods (another shingles no-no).
Growing up, sugar was my mom’s language of love. Our kitchen cupboards and counters were
always full of Tupperwares like the one in my dream. Besides the Wahls diet, it was time for me
to learn love in a new, sugar-free way.
Two days later, a workout buddy joined me for part of my long weekend run. He left, came back,
and gave me one of those medallions that circulate around 12-step groups. The inscription began,
“I love you, my child….”
At a workout a week later, a stranger got on the machine next to mine. “Are you the guy who
used to be in a wheelchair?” she asked. “Can I ask you about your diet?”
You can’t make this stuff up.
How’s the diet working for me? Stay tuned.
                                      Moving on Purpose


Meet John Pepper, my new role model:
       My walking companion, John Pepper, was diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease, a
       movement disorder, over two decades ago. He first started getting symptoms nearly fifty
       years ago. But unless you are a perceptive and well-trained observer, you would never
       know it. Pepper moves too quickly for a Parkinson’s patient. He doesn’t appear to have
       the classic symptoms: no shuffling gait, no visible tremor when he pauses or when he
       moves; he does not appear especially rigid, and seems able to initiate new movements
       fairly quickly; he has a good sense of balance. He even swings his arms when he walks.
       He shows none of the slowed movements that are the hallmark of Parkinson’s. He hasn’t
       been on anti-Parkinson’s medication for nine years, since he was sixty-eight years old,
       yet appears to walk perfectly normally.
       In fact, when he gets going at his normal speed, I can’t keep up with him. He’s now
       going on seventy-seven and has had this illness, which is defined as an incurable,
       chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder, since his thirties. But instead of
       degenerating, John Pepper has been able to reverse the major symptoms, the ones that
       Parkinson’s patients dread most, those that lead to immobility. He’s done so with an
       exercise program he devised and with a special kind of concentration.
                From The Brain’s Way of Healing, by Norman Doidge, M.D.
John Pepper’s and my movement challenges are remarkably similar, plus we have two key
things in common: “an exercise program he devised” and “a special kind of concentration.” His
exercise program is walking. You know about mine. As for concentration, most people’s walking
movements are unconscious. That’s why they can walk and chew gum and talk on their cell
phones and walk the dog at the same time. For all his years of practice, John Pepper hasn’t been
able to get to that level. Instead, he walks and controls his tremors consciously. His mind has to
stay on the job; if he gets distracted or takes a day or even a moment off, his symptoms come
back.
My gait-trainer Tracy taught me the same thing. When I walk, I have a mantra I chant to myself:
“bend the knees, pick up the foot, wide stance….” If I stay on it, I can walk. If I leave it just for a
single step, my right foot immediately drags, even if I’m wearing the WalkAide. I hear the sole of
my shoe scuff against the pavement; I know I’ve lost concentration.
Pause. Note to self: stay focused. Take the next step.
When I first started my new walking practice, Janet sometimes came with me. We’d hold hands
and talk. Now I mostly go alone. Or if we go together, we don’t hold hands, and we don’t talk. I
have to concentrate. John Pepper has a couple decades of practice on me; he has learned to walk
and talk at the same time. I’m not there yet.
I wonder if I could learn to run the same way. Elite marathoners churn out 180 steps per minute.
For a 2:10:00 marathon, that’s 23,400 steps. That’s a lot of focus, a lot of mantras chanted. But it
may not be as far-fetched as it sounds, because of a third thing John Pepper and I have in
common: a fascinating bit of brain functioning that backs up what we’re doing.
We’ll talk about that next time.
                             The Straight Dope on Motivation


There’s a scene in my “What’s Your Impossible” video where I tap my chest and say, “I don’t
have to get motivated to do this. I run from here.”
I should have tapped my head. The heart is where we feel motivation, but the brain is where it
happens. It’s all about dope -- dopamine, that is.
Dopamine is the brain chemical behind “the pursuit of happiness.” When we think about getting
moving on something, it runs a cost-benefit analysis, and if the perceived reward outweighs the
cost, it gets behind the idea. We feel motivated. We get going. But if the ledger comes up short,
dopamine settles back on the couch and asks for more Cheetos.
I often experience MS that way -- like a Great Big I Don’t Wanna. I usually go work out
anyway, and feel better when I’m done, but not always. Sometimes there’s no feel good reward.
And sometimes (rarely, thankfully), it’s just too hard to push through, so I quit early, or don’t go
at all.
Norman Doidge explains all this in his profile of John Pepper, the “conscious walker” with
Parkinson’s Disease we met last time:
       The conventional view is that dopamine is essential for movement, and because people
       with [Parkinson’s Disease] have too little…, they can’t move. But it turns out that
       dopamine is also essential to “feel” that it is worth making a movement-- that is, people
       need dopamine to feel motivated to move in the first place.
       Thus dopamine has at least three characteristics relevant to [Parkinson’s Disease]: first, it
       enhances motivation to move; then it facilitates and quickens that movement; and finally
       it neuroplastically strengthens the circuits involved in the movement, so that movement
       will be easier next time. But if there is no motivation, no movement will occur.
       A recent study shows that the “motivation to move” goes awry in [Parkinson’s Disease].
       The importance [of this study] for understanding Parkinson’s cannot be underestimated:
       it is not simply that [Parkinson’s Disease patients] have an inherent inability to move
       normally and at a normal speed; the motivational component of their motor system is also
       fundamentally compromised.
       Parkinson’s Disease appears in its symptoms as a physical movement disorder, but it has
       roots that are “cognitive” or “mental,” and is thus as much a mental as a physical
       disorder.
       Which is precisely why it is problematic to teach Parkinson’s patients that the loss of
       dopamine prevents them from moving! This instruction will only reinforce passive
       resignation, at the very moment when that attitude needs to be undermined.
       This motivational lack is not a product of laziness or apathy or weakness of will. Rather,
       the brain’s dopamine-based motivation circuit often cannot energize particular
       movements, even when desired, and this appears as weariness or lassitude.
       That John Pepper was able to motivate himself to move, despite limited dopamine, attests
       to the vital force of his mind and will. But to translate that motivation still required a
       “neurological” discovery on his part. He still couldn’t do normal, everyday walking,
       which is automatic and habitual… until his conscious walking technique got around this
       circuit and allowed him to use other circuits.
               From The Brain’s Way of Healing, by Norman Doidge, M.D.
In other words, by doggedly sticking with his intent to walk, Pepper recruited other parts of his
brain to help him stay with it. Reminds me of someone I know.
Knowing about dopamine takes the mystery out of motivation. It also tells us there’s a
neuroplastic reward waiting for us if we can somehow defy the Great Big I Don’t Wanna and get
moving. We might not see the reward short-term, but long-term… yeah maybe.
Maybe something like learning to walk again.
                                        Time To Move


I thought John Pepper’s conscious walking story was the main gift for me in Norman Doidge’s
book The Brain’s Way of Healing, so I didn’t read the rest of the book and sent it back to the
library. Then I reordered it because I wanted the quotes for my post about dopamine and
motivation. There was a waiting list; it took several weeks to get it back; it came just in time for
me to write the post. I typed up the quotes, put the book in the return pile. That night, we had
dinner with some friends. He’d read the John Pepper post, and said something about it . His wife
chimed in, “Conscious movement… that sounds like Feldenkrais.”
I’d heard of Feldenkrais… something about massage, I thought. Then I remembered: the next
chapter in Doidge’s book was about Moshe Feldenkrais and his healing method. When we got
home, I took the book off the library return pile and read the chapter.
The Feldenkrais Method isn’t massage, physical therapy, kinesthesiology, yoga, energy
therapy… but it’s sort of all of them. Here’s the description on their Denver/Boulder
practitioners website:
       The Feldenkrais Method is a form of somatic education that uses gentle movement and
       directed attention to improve movement and enhance human functioning. Through this
       Method, you can increase your ease and range of motion, improve your flexibility and
       coordination, and rediscover your innate capacity for graceful, efficient movement. These
       improvements will often generalize to enhance functioning in other aspects of your life..
Bottom line: the Feldenkrais Method teaches you to move. And that has included people with
MS, Doidge’s book said.
I’ve gotten gun shy about diving into every new thing I hear about that might help me, so I sat on
the Feldenkrais idea for a few days, now and then glancing at the Denver practitioners’ web
pages. Then one afternoon the urgency came. I looked again, and Ray Little’s name jumped out
of the listings. I thought of further scanning the list, but no, he was the one. He had worked with
MS, his bio said. I made an appointment.
For the first hour and a half, Ray talked incessantly while I sat on an exercise ball and he gently
rubbed or manipulated a foot, leg, hand, arm, telling me to twist my hips, bend my spine, rotate
my shoulders. Walk with your whole body, he said. Walk from the ground up. Use gravity to
help you. Use counterbalance. It’s all about flow. On it went. “You obviously have an inner
practice,” he said, “So I might as well tell you all the theory, because you can absorb it.”
“There,” he finally said, “I had to teach you how to walk while you were sitting down so you’d
know how to do it when you stood up.”
Ray’s studio is a long room with a soft rubbery floor. For the next 30 minutes we walked back
and forth -- comfortably, easily, in a way I haven’t in years, maybe ever. Ray continued his
commentary, sharing my amazement and joking about it whenever I got out of the flow.
The next day I walked 12 blocks without the WalkAide, in a new easy walking style that Janet
labelled “sauntering.” It’s like you start a wave from the ground up and ride it. As soon as you
get out of sync with the wave, everything stops working. It’s as hard to explain as the
Feldenkrais Method is to describe. But it works. Amazing.

In last summer’s 2014 Running Past Our Limits Update, I said dealing with MS sometimes feels
like losing a race against time:
       When I saw my neurologist six months ago, I told him I’ve been surprised at how quickly
       my physical abilities are degenerating. It’s like there’s a timer running. I’m awesome on
       the machine at the gym, but when I walk it feels like time is running out. And running?
       Time ran out on that a long time ago.
I don’t feel that way anymore. For one thing, the race metaphor isn’t accurate. MS doesn’t have
its own speed or timeline apart from me. It’s something I have, something I do. It’s a friend and
adversary. We work together.
And for another thing, my life with MS has changed radically these past several months.
Winning? Losing? I can’t say. But I can say the changes have been unreal.
A couple weeks into gait-training, I strapped on the WalkAide and walked around the block.
Before that, I could go maybe half a block with a cane.
Nowadays, the cane stays in the closet. I like it best there.
A few times I’ve walked home without it from a workout. From a workout!!
I have to go back two years in my workout journal to find the same level of results I’m getting
now. I hit a nasty dip last month, but also bagged an aggressive goal I’ve been working on for six
months.
My successes have been catchy: a couple friends at the gym changed their workouts when they
saw what I was doing with mine, setting their own aggressive goals. We inspire and challenge
each other. It’s been fun.
I’ve toned down the Feldenkrais saunter a bit, but regularly use it to go 4-8 blocks without the
WalkAide. Thus far the record is 12.
My latest thing is to go up and down the stairs without holding onto the railings. I’ve pretty well
nailed a step at a time; now I’m going faster, and doing two steps at a time.
And you know how you stand on one leg and put the other in your jeans? I haven’t been able to
pick up my right leg and do that for years. Now I’m batting about .250.
Or how you sit down, pick up a foot, put a shoe on? Same deal.
Or how about going around a corner without holding onto something to keep your balance? Or
carrying something as you go up or down the stairs? All these little things that aren’t so little; not
anymore they aren’t.
My core strength has never been better, and just this past week I finally activated whatever
muscles you use to sit on the floor and lift your leg up -- again my right leg.
Plus with the Wahls Protocol/Paleo diet I just feel better. Stronger. More energy. Eating sugar
free is one of the best gifts I've ever given myself.
The list goes on and on. Amazing. Truly amazing. I say that a lot. It all takes conscious effort;
maybe that will continue, maybe not. Who cares.
I mean really… who cares?!

I’ve been at my machine marathon training for over three and a half years. The original idea was
that one day I wouldn’t need it anymore -- I would graduate to actual walking and running. I’ve
sometimes wondered how I would feel when graduation day came. I’ve been thinking more
about it lately, because for all my workouts have given me, they haven’t given me in several
years the progress I’ve made in just a few months with the WalkAide, gait training, Wahls
Protocol/Paleo diet, and Feldenkrais treatment. So maybe I should just can the workouts and go
for more walks, what do you think?
In the blog series before this one, I shared some early draft excerpts from a book I’m writing
about a new inner game I’ve begun to apply to my training. In one of those posts, I admitted that
I no longer believe I can actually run a marathon one day -- which puts the kibosh on the whole
plan, doesn’t it? I mean, I was going to quit my machine workouts when I started walking and
running again. I’m walking now, but not running. Isn’t that good enough? Wouldn’t this be a
good time to quit?
That idea got a major boost in the past couple months, when my brain and body staged a
rebellion against my workouts. Apparently all my dopamine vanished: I have no motivation, I
get no reward. My metrics have fallen way off. One day both legs gave out in the middle of a

Another friend saw me in the locker room. He looked worried. “You okay?” he asked. No lie this

end my Adaptive Motion Trainer days for good. Not for the original reason, but because I just
couldn’t do it anymore. Originally desired result, but for the wrong reason… entirely,
thoroughly, completely the wrong reason.
I bothered over that for a couple weeks, and then instead of letting my brain run around in circles
some more, I let my body decide. Duh.
I like the way my workouts make me feel. They’re extreme; they push my limits. And I like the

again.
Here’s the plan: four days a week on the machine, 90 minutes and 1,000 calories per session,
more on the weekend. Plus still alternating strength, speed, and endurance training like the real
marathon guys. Then on the alternate days I walk and do the stairs. And everyday I do all the
other stretching and core and other exercises (the daily part isn’t new; but I keep adding new
stuff all the time).
Thanks body, for making that easy.
Sometimes we just need to know where to get good advice.

         THE END AND THE BEGINNING


                  Hindsight: always so clear.
                     And always so smug.


Our inner game has to work in real time and space or not at all.
                                         Three Years


Running Past Our Limits 2014 marked three years of training. What did I have to show for it?
       MS, for one thing.
       Plus a remarkable side effect: except for MS, I was healthier than ever.
       Which created a quandary for my inner game.
Getting rid of MS wasn’t the game when I started, but three years later it was, and I was losing.
Meanwhile I was winning the mind-body wellness game. Losing one game while winning the
other wasn’t sustainable; my inner game wasn’t doing well, trying to hold conflicting results in
tension. I was suspended between the two, in limbo. Something had to give.
I needed to play a different game with MS. I needed new perspective and awareness, meaning
and purpose, beliefs and aspirations, thoughts and behaviors, focus and goals. I needed an
outlook that could embrace both MS and wellness, and let me keep up my extreme training
experiment without discouragement.
I didn’t know all that when I finished Running Past Our Limits 2014. I just knew I was stuck. So
I did what I do when I get stuck: I wrote. I’m a writer, a word guy -- words go deep with me, dig
stuff out of my soul, haul it out where I can take a look. Right after Running Past Our Limits
2014, I broke with precedent and started the 2015 installment instead of waiting another year, as
I had before. I also started writing this book. Those two projects working in tandem gave me the
hindsight and insight I needed.
Only this time, the writing was different. There was an urgency to it; I wrote out of compulsion
more than choice, rarely understanding what I was writing or why, sometimes getting insight
from dreams and often waking up to whole new chapters I copied out, like taking dictation.
Eventually, I got the new inner game I needed, but the revelations came slowly, only after
hindsight had a chance to sift through all the new material my writing was generating. Reflecting
on three years of Running Past Our Limits blog posts, I saw a lot of things I didn’t see when I
wrote them.
                                   Hindsight: always so clear.
                                      And always so smug.
Hindsight’s tone is oh yeah, I had it figured out all along. Don’t be fooled. The journey teaches;
hindsight trails along behind, taking notes until one days it’s ready to deliver its summation. I
didn’t know all along; it took months of reflection and general soul trouble to coax out of me
what you’re about to read.
                                           Either/Or


Hindsight eventually revealed that I had developed my inner game around an either/or: either
MS will get worse, or I will make it go away.
They call that “dualistic thinking.” We get fixated on perceived opposites, choose one over the
other, label it as “good” vs. the other’s “bad,” and shut ourselves off from other creative
solutions.
Faced with the dualistic choice I had framed for myself, I did what any life athlete would do: I
threw myself into making MS go away. I was fierce, doing that. Plus inspired. I’m good at
inspiration. Life athletes do inspiration really well; it’s our fuel of choice. Inspiration gets us
going, keeps us going. We don’t get inspired, we are inspired. It’s what we do. Facing down big
challenges is particularly inspiring. It heightens meaning and purpose, sharpens intention and
focus, mobilizes courage and fortitude. The bigger the better. Bring it on.
I liked who I became, being inspired and fierce. People were inspired by my ferocity, admired it,
got inspired with me. But ultimately there was always a qualifier, an unspoken, “Well we’ll see.”
See what?
             We’ll see if all your determination and effort gives you what you want.
After three years of training, I still wasn’t better. There was no denying it anymore: the outcome
I wanted had become impossible.
                                            Impossible


                              I have MS. It’s supposed to get worse.
                                      I must pursue healing.
                                  But wait.. healing is impossible.

When exactly did overcoming MS through my training become impossible? The sequence went
like this:
        I faced a physical challenge I thought was a rehab issue.
        I got an idea for a new kind of rehab.
        I recast my entire personality and life into the mold of an athlete.
        So far so good, but then I was diagnosed with a chronic, progressive, degenerative
        neuromuscular disease called MS that explained and defined my physical challenges in a
        new way.
        I dealt with the diagnosis through all the predictable stages of denial, anger, grief, and all
        the rest.
        Now I needed not just an idea, but a whole bunch of new ideas.
        I started by redefining “primary progressive MS” as “Elite Level MS.” and made my
        training as elite as I could.
        “Life athlete” became my operative self-concept.
        I structured every waking and sleeping hour of my life around my training.
        I became an autodidact on sports psychology, neuroscience, nutrition, diet.
        I learned new scientific theory, and put it into practice.
        I drank snake oil by the case, and gulped down placebos.
        I was remade from the inside out.
        My thoughts, outlook, and vocabulary changed.
        I went on a mission to share what I learned -- in books, blogs, and workshops.
        I got into the best shape and overall good health of my life, until now, every single day, I
        routinely, habitually, deliberately experience more levels of consciousness, awareness,
        mindfulness, wellness, and all kinds of other new realities I never knew existed before all
        this started.
All that… in the midst of all of that… that’s when it happened… that’s when my goal became
impossible.
Why do I say that?
                           Because none of that had any effect on MS.
I’d been running a ledger: MS on one side, wellbeing on the other.
       MS is my brain full of “multiple scarring,” foreign bodies in my spinal fluid, damaged
       myelin sheaths around nerves, an autoimmune system that can’t tell the difference
       between friend and foe, stuff leaking from my digestive track into my bloodstream and
       getting attacked, and a bunch of other organic realities. All that results in mind and body
       fatigue, loss of strength and balance, numbness, muscle tightness, cramping, spasticity.
       MS makes walking an ordeal, running impossible. MS means I can’t do lots of things on
       purpose that I used to do on autopilot.
       Wellbeing is overall mind-body health that has never been richer, fuller, more awesome.
       I’ve done my best to deal with MS, embrace its losses and turn them into gains, and my
       whole health system has vastly benefited as a result.
MS had become my life practice, the reason for all that wellness. How could I not be grateful,
and amazed?
But there was still one critical entry missing from the wellbeing side of the ledger -- one gift MS
hadn’t given me that I wanted more than all the others:
                                          To be rid of it.
Without that entry, the ledger sums to impossible. MS wins. My inner game did its best to play,
but it lost. It couldn’t beat impossible.
                                    Disappointment Redux


The sequence I just described was the same one I wrote about in Life Beyond Reason. I’d been
here before; I was repeating a pattern. It goes like this:
       I face a challenge, get an idea, get inspired, launch with enthusiasm.
       The parade of surprises and challenges begins. I react, adjust, press on. More surprises,
       more adjustments. I keep pressing on, try new things, download patches, create work-
       arounds.
       More surprises pile up, and more adjustments. They compound and magnify, take on
       their own substance and energy.
       Before long, I’m focusing on them, not on the original goal, making adjustments to the
       adjustments.
       I turn increasingly inward. My inner game becomes the game. The goals I started with
       drift, go unmet.
       My internal dialogue and outward actions become noisy and dissonant. I lose clarity,
       focus, decisiveness, confidence, effectiveness… and eventually lose heart. I think about
       giving up.
Anybody else ever done that?
I thought so. Just had to ask.
Challenges and surprises are part of the deal when we get inspired and move toward a big goal.
No movement, no problem, but get inspired and get moving, and we want to keep up the
momentum. We dig deep, get resourceful, solve problems. Good for us, but in time all that
adjusting starts to thicken, pile up, get heavy. Unresolved doubts accumulate around the edges,
create drag and fog our goggles.
All of that is to be expected, but the problem with the pattern is that it ends up in frustration and
discouragement. That’s where I went when my training wore on for several years without
altering the reality of MS in my life. As a result, my inner game got bogged down in something I
came to call “the Faith Follies.”
                                              Faith


We want something that’s not part of our reality. Faith is how we go about making it happen --
how we make the invisible, intangible, unreal, and impossible into their opposites. Making that
happen is faith’s job.
There are two sides to faith. One is what we have faith in: people, God, science, religion, other
disciplines and ideologies. The other is how we practice our faith -- how we think, speak, and act
because of what we have faith in.
Our inner game uses faith both ways. It takes the things we believe in and turns them into
thoughts, words, and deeds, in order to bring things we want into our experience.
As we practice our faith, we carry out an ongoing inner conversation among what we want, what
we think we can expect, and what’s actually happening. And at some point, either our faith is
achieving the outcome we want or it’s not. When it’s not, it’s easy to fall into the Faith Follies.
                                         The Faith Follies


Here’s that definition of ethos again:
         Ethos is our characteristic spirit, as manifested in our beliefs and aspirations.
Our beliefs and aspirations spring from our core, our ethos. Our lives and psyches are thick with
beliefs and aspirations. They pile up in layers: beliefs about our beliefs, beliefs blurring into
aspirations, aspirations made of beliefs turned inside out. We believe something, we aspire to
something; we aspire to beliefs that will manifest our aspirations; we believe our aspirations will
prove out our beliefs. At each deeper level the distinctions blur and thicken, become more
substantial and foundational, guiding and shaping ourselves and our lives.
                        Once we believe things, we believe in our beliefs.
                  Once we aspire to things, we believe in our aspiring for them.
We want our beliefs and aspirations to be strong and dependable. We want to be able to lean and
stand and rely on them. We want them to be True North, to keep us aligned. We want them to be
bigger, stronger, more dependable and vigorous than we feel we are, so we extract them from the
substance of ourselves and project them outside of ourselves, give them names to shore them up,
turn them into ologies, rules, theories, doctrines. We take them from outside and drive them deep
into our internal soil.
                        We practice our beliefs and aspirations by faith.
Faith is what goes on in our consciousness when we reach inside and put our beliefs and
aspirations to work where we can watch how they do.
We live in a constant faith experiment. Faith is a work in progress, and so are we and our lives.
We constantly test, challenge, refine our faith. We hold it to high standards; we want it to
perform.
We don’t really know how beliefs and aspirations become manifest, how we use them to create
realities in our lives. We don’t know how that works, not really. So we’re always tinkering with
our faith, trying this or that -- maybe something we heard or read somewhere, or maybe
something we dreamed up in the silence.
        When faith fails to perform, we feel we owe it an explanation, a second chance.
Faith sometimes works, or seems to. Or doesn’t, or seems not to.
When it doesn’t, we feel edgy and off balance. We don’t have what faith was supposed to bring
us. Something must be wrong with our faith. We examine it, look for conditions, contingencies.
We make excuses for it, tweak, adjust, clarify, equivocate.
Or maybe we’re the problem. We examine ourselves. We test our motives, the purity of our
practice.
We might even examine the things we have faith in. This is touchy ground: our beliefs and our
beliefs about beliefs are sacred to us. We hold onto them, believing. To examine them is
sacrilege, by definition.
After all that, faith sometimes works, or seems to. Or doesn’t, or seems not to.
Rewind, repeat.
We work our practice of faith like it’s something to be cured and corrected, gotten right. All
because we don’t know how faith works, not really. Or if it does. We wish we knew. We keep
trying. We don’t give up.
                     Too much of that, and we get caught in the Faith Follies.
Faith gets stuck a loop: faith in faith, beliefs about beliefs. Faith circles back, returns to itself,
becomes its own focus, its own end game, something to be figured out, improved. Faith becomes
its own weather system: we search the skies for winds and omens, wary of the static build up,
the Sturm und Drang.
                            The Faith Follies take us off our inner game.
Too many loops, too much not getting it right, and our inner game practice becomes a maybe, an
unsubstantiated possibility -- tenuous, weak, unreliable. Disappointment, discouragement,
disheartenment, despair become our inheritance and our legacy. We’re tentative, uncertain,
indecisive, unfocused.
We’re no longer practicing our inner game. We’re practicing faith for its own sake. We’re
languishing in the doldrums, not going anywhere and still calling it a voyage. We’re out of our
game.
That’s the Faith Follies.
                                           You Again!


I’d been caught in the Faith Follies before -- several times during my life, as a matter of fact, and
more on that later, but most recently and dramatically during my Life Beyond Reason days.
Producing a theater spectacle proved to be impossible -- not for somebody else, but for me, the
person I was then. Crashing up against that impossible resulted in personal transformation and a
whole new inner game.
My faith practice fell into the Faith Follies again when it ran up against the impossibility of MS.
My Life Beyond Reason inner game, good as it was, hadn’t dealt entirely with the Faith Follies.
That’s why the pattern repeated itself.
I can think of lots of reasons why, but mostly it was about control.
                                   Cause and Effect Faith


Cause and effect is the scientific gold standard. We love it for the rest of life, too. We look at
someone and say they’ve got it down to a science, and aren’t they something. Art is fun, but
science… now you’re talkin’. Cause and effect rocks because it gives us control.
My inner game was all about control. And why not? Control makes for the ultimate inner game.
If we have control, we can predict outcomes. Faith becomes a formula: do this, get that. Simple,
effective, appealing. Line up all your causes and effects and put your faith on cruise control.
Don’t I wish.
My attempts at cause and effect faith made me say things like this:
       Some online research revealed what an incredible gift I’d just been given. The
       AMT/Open Stride would teach my body perfect running form, which would get patterned
       directly into my brain and the rest of my nervous system, until one day I would transfer
       the running (not gliding!) movement to the earth beneath my feet.
                Opportunity Knocks 2013
       God bless that rat. It’s my hero; I want to be just like it. We all know you can’t move
       again after your spinal cord is severed. The rat proved us wrong. We also know you can’t
       win a marathon if your MS makes it hard to walk. How about if I be the lab rat who
       proves us wrong?
       The rat needed scientific help, and so do I. Seriously, now: if by any stretch you know
       someone who is seriously researching MS and exercise, would you please email me with
       an introduction?
                Science and Snake Oil 2014
For awhile, I really believed science was on my side. What began as a hunch -- maybe I could
use moving on a machine to reprogram my body and brain to move off the machine -- turned out
to have neuroscientific validity. Mine was an extreme and unproven hypothesis, yes, and
obviously I wasn’t going about my experiment scientifically, but it wasn’t too farfetched to think
neuroplasticity and re-myelination might make it happen for me.
I went along for months believing that science might be on my side, but after three years, my
training hadn’t reversed the effects of MS in my body. There was no cause and effect, and least
not yet.
In the meantime, I hedged my control bets with cause and effect’s lookalike: correlation.
                                        Correlation Faith


Science has its place, but its place isn’t every place. “All things being equal” works in the lab; in
the rest of life, the variables keep varying.
We shouldn’t be surprised. For over a century, science has been telling us about quantum
control, where our participation and expectations change both outcomes and observations. We
know that in the lab, but in the rest of life most of our brains haven’t caught up with quantum
theory; we’re still working on Einstein, or we’re all the way back at Newton, or maybe
Descartes.
When we can’t turn our inner game into a science, correlation steps up and asks if we’d like a
hand. Correlation is the placebo science acknowledges but doesn’t respect. This was going on
and that happened, we’re not sure why, but there’s this odd coincidence, there’s no proof one
causes the other, but still….
That’s correlation.
Who can build faith on that?
We do it all the time. We dignify correlation with cause and effect status. We practice it like it’s
science, eager as we are to de-mystify the Mystery, lay a hand on the beast and not get devoured.
Besides, who knows, maybe one day correlation will prove out, grow up and become cause and
effect. It will be the new science; we’ll have been right all along in our believing.
You saw my version of correlation faith in Running Past Our Limits:
        The highlight for me came when a medical researcher talked about the placebo effect:
        how people get well not from “real” treatment, but because they believe they will. “Find
        your placebo,” he advised. “if you think it will help you, it probably will.” I felt smug;
        I’d already found mine. Is it crazy to believe that elliptical marathon training will get me
        across a finish line? No crazier than people getting better taking placebos.
        Find Your Placebo (2013)
        The rat introduces the X Factor: how does the desire to move generate healing? The
        scientists don’t know, and the rat’s not talking. If it could, it would probably sell us snake
        oil, and we’d probably buy.
        Last year I wrote about the “placebo effect”: believing yourself into an altered state in
        which the healing you want actually happens, even though it’s not supposed to, there’s no
        rational reason why it should, and you’re actually putting your faith in a sugar pill.
        I know one guy who got rid of his MS by visualizing miniature beavers chewing up his
        brain scarring (“Multiple Sclerosis” means “multiple scarring”). I’m not making that up.
        If that’s not a placebo I don’t know what is. I’ve haven’t tried his approach, but I’m not
       above a daily practice that includes mind tricks, even though I honestly believe some of
       what I do is pure snake oil.
       Science and Snake Oil (2014)
Correlation is science and snake oil teaming up to make a joke at their own expense, tantalizing
us with a mockery of themselves. Correlation only plays at cause and effect. When pressed, it
retreats into knack, happenstance, and superstition, then comes clean, reveals the joke it’s been
playing on us, on our science and snake oil, on our faith:
                              Correlation isn’t in control after all;
                                       it’s just faking it.
                                      Magical Thinking


Psychologists call correlation faith “magical thinking.” Again, the issue is control. Control not
working for you? Okay then, give it up entirely and look for miraculous intervention.
I’d had a long history of magical thinking. Like the Faith Follies, it also predated my Life
Beyond Reason inner game, and therefore was embedded into it, undetected until MS smoked it
out.
Magical thinking skews your risk/return curve toward high risk-taking. If you want something
that’s apparently out of your control, then why not take a leap of faith to get there? Maybe
whoever or whatever is in control will bail you out, throw out a net to catch you.
I had a bad case of that, and the result was a boom/bust pattern in my life. No, not always -- there
were times of respite, sometimes significant -- but inevitably, yes, the cycle would repeat. The
Strobe and the Faith Follies sat at opposite ends of a pendulum arc: when I reached the limits of
one I would throw myself back on the other. All in the name of finding the magic source code to
get me what I wanted.
There are less destructive and more productive ways to practice faith; I just never got to them.
I’m being brutal with the honesty here, but hindsight rarely minces words.
I’m not saying don’t have faith in things, or don’t take placebos, or any of that. I’m just saying
that when my faith practice -- my inner game -- didn’t get the results I wanted, I leaned harder on
old habits in pursuit of the elusive and illusory goal of control. The pursuit was in vain, but the
screwy thing was, my risky faith practice won me a lot of approbation, both from myself and
others. Magical thinking looks and feels like what we think faith should look and feel like.
Apparently I was a man of great faith, not to mention great imagination and inspiration.
Too bad my MS wasn’t getting better as a result.
More brutal honesty.
Sigh.
Magical thinking is related to something else that hindsight revealed about my inner game: it
had become infatuated with something I came to call “the Strobe.”
                                The Strobe And The Magic


The Strobe is when you’re watching a magic trick and suddenly at the key moment a strobe
flashes off at the edge of your peripheral vision, distracts you, makes you miss how the magic is
done.
It’s a cheap way to do a trick, and a real magician would never do it, but that’s what happens
when we’ve got the Strobe working in our thinking. One moment there’s one reality and
suddenly the next moment Flash!! there’s another.
                                      Flash!! It’s a miracle!
                                        Flash!! It’s magic!
We like flash. It looks the way we think magic ought to look, so we believe in it, desire it, seek
it, have faith in it. We’re dazzled by it, in awe of it. We want to be dazzled because our lives
seem so non-dazzling, so devoid of magic.
The Strobe makes us think the magic comes from a reality other than this one. We can’t build an
inner game on that. We have to play our game in this reality. We need the real magic of our real
lives.
Not so, says the Strobe: what we want lies in an alternate reality, and our task is to go there to
get it and bring it back here.
Figuring out how to do that becomes our focus.
Game over.
Enthralled by the Strobe, we miss the real magic, how it’s created. We buy an illusion instead:
that the magic we need is removed, aloof, detached, inaccessible.
That’s the Strobe. My game was full of it.
                                     Life in Two Realities


The Strobe is where we get the idea that we’re inadequate, wrong, misplaced, that life isn’t
working because we’re not, there’s something screwed up about us. There’s an ideal of us and
our lives that we could have if we could just figure out how to reach it, and things would be
better if we could, but we can’t so they aren’t.
Better than what?
                                        Better. Than. Here
                                        Better. Than. Now.
                                         Just better, okay?
Better. Than. Here. becomes the standard we live by. If we have to be this self living this life,
then too bad for us. No, we have to reach outside ourselves, get what we want from somewhere
else because we’re not getting it here, and we’re not going to. We’re not okay. We’re not living
up to the ideal of us. We can’t be trusted.
So go the accusations.
All that is Strobe thinking -- projecting, pretending, dealing in hypotheticals, trafficking in what
isn’t, looking for what might and could be anywhere but here.
Strobe thinking makes people say things like this:
       You’ve long since figured out that this series of blog posts isn’t about running marathons
       on the elliptical machine. It’s about doing things we know are impossible. Apparently we
       can live in two realities at once. In one, there is no possible way we can do the thing we
       want. In the other reality, we can, and the first reality’s negative opinion doesn’t count.
       Gliding Into the New Normal (2012)
       In the meantime, my new rehab theory wasn’t proving out. The faster and further I ran,
       the less I could walk. It made no sense: on the machine I could do the impossible; in real
       life I could barely do what’s possible for most people. Diagnosis and Denial (2013)
       When I saw my neurologist six months ago, I told him I’ve been surprised at how quickly
       my physical abilities are degenerating. It’s like there’s a timer running. I’m awesome on
       the machine at the gym, but when I walk it feels like time is running out. That’s my
       reality. Or is it? No it’s not, but you already knew that. I don’t like living with that timer,
       so I relegate it to an alternate reality and go on living in the reality I’ve chosen, where
       working out aggressively on a machine turns into healing. Life In An Alternate Reality
       (2014)
Strobe thinking says impossible happens somewhere else, not here, not now, not in this life, but
in some alternate universe where it remains inscrutable, unreachable.
Strobe thinking doesn’t work for an inner game. Our inner game has to work in real time and
space or not at all.
Strobe thinking doesn’t just weaken our inner game, it destroys it, leaves us disturbed, divided,
fractured, in tension.
       All this fracturing and tearing our souls in the name of Better. Than. Here.
       All this because we believe in two realities: one here but inadequate; the other Better.
       Than. Here.
       We don’t have Better. Than. Here. We have to get to an alternate universe to get it, or
       figure out how to get that alternate universe here, where it can rescue us.
       But we can’t. We can’t make it happen.
       All this because we’re living with two realities… meanwhile living in neither.
                                             Visualizing


Athletes use visualizing. I did, too. Visualizing is when we use our imaginations to create a
reality in our brains -- shooting free throws, playing piano -- and our brains return the favor by
making it happen in real time. Too bad that’s not the way I practiced it. That way I practiced it
was full of the Strobe.
You saw this in Meet Coach (2012):
          I also started visualizing. You hear about athletes doing that. If nothing else, it’s a good
          way to pass the time when you’re on an elliptical for an hour – a whole lot better than
          watching the energy-sucking crap on all the TV’s. I’d see myself running a marathon,
          being the shocking old white dude who could keep up with those guys from Kenya and
          Ethiopia who can run impossibly far impossibly fast. I became an international marathon
          phenom. It was fun, and the best part was, I always won every race.
I have a good imagination; visualizing comes easily. I got choked up, imagining groups of
supporters along the race route. My eyes and heart welled up; I felt the love. It was like watching
an inspiring movie but then it was over and nothing had changed. I went blinking and squinting
back into the lobby, thick with the smell of popcorn.
I said in the above blog post that my visualization practice was a “good way to pass the time.”
Really.
I’m not in this for a good way to pass the time.
I hadn’t won any races, I wasn’t an international marathon phenom. I knew it, and so did MS.
The Strobe is subtle, and devastating.
                                             Imagine


I’d been playing games with imagination, too.
A friend read an early draft chapter of this book where I said impossible is something we can’t
imagine, and that my goal of winning marathons was impossible because I couldn’t imagine
doing it. He emailed this comment, calling my bluff:
       It’s difficult for me to get my head around the idea of aspiring to something I can’t even
       imagine. If I can’t even imagine it, how do I know it’s impossible? In my own experience,
       and I suspect this is true of most others, is that if I aspire, it is always to something I can
       identify.
I wasn’t being honest, saying I couldn’t imagine being able to run. I could imagine it -- so well,
that I could feel what it would be like, just like I could feel the love coming from those cheering
crowds I visualized while grinding away on the elliptical. Plus, my subconscious was on board
with the idea of running and moving easily again: I sometimes dreamt about it, and still do.
Trying to say something is impossible when I could create it in my imagination and
subconscious was dishonest and disingenuous.
Busted.
I love imagination; it makes life fun, rich, deep, marvelous. And like visualization, it’s a
powerful inner game tool, just not the way I was using it.
                                          I Am Aware


… of people who got quick relief from MS through diet and visualizing. I wrote about them in
Running Past Our Limits. A couple are my friends. Awesome! Great for them! I wish it was so
for me. But it’s not.
MS wasn’t impressed with my reaching for Better. Than. Here.
Or my misguided attempts at visualizing and imagining.
My inner game thought they should work; it kept trying to fix them.
Meanwhile, MS was like a siege, settling in for the long term, my symptoms getting
progressively worse. My training had been reduced to throwing itself ineffectively at an
immovable and unrelenting reality.
I could engage in the Faith Follies all day long, trying to turn my faith into something that could
get miraculous results like those other people. I could, and have, but maybe hindsight’s best gift
to me is that it stopped me from doing that anymore.
My faith practice had become old and clichéd, had lost its vibrancy and freshness, its verve and
power. I needed to find a different way.
                                 It Could Have Been Worse


“But just think how much worse off you might be if you hadn’t been doing what you’ve been
doing.”
People will say that. They mean it as encouragement, like it will motivate me. It doesn’t. It feels
like a patronizing pat on the head. Besides, it’s the weakest shot at correlation you can get.
Even my neurologist trotted it out. He’s a neurologist, for crying out loud -- a medical science
nerd. He ought to know better.
Why would I want to think about how much worse off I might be? Besides, how could I ever
know?
Same thing when I tell someone I have MS and they say, “I’m sorry.” I’ve learned to say, “Don’t
be sorry, I’m not. MS is part of who I am, and I’m not sorry about me. MS has given me a lot of
gifts. I’m not sorry about it. You don’t need to be either.”
Or when people who know I have MS ask if I’m having a good day. Again, I’ve learned to stop,
look them in the eye and say, “You know, good and bad days don’t work for me anymore.
They’re not helpful. I don’t have good and bad days. I don’t know what’s going to happen
moment by moment. So good and bad are irrelevant.”
Yes, I get some looks. They’ll think about it, maybe, or maybe not. There’s one guy at the
gym… He means well, I’ve given him the good/bad speech. but he just won’t give up on the
question. I’ve learned to ignore it, change the subject. Some conversations aren’t worth the
energy.
“Sometimes you’re like a porcupine,” my wife said recently. She had just asked if I could
“manage” to do something, and I growled at her. We talked about it later. I get prickly; I admit it.
She was trying to be considerate, she said. What kind of consideration do I want instead?
“Don’t make MS an issue,” I said. “Don’t try to guess how I feel. How I feel is irrelevant. I don’t
care how I feel. I never know what’s going to happen when I sit or stand or take a step. If I can’t
do something, I’ll tell you.
“You’ll have to trust me.”
That’s when we both chuckled. I can’t always be trusted to know my limits.
Which is kind of the point, isn’t it?
                              One More Try With The Boulder


Ironically, it wasn’t the Strobe or the Faith Follies that finally crashed my inner game, it was all
the new progress I wrote about in Running Past Our Limits 2015. I didn’t realize it at the time
(again, hindsight had to show me), but trying all the new things I wrote about -- my uneventful
stint at being a lab rat, conscious walking, the WalkAide, gait training, Feldenkrais, the new diet
-- meant I was finally ditching the Strobe and the Faith Follies.
It all looked like progress, but in the end I wasn’t any closer to my goal. All that new progress
amounted to no more than one last gallant heave-ho.
Like Sisyphus, I almost got the boulder over the crest of the hill.
Almost, but not quite.
The boulder rolled back down the hill -- over me, on its way down.
My inner game finally had to give up.

                         but more than that, my desire to heal myself of MS,
                                       really was impossible.

A long time running on inspiration without results was wearing me out. Inspiration is life itself to
me, but life itself was getting weary.
At stake was the most inviolate drive of a life athlete’s soul: that urge to do something about it.
It’s our very last line of defense. Take it away, and we are nothing. We don’t exist anymore, not
in this world or any other. It’s over.
And yet…

the end of Running Past Our Limits 2015, it had shut down. There was only one thing left to do.

                                   It Has To Be That Way


When I finally faced the impossibility of MS, the first thing to do was give up.
It has to be that way. Nothing we’re already doing is any good against impossible. That’s
obvious: if it were, what we used to call impossible wouldn’t be impossible anymore, it would
be our reality. To say we’re giving up isn’t accurate, not really; it’s more like admitting the
impossible newness that impossible requires.
Impossible means it’s time to start -- not start again, just start -- start something so new we can’t
imagine it. Impossible is so new, it doesn’t even exist. We can’t get to that impossible newness
by doing what we’re already doing. As long as we keep trying the same things, impossible
remains… well, impossible.
But here’s the amazing thing: the newness we need is there for us. We just need a different way
to find it -- a whole new inner game. That’s why this book doesn’t end here, with me giving up.
There is a way to get to the newness. But before we do, we need to entirely give up on what’s not
working. Hindsight is good with history, not with moving ahead, and newness is way out of its
reach. For me to move ahead, I needed a new guide.
Impossible itself stepped up. First it blasted everything I’d been doing all the way to my
delusional alternate reality. Then it met the conditions I didn’t even know I’d been placing on
giving up. There were three:
        Giving up had to make sense;
        It had to preserve the wellbeing side of the ledger;
        And it couldn’t betray my friends.
                                          The First Two


Hindsight had already supplied a list of things it made sense to give up. The Strobe and Faith
Follies were top on the list, and easy to let go.
Inspiration and imagination could have been hard -- I’d held them sacred for a long time -- but
once I realized I didn’t have to give them up, the issue was how I’d been practicing them, they
were easy, too. I could stay inspired and I could still make things up.
My training regime was also easy. The last Running Past Our Limits 2015 post, Time To Move…
On?, announced that I would “run” less and walk more. That idea lasted about two weeks. I
missed how my aggressive workouts made me feel, so I reinstated them and increased their
intensity.
The rest of the wellbeing side of ledger also remained intact. No need to give any of that up.
As for the MS side of the ledger, it didn’t make sense to give up on getting rid of MS: that
would be the end of too much -- mostly the end of me. I wasn’t going there. But how could I
not? That would be more denial, wouldn’t it?
I’d been wrestling with those questions for years. But this time, finally, I saw that that what I
needed to give up on was getting rid of MS as an outcome I could control. The idea wasn’t to
give up on getting over MS, but to give up on believing that anything I can think or do will ever
make that happen. Quit trying to control my may to an impossible outcome. Quit trying to be the
judge of whether things are working or not.
Okay, well, I can do that.
Plus, if impossible is not an outcome to be achieved, there is no failure or shame in not achieving
it, or any lack in not having it.
This is looking good.
But what about that internal locus of control that life athletes are so good at? If you mess with
that, it’s a set up for despair -- or for unhappiness and unproductiveness, which might be the
same thing.
Something I read helped with that. Turns out that people with an internal locus of control tend
not to lose it, no matter what. This is from The Organized Mind, by Daniel J. Levitin, Ph.D.,
professor of Psychology and Behavioral Neuroscience at McGill University.
       Locus of control appears to be a stable internal trait that is not significantly affected by
       experiences. That is, you might expect that people who experience a great deal of
       hardship would give up any notions of their own agency in the face of overwhelming
       evidence to the contrary and become externals…. But the research doesn’t bear this out.
So… even if I give up on control, my urge to do something about MS won’t go away, and I
won’t fall into despair. I’m liking this. It makes sense.
Let’s see… giving up makes sense, the wellbeing side of the ledger is still intact, that makes two
out of three. Now how about my friends? How can I tell them I’m giving up and not feel guilty?
Turned out I already had.
                                           No I Don’t


When I began writing this book, I posted a series of drafts on my blog, thinking reader feedback
might help me understand what I was writing about. A post called No I Don’t was part of that
series. In it, I summarized much of the book’s contents without realizing that’s what I’d done.
We’ve been talking about hindsight. No I Don’t was foresight, too. It started like this:
       Some people following this blog have asked: Do you really think you can run a
       marathon?
       We were going to get there eventually, but since you asked, we’ll go there now.
       The answer is, no I don’t. I don’t think I can run a marathon. There is nothing in my
       experience or imagination to suggest that my mental and physical training, diet, or
       anything else will make it possible.
Most people were surprised. Others were also relieved: it was about time. And some didn’t
believe me, because right away I started Running Past Our Limits 2015, with its reports of new
progress. Apparently, if I had given up, it hadn’t changed anything. True. Nothing had changed
on the outside: I was still doing what I was doing. And nothing had changed on the inside either.
Not yet. That was coming.
But in the meantime, apparently I hadn’t betrayed my friends’ support after all. At least they
didn’t think so. I was still in the MS game -- quite how wasn’t clear -- and they were still in it
with me. They were giving me the benefit of the doubt, wondering -- like me -- where this new
adventure in ethos was going to take me. I speculated about that in the rest of the blog post, with
an accuracy that was amazing, considering I had only the vaguest sense about ethos at the time:
       The follow up question is: Then, um, why do you still want to do it? The answer is both
       more complex and more important, because it’s central to the inner game of ethos.
       (This book is a work in progress; I’m answering with the understanding I have at this
       moment.)
       Remember our definition of ethos: the characteristic spirit of a culture, era, or
       community as manifested in its beliefs and aspirations. Now let’s personalize it: ethos is
       the characteristic spirit of a human being, as manifested in his or her beliefs and
       aspirations.
       For the first couple years, I aspired to run a marathon and believed I could train my body
       and brain to do it. I don’t believe that anymore. Running a marathon has moved out of the
       realm of belief and aspiration. That inner game isn’t working. I need a new one: the
       inner game of ethos.
       Ethos operates by a different set of dynamics than beliefs and aspirations. Ethos is
       manifested in our beliefs and aspirations. Ethos is primary; beliefs and aspirations are
       secondary. Ethos is the source; beliefs and aspirations are the outward expression.
       We often use the term “faith” for the inner game of beliefs and aspirations. We think
       faith doesn’t need to play by the normal rules of life, that by faith we can aspire to things
       that aren’t possible. Faith encourages us to think on one level (it's not possible) and
       believe on another (by faith it's possible).
       The trouble is, our psyches don’t do well holding that kind of conflict. We often resolve
       it by reference to a sense of something -- a higher power or personality or purpose or
       meaning -- that’s bigger and better and more knowing and powerful than we are. That
       something holds a cosmic trump card: if it plays the card, our reality will change.
       Faith can be hard to sustain. We fuel our faith with the inspiration we feel when our
       aspirations reach beyond the realm of the possible. It’s noble to reach high. We like the
       idea that there are realities out there, beyond our own reality, that like it when we do, so
       much they they just might respond by stepping up and doing something miraculous for
       us.
       Ethos invites us to a different kind of practice. Faith evokes both conflict and inspiration,
       but ethos sits down right in the middle of all the opposites, paradoxes, conundrums,
       contradictions, oxymorons, inconsistencies, mysteries, enigmas, and all those other
       shadowy, shifty cousins that hang out in our psyches. Ethos doesn’t believe all those
       conflicts will resolve in our favor. Instead, it allows all of them to exist together without
       resolution, then dissolves them, makes them irrelevant. Cause/ effect. Here/ there. Now/
       then. Have/ have not. Right/ wrong. Good/ bad. Energy/ mass. Knowing/ not knowing.
       Purpose/ aimlessness. Meaning/ emptiness. Sacred/ secular. And all the rest. Ethos
       practice moves us into, through, and beyond them, unplugs their significance and control.
       Ethos practice doesn’t play by the rules of inspirational faith. Those were the rules before
       life as we know it ended. In our new, post-apocalyptic world of ethos, the issue is not
       how we believe our way to the impossible things we aspire to -- i.e., how we live by faith
       -- but who are we in the process -- how we embody all those opposites, paradoxes, and
       all the rest. That is the practice of ethos.
       For now, that’s your answer. It’s going to take a whole book to get there, but that’s where
       we’re going.
That blog post was both hindsight and foresight. Now that I’d written it, I needed to broaden and
deepen into its meaning. There was more, much more ground to cover on my journey into ethos.
And yes, it will take the rest of this book for you and me to go there together.
                                              Teardown


We give up on many levels. We start with the known and familiar, end with the hidden and
surprising, even shocking. We progress through layers of perspective and belief, knowing and
projection, then move down through those uncomfortable layers of emotion and shadow.
It’s a total teardown: first the plaster, then the lath, the studs and old wiring exposed and dust
balls and places where the builders penciled dimensions and did arithmetic and wrote their
girlfriends’ names… keep going until we’re down to the bare frailty, the musty-smelling
framework and the chalk marks on the floor.
It all goes, down to the beliefs and aspirations most sacred to us, and with them all those things
we’ve had faith in, and the ways we’ve practiced faith… eventually all of it, abandoned, given
up, everything gone until all that’s left is what lies at the core of us -- that characteristic spirit I
would come to know as ethos that shaped everything in the first place, the original design of us
that got lost in all our building. Everything else must go, and with it all the ways we’ve
constantly messed with the design until we lost sight of it altogether.
When all is gone, we stand on the brink of ourselves. We can’t go any further than ethos and still
be here. Below and beyond is nothing and death, non-being and non-life, the end and
annihilation.
The new will arise, but only after all is given up, moved into nothing until what is left is only
nothing.
Only…
Nothing.
All gone, given up. Everything we wanted, aspired for, believed in… all of it gone.
That’s impossible.
And that’s the point.
                                            The Void


Move out the Strobe, the Faith Follies, cause and effect, correlation, misuse of inspiration and
imagination, and now what’s left? Of me? Of my inner game?
Not much.
“Not much” is hard on the human soul. It leaves an immense emptiness -- not just a void, but
The Void.
The Void is a black hole gorging itself on your reality, annihilating it, leaving only a gaping
nothingness. Meaning and purpose vanish -- crushed, disintegrated. You feel their absence --
thick, dense, heavy beyond weight, dark beyond lightless.
There was one particular day in the giving up process… feeling the pull of The Void… maybe
one day I’ll have the will and the words to describe it. Many others have, for centuries. They can
speak for me now -- and for you, too, if you, too, have experienced The Void. You know if you
have. Or if you are.
For now, I just have one word to describe the experience: empty.
Empty like never before.
Empty like never before… it’s part of the deal when our inner game is torn all the way down.
Facing impossible takes us to The Void, then over the edge and in -- into the nothingness and
meaningless and purposelessness. If we don’t go there, there won’t be anything left of us to start
rebuilding after all the tearing down is over.
No that doesn’t make sense. Disappearing in order to remain and rebuild is one of the many
contradictions we’ll run into in our journey into ethos.
Meanwhile, we still have a game to play. As for me, I still needed to get on the machine and go
like a banshee. Never mind why. Never mind The Void. I needed to run, that’s all.
And that, as we will eventually find out, is the essence of ethos.
                                         Mercy Revisited


Facing The Void, I turned for help to an old friend -- a blog post you’ve already read: Mercy
(2014).
I adapted it from a chapter in Life Beyond Reason. Both the post and the chapter prompted some
of the most heartfelt reader responses I’ve ever gotten. No wonder: what a relief, to know you
can quit without shame. I felt that relief, too, when I wrote about mercy, but when I tried to apply
it to myself, I got stuck. Mercy didn’t apply, somehow, not to me.
Maybe mercy could help me now. I returned to the post.
          Maybe mercy reveals that we didn’t really want what we said we wanted after all, that we
          didn’t fully know our hearts’ desires when we started, and now the pressures and
          obstacles have revealed a deeper and more abiding truth about what we’re after.
No, that wasn’t it. I still wanted what I wanted.
          Once we know that, there’s no point staying with what would become pointless suffering.
But my suffering hadn’t been pointless: the wellbeing side of the ledger was proof of that. It
would be pointless only if measured against a specific outcome, but now I’d let that go.
          We’re not quitting, we’re accepting an invitation to a more deeply satisfying outcome.
To quit short of an outcome is a source of shame. Life athletes don’t do that. But take away the
outcome, you take away the shame. That in itself is a “deeply satisfying outcome.”
          We reach the place of mercy when we pass the point of no return.
I was there, no doubt. That was apparent in the way I’d been spinning my way into an ironic lack
of awareness and integrity about my inner game. Consider this, for example:
          People sometimes suggest experimental drugs they’ve heard about, or theorize about
          mechanical gizmos that might help.
          Maybe it looks like I’m crazy or stubborn, but something inside tells me that, if I adopted
          a new goal and modality, my soul would suffer. I’d give my power away; my heartfelt
          project would become someone else’s. Never mind that their terms might produce
          success as they define it, I’d hurt my soul if I went there. Sorry, I just can’t.
I wrote that in Mercy (2014), and then just a couple months later I was happy to report on the
benefits of my new WalkAide -- clearly one of those “mechanical gizmos” I’d just dissed. Talk
about irony.
Mercy forgave that, too. It offered a hand:
       Mercy... doesn’t call us back to what we so desperately wanted to leave behind, it invites
       us forward to what we really wanted in the first place. When we’re in the place of mercy,
       it’s not time to quit, it’s time to begin.
Yes, but how?
       Quitting and mercy feel different, and we know the difference.
Apparently I needed to follow that feeling of relief that had touched my readers and me.
Yes, but how?

I didn’t need to know how. It just happened. When I finally reached for it, mercy rushed in with
welcome relief. I surprised myself, reaching out to embrace it. Years of resisting vanished into
eagerness.
Relief came with reassurance. I wasn’t giving up on myself, just the opposite: I was giving up
on everything that wasn’t myself. I wasn’t even giving up on the impossible. I was only giving
up on…
       The Strobe.
       The Faith Follies.
       Control.
       And all the rest of what hindsight had been showing me.
Hindsight revealed the pattern of aspiring and adjusting, the thick and heavy accumulations, the
ineffective gamesmanship. Mercy made them irrelevant.
Hindsight ran the ledger. Mercy closed the books.
Hindsight brought chastising. Mercy brought peace.
Together, they brought an end, and a beginning.

There are two parts left to this story: how it ends, and how it gets there.

Writing this book was a lot of mystery and discovery, but one thing was clear early on: there
was a problem with the ending.
I could see one ending coming; I wanted another. I’d been in that position before. The whole
time I was writing Life Beyond Reason, I wanted it to end with the show going on. It didn’t.
Eventually, I wrote a chapter saying I was okay with that. Apparently there’s being okay, and
then there’s being okay. I hadn’t gotten to okay, hadn’t made my peace with impossible, and so I
ran into the same issue writing this book. I wanted it to end with me winning a marathon.
I wrote a final chapter where that happened. It made for wonderful cinema -- the motorcycle
escort, me breaking the tape, the jubilant supporters, the swelling music….
Sigh.
Not this time. MS wasn’t just MS anymore, it was impossible, and impossible meant an ending I
didn’t want. I needed to write it. Unless I did, I couldn’t move on.
My daughter Hilary writes historical novels. She finished one recently and plopped the
manuscript on the couch. I started in, from the beginning, but after a couple late nights, I jumped
ahead and read the ending. She’d been telling us the story for months, reading snippets. I knew
the characters, knew where they came from; I had to know where they went, what happened to
them.
Reading the ending didn’t ruin the story. I still needed to go back and find out how they got
there from here. Going back to find out was just as surprising as good stories are.
That experience encouraged me to jump ahead and give you this book’s ending right here, when
there’s still a lot of pages left to go. This used to be… well, at the end, where you’d expect it, but

        July 2015
        I have felt this moment coming for several weeks. Back in May, I thought I might do
        something like this the same weekend as the Colfax Marathon, but then no…
        There was no need to check the winning time. There is no winning time. There is no time,
        only this timeless moment.
       And this time, unlike that first morning in April that began this journey, I wasn’t afraid of

See you there?
                                        The Beginning


That’s where this story ends. Here’s where it began, as best I remember:
I wanted to learn to box. I would need a gym. I went out to the dilapidated structure we called
“the garage” but never put a car in, moved some things around to clear a space.
I stuffed a gunny sack with old newspapers, found some rope, and hung it from a rafter. That was
my heavy bag.
There were a couple rusty pulleys attached to adjacent wall studs, at shoulder height. My mom
bought bleach in gallon bottles; there were always empties around -- Clorox bottles were white,
Purex were robin’s egg blue. I got one of each, filled them with sand, tied rope to the handles and
looped them over the pulleys. I stood with my back to the wall, pulled them up with a punching
motion. That would work. I’d get strong and be able to pack a punch.
I pestered my parents for two pairs of boxing gloves -- Everlast, 6 ounces -- from Ben Franklin.
Also a light bag, which my Mom ordered from “Monkey Ward” -- as she called it.
I set up a ring in the basement, stringing ropes between support pillars, next to the oil-burning
octopus of a furnace. I swiped my mom’s kitchen timer to time the 3 minute rounds, with 1
minute of rest.
I checked out every book on boxing they had at the public library. There were three. I renewed
them all until I had them memorized.
One had black and white pictures of boxers, with directions for how to throw a jab, left hook,
right cross, uppercut, how to keep your guard up and counterpunch and clinch. It gave training
tips, like don’t lift weights, you’ll get musclebound, and one about something called roadwork,
which it never defined and I never could figure out what it meant.
The second book was called Knockout, about a kid who learns to box to defend himself from a
bully. He and the bully end up in Golden Gloves, and he wins their first fight when all he has is a
left jab. I worked harder on mine.
The third was an oversize history of heavyweight champions, with lots of old pictures -- black
and white, again. I learned them all, beginning with John L. Sullivan from the bare knuckle days,
when bouts went unlimited rounds that began when both fighters stepped up to toe the line, and
ended if one of them couldn’t, or his corner threw in the towel (literally). I learned the other
names, too, and their styles: Ezzard Charles and Jersey Joe Walcott, the boxers; Jack Dempsey
and Rocky Marciano, the brawlers.
Sports Illustrated ran an exposé about the Jack Dempsey-Jess Willard fight, saying Dempsey’s
trainer used Plaster of Paris to wrap his hands. He had cement in his gloves! I memorized that,
too.
I followed the annual Golden Gloves tournaments in Minneapolis Tribune. Why didn’t we have
Golden Glover in our town? That’s for the big cities, my dad said.
I drew pictures of boxers on my tablets. Kids would come around at recess to watch me draw.
I saw a preview of Elvis Presley in Kid Galahad at the movie theater. My parents wouldn’t let
me go.
We could get NBC and CBS -- they broadcast from towns close enough to pick up on our
rooftop antenna -- but ABC from the Twin Cities only if the wind was from the east. I watched
the Friday night fights with my Dad when the wind was right. One night it was Archie Moore, in
the fight where his trunks kept sagging down and the ref would occasionally step up behind him
and pull them up.
I listened to the heavyweight championship fights on the radio -- the big console radio in the
living room at first, then a transistor I could take to my room. They started after 9:00 -- I had to
get permission to stay up. That was the era of Floyd Patterson and Sonny Liston, and Cassius
Clay, before he was Muhammed Ali.
My friends came over to box in the basement. An uppercut sent one of them flying backward
into the laundry baskets, breaking all the ropes on the way through. Guess you needed stronger
ones for a boxing ring.
Now and then I’d boxed with the sheriff’s son over at the jail. His family lived on one side of the
building, the jail was in the other. We boxed in the open space above the landing on the second
floor. Sometimes if they had a prisoner he might watch and cheer. We took our breaks sitting on
a bunks in an open cell.
At night I shadow-boxed in my bedroom, in front of the mirror over my dresser.
I was seven years old at the time.
At least, when I started. About the time I turned eleven, my Dad told me I didn’t want to be a
boxer, I’d get cauliflower ears, and besides when I went to junior high next year some big kid
would think I had a chip on my shoulder and knock it off. That didn’t sound good, so I quit.
Thus began my life athlete practice. Athletics, things I wanted to learn, have, and do, art, music,
building things, hobbies and crafts, my jobs, career, vocations and avocations… it was always
total immersion, cross-disciplinary, hands-on learning. It was my approach to life, what I did.
The approach stayed with me through school, college, postgraduate and professional education,
adult life. The first time I got asked to join someone’s board of directors as a young adult, I
asked why. “Because whatever you do, you always throw yourself into it.” was the reply.
I do interactive workshops on finding and pursuing your life passion. The first exercise is “What
do you already do?” I explain it this way:
       You find important clues about your Passion just by looking at how you arrange your life
       already. There are some things you do that go beyond the categories of work vs. leisure,
        personal vs. family or business, etc. You do them just because you want to, because you
        like doing them or you’re good at them (probably both).
        Think about how you already spend yourself. What do you like to think about, read
        about, talk about, learn about? What headlines do you click on? What do your activities
        and projects at work or away from it gravitate toward? What role do you usually play at
        work, with family, in social settings? What are your hobbies and favorite pastimes? What
        shows do you watch, what magazines and articles do you read? What do you like to talk
        about? You get the idea. Write about it.
The whole workshop is an elaboration of that exercise. Dig deep enough, and you find an entire
lifetime -- not just the things you do and have done, but how you go about doing them. When we
live with passion, we reach for the same interior framework. The subject matter changes, but the
way we go about it doesn’t.
Spend some time reflecting, and you’ll see your internal framework for how you go about life. I
did that, writing this book, and hindsight picked out episode after episode of my inner game at
work. It was my signature.
Once a life athlete, always a life athlete.
Ethos then wrapped my life athlete practice in rich, deep meaning.
No longer The Void, but life right on the edge of it -- where ethos abides.

I run, I write.
Gotta love the simplicity.

Yes.

The thing that gets me about this concept of ethos is how dang simple it is.
Sometimes it seems like life should be hard, because it’s big, confusing, uncertain, and I’m
pretty sure everyone is just making it up as they go along. But then there’s this: you do what you

spend most of your time doing it or not, it will be there and you will be doing it.
That’s all?
That’s grace.
I’ve always been dancing. Sometimes I was doing it professionally. Sometimes I didn’t think I
was dancing. Sometimes I was busy trying to do something else. Sometimes it was all I lived for.
It doesn’t matter, because it is what I will do, what I always have done, and what I will always
continue to do.
Sometimes I make money doing it, and sometimes I don’t, and quite frankly, either way doesn’t

When I dance, I am.
It’s that simple, and that’s the beauty of it. That’s the freedom of it.
The thing is, no one can take it away from me. It’s mine, it is me, it is the marrow in my bones.
To take it away you’d have to take away my soul, and I’m pretty sure no one can do that.
You could take away my legs. I’d find a way to continue. You could put me in a room by myself
with no music. I’d dance to pass the time. You could take away my hearing; no problem. Prevent
me from ever doing a performance (good luck!), keep me from being a professional, whatever
you like, it doesn’t matter.
This idea of ethos gives me the freedom to fail.

Grace buckles my knees. I think it will buckle yours too.
When you think of ethos in these terms, it’s hard not to see something spiritual in it. I am not

Ethos is simple, but my god is it fulfilling. And literally all you have to do to experience the

habits, only your mindfulness. You were always doing what you will do. You always have. You
always will. There were no woods, there were no monsters. You are already everything you have
ever needed and ever will need.
That’s it?
That’s grace.

Alan Lock always knew he’d be career military. A genetic eye condition made him legally blind

All his life, people had been telling swimmer Maarten van der Weijden he was destined for

cancer with a 30% survival rate.
What happened next? Alan became the first legally blind person to row a boat across the Atlantic
Ocean. After three years, Maarten’s cancer was gone. Five years later, he won Olympic gold.
Neither of them was a positive thinker. In fact, just the opposite. “No matter what people say,
there were no positives in losing my sight,” Alan told the authors. His secret to success? “I
always expect the worst,” he said, adding “I knew I was doomed.”

a feeling they could do something about it -- a feeling of personal power that said “It may be
over for me, but I still think I can do something about it.”
Surprisingly, that attitude can co-exist with a lack of positive thinking -- a fact that apparently
drives some psychologists crazy. Supersurvivors cites numerous psychological articles
describing this attitude as “delusional” and based on “illusions of control.” One psychologist said

exaggerated sense of personal control, and overly optimistic expectations about the future.”
Okay then.

now I’m going to figure out what I can do.” When he decided to row across an ocean, “People

and physically. I was shooting for a watershed moment.”
He got his watershed moment alright. Quite literally.
We would call Alan and Maarten realists, pessimists even. There was no bravado or can-do
spirit, no hope for a miracle. They knew their odds were poor. They expected adversity and got
it. Yet they did what they did anyway, for no one but themselves.

my physical training, diet, and all the rest, and somehow I think it matters.
Call me delusional, I guess.

                                       Koan Language


In time, I discovered that this new language had a name: it was koan language.
I don’t remember why, but I Googled “koan.” Wikipedia says a koan is “a story, dialogue,
question, or statement which is used in Zen practice to provoke the ‘great doubt’ and test a
student's progress in Zen practice.”
In other words, koans make us go “Huh?!?”
I’d been writing koan language. What I was writing was provoking the Great Doubt.
Koans, parables, riddles, myths, prophecy, poetry… they all affect us that way. They don’t use
words the way we normally do. They strip away our presumption that sensible, logical, and
orderly language is how we know things. Koans don’t want us to know in that way. Instead, they
want us to experience what’s being said, and make sense of that experience for ourselves. That’s
what the Great Doubt is for.
The Great Doubt doesn’t lead to great reasoning, but to great discovery. The oracle doesn’t speak
in riddles just to be contrary, just to send us on annoying scavenger hunts for the perverse fun of
it, it gives us treasure of the most personal kind: an invitation to find the meaning of its riddles
within ourselves and for ourselves.
                                        Ethos Language


Koan language is perfect for ethos. Ethos is not easily known; therefore its language does not
easily give up its secrets.
Ethos shelters itself in obscurity and elusiveness. It defies language, taunts, beckons, appears as a
shimmer then vanishes, gives us just enough of a glimpse so we think we’re getting it, then darts
off again. Ethos is slippery -- words and their meanings slide off it.
There’s wildness to it. We approach, and ethos seems curious and drawn to us but not sure, so
we need to be real still and shut our eyes and resist opening them when our neck prickles and we
know it’s there, right there, so close we could snatch it, so we squeeze our eyes shut and sit on
our hands because if we give in to the temptation to look or to try to grab it, it will be gone, and
we’ll have to start all over again.
Dealing with ethos language, we run smack into the Great Doubt, and we get annoyed and
irritated, want the words to quit being spoiled and contrary and just come out with it and tell us
what they mean. They won’t. We have to find their meaning for ourselves; there is no riddle
answer key.
Such is the gift of ethos language: words conveying mysteries, beckoning their own discovery,
giving up meaning not in understanding but in experiencing. To experience ethos, we must
embody it. To embody ethos, we must also embody its language. And to embody its language,
we must experience it.

                           We Are The Truth About Ourselves

Language does not embody the truth of itself, doesn’t make truth its own, bring truth into itself,
into its own reality. Language only directs us toward truth existing apart from itself, only thinly
captured in words.
So it is with language. It is different with us. We are the truth about ourselves. We are moment
by moment true. The truth of us is in and about and around us. We live within that truth, embody
it, experience it, cannot live outside and apart from it, cannot be separate from ourselves, cannot
be anything less or more than ourselves.
Ethos knows no separation of us from ourselves, knows only the truth of our embodiment of
itself, of our being imprinted and permeated with ethos in our wholeness and in the individual
cells and systems of us.
To know the truth of ethos, we must know the truth of ourselves.

Immanence means that the reality of us -- the mass, energy, consciousness, and ethos -- comes

Immanence is neither limiting nor fatalistic. It compresses and compacts us until under its
pressure we explode into a personal universe of ever-expanding and opening possibilities.

Therefore it is not a choice.

                                    Beginning With Mass


We have an idea, a passion awakened, a dream wanting to come true, We will change things,
make things. We will be that, live that, have that, know that. That’s us, that’s who we are, want
to be.
Now if only we can dare to believe that the world wants the same thing. Surely it will: this is us,
who we are, why we are here, our purpose, calling, passion. Surely the world must want this.

design, reshape. We do what we know, and mass is what we know. If there is something that
wants doing, it must want doing in mass. Otherwise how will we know? We know mass. Mass is
there, undeniable. We can see it, feel it, measure it. In the mass of our lives is where we will find
the evidence of us -- know what we have done, and become.
We start trying to move mass. We fixate on the mass we’re trying to move, imagine the energy it
would take to move it. We throw things at it, throw ourselves at it. We climb it, tear it down,
reassemble it. We have done this before. It didn’t move then either.
Meanwhile, the mass of our lives already in place waited for us to return to it, and so we did,
unmoved, unchanged.
We missed something, with all this focus on the mass of us: we missed the opportunity to
become.
Mass formed from the life we have decided to leave behind won’t move, won’t reshape itself,
won’t be and become. That thing we must do and have and be… that thing has a name… it is our
name, all that signifies the incarnate expression of us. That’s what we missed.
To be and do and have, we must be and become, must incarnate, must become flesh, energy and
mass joining together into something we can step back from and point at and tell others to see
and experience it with us.
To do this, we need the mystery, the invisible life, the dark energy shaping the dark mass of us.
We know little of these. We cannot see them. We know visible; we know less, much less, maybe
nothing, about invisible, or how to enter it.
We must learn.

what is not, make idea visible and substantial. This is what we must know, must experience.

We know some aspects of our incarnation better than others, and we know more about some
things at some times than at other times. Now it’s body we know about, then it’s soul. Now it’s
this, then it’s that -- fragments of knowledge attached to fragments of us. Only we’re not
fragments, we’re the whole thing, all that mass and energy constantly changing its own form and
shape, one becoming the other and back again, so that at any moment it’s hard to tell which is
which.
Fragmented knowing works well enough to get by until it doesn’t, until something happens in or
to us that makes it not okay anymore. We get to that point, and yes we need awareness and
consciousness and mindfulness and the other names we give to that kind of knowing, but even all

                              The Language of Ethos (Redux)


The language of mass has form and substance. We can observe it, engineer it, make it precise,
certain, stable. The language of mass merges truth into belief, stills and solidifies it so we can see
it, hold it, rely on it to speak of what exists apart from us, what doesn’t need our effort to
substantiate itself. The language of mass tells us a chair is a chair, so we know we can sit on it.
We use the language of mass when we want to invest words with great truth -- words like God,
divine, mystery, faith, soul, spirit. These words are the most massive of our language -- footings
driven to bedrock, anchoring our allegiance to the truth we want them to express, and our
standing relative to it.
Truth thus grounded satisfies us. We feel better thinking we rightly understand our world and
who we are in it, that we know false and folly clearly enough to distance ourselves from them
and, thus purged, know our boundaries and limitations. We can relax now, sheltered in the
solidness of what we believe, what our belief requires of us, and where we stand if we do or
don’t comply with it.
The language of mass is the language of time and sequence and predictability, of reasonableness
and control. It knows what is and is not, what can and cannot be. It makes us staunch, stable,
secure, still, steady, sure. It confirms for us the sacred we must preserve, guard, maintain.
It is not so with the language of energy.
The language of energy knows not the steadiness of truth but the pulsing of it, the appearing and
disappearing, shaping and becoming of it. Energy words bounce and dance with truth, bend and
shift with it, seek not to embody it but to reflect it, not to capture it but represent it. They fly by
and we catch a glimpse of what we thought was truth, but they are gone before we’re sure.
The language of energy is madness and surprise. It soars, dips, swoops near and spins away,
darts here then there, shimmers in many places at once, appears, vanishes, plays games with us,
teases, mocks, entices, refuses. It enters our vision and withdraws from it, prefers the shadows
just shy of revelation.
The language of energy is invitation and opening, adventure and exploring, growth and
becoming. It is Loki, the fool, the jester, the irreverent, the fringe, the risky, the odd and
inadvisable. It is the confounding and the conundrum, the puzzle and the riddle, the game and the
chance. The language of energy dismantles, disarms, and disorients us, leaving us aware only
that we are moved, acted upon strangely and unpredictably.
The language of energy acts on mass, shudders it, moves it, splinters it and shapes it back
together, alters, changes, transmutes and transforms it. Once energy has done with mass, we are
left with mass again, and we forgive energy because we prefer it that way. Our need and
preference for mass yearns into the center of all that shifting, all that beckoning and pushing
away, seeking a place to settle, to calm down the agitation and expanding, the dizzying and
disorienting.
The language of mass anchors belief to knowledge and observation. It roots and defines our
perception.
The language of energy spins our beliefs and our beliefs about our beliefs, our practices and
perceptions, our experience and embracing and engagement.
The language of ethos is neither, and both. It is the clash and merging of the two languages,
where they meet and separate, join and divide.
We reach for the language of ethos when our events and times squeeze the energy out of the
mass of our lives, free the truth we’ve so carefully captured and preserved and en-massed, so that
it escapes and becomes disembodied, haunts and howls, shrieks and sings in noises and tones we
cannot hear, only feel their impression passing over us and gone.
Sometimes this is our choice, the result of our decision to forsake the comfort of mass and
embrace the risk of energy. More often it happens when truth and mass have been separated
despite our effort to hold them together, when the energy cost of sustaining the mass in the
absence of the truth it once carried is too high, when the truth that once was fails us, separates
from ourselves and our world, leaves us at risk of losing the truth of ourselves and our lives
altogether.
Those are the times when nothing but the fullest expression of ourselves in the moment will
suffice. Only the truth not captured in our words and beliefs but the truth of our experience will
do -- the truth of our threatened existence, out at the edge of eternity, the edge of the expanding
universe of our lives, the life flowing through us and out to the edges of our origin, where we are
incarnated into existence, not as we once were but as we are now, must be, need to be.
Going there, our words shift and vibrate, truth spilling out of them, resisting our efforts at mass
and knowledge and control, leaping and bounding away from the reach of the energy we have
known as meaning and truth. The moment separates from time, enters eternity, comes back
indescribably and unknowingly, made new as we cannot comprehend, and in that moment in
time and beyond time we are re-created, re-incarnated, made new and needing to re-enter life of
mass and energy, desperately wishing we had their languages readily at hand.
That’s when we need ethos, and the language of it, the language of end and beginning, of over
and again, never and newness. The language of ethos is truth splintering into shards of meaning
hopelessly broken, the raw material of us struggling to make sense of our lives and survive,
desperate to re-form, to become whole as we weren’t before but as we have always and never
been.
Such is the language of ethos: defying words to hold it, needing us to embody it.

The time for us is now because now is the only time there is.
Time is not an option, a commodity, a mass, a packaged possibility that we can handle,
manipulate, treat as something that might be. There is no ribbon of time stretching from past to
future, there is only this moment, this inhalation. Time is only here, now, because we are only
here, now. Time and self, faith and action… these are now or they are not.
Here’s the moment, right now -- how big is it?
Here’s eternity -- how big is that?
And which is bigger, time or no time? This moment or eternity?
They’re the same size, because time and eternity are both as big as us asking questions about
them in this moment.
                                    The Sequence Fallacy


Cause and effect and correlation activate a belief that there’s a connecting cable called “time”
that comes from what went before, runs through this moment, and continues on to what comes
after; that the cable is linear; that it runs unbroken and evenly paced from our beginning to our
end like some kind of cosmic one-speed conveyor belt, so that if we put things on it today,
they’ll be moving along into our lives tomorrow.
If we can just get to the controls of that conveyor belt, we can make things happen for ourselves.
We know better. We know there is no one-speed conveyor belt of time, but that time has variable
speeds, that just because clocks go tic-toc (or used to) doesn’t mean time does. Ask any kid
eyeing an unopened gift, athlete waiting for an event, lover holding vigil, artist immersed in
creation… they know that the flow of time changes speeds, lasts an eternity, breaks in an instant.
To build our lives with a different awareness other than what we know about time is to embrace
the sequence fallacy.
We create myths and rules to support the sequence fallacy, to make time reliable and predictable.
It’s neither. Time won’t always tell; it may be the last to know. But we persist: we say time is
(or isn’t) on our side, that our timing was right (or wrong), and we nod as if we know what we’re
talking about, sustaining the deception.
The fallacy deceives us all the way into a constant state of incompleteness, of waiting for things
to get here, accompanied with fears and judgments about whether they will or won’t. We’re
constantly uncertain, on edge.
What if, when we say our timing was perfect, we don’t mean the conveyor got us somewhere at
just the right moment, but instead we mean that things lined up for us in space and time in ways
that can’t be measured by reference to either?
What if:
       Effect precedes cause?
       After precedes before?
       The answer precedes the question?
       The question is asked because the answer exits?
       The desire exists because the satisfaction is waiting for it?
       The solution waits for the problem to be posed?
Considering these possibilities, we slip beyond the sequence fallacy and enter the here and now.
And where else could we be?
                                     FAITH AND HOPE


                                        And Now, Faith


Faith can only be now because we can only act now. When now is over, so is faith. It can rest; it
has done its work.
Faith must be now because it must be incarnated, and incarnation is only and always now. The
time for the object of our faith to be expressed, realized, embodied, incarnated is now. There is
no other possibility.
Faith that is not now cannot be. Faith that is detached from incarnation is no longer faith.
Faith is this time or no time. Now or non-time. Now or never. No time like the present. There
can’t be anything else. There never was anything else, there never will be. There is no other
moment for faith. It has no other residence, no other time. This is it. Anything faith does, it does
here, now.
Life was not then, without us. Life does not go on next, without us. Life needs us to go on. If we
had never been, there would not have been life. Not for us, not our lives. Life for us is not what
we were or might be, it is only when we are present with life manifesting itself in us.
Every time faith creates the moment, we save ourselves from not being. And the world, too. Us
in the world, existing together, not existing if we don’t exist together, both us and our world
existing together, by faith.
                                        Hope and Faith


In ethos practice, hope is the state of mind created by our urge to do something about it. Only
that. And that is enough, and all there is of hope.
Hope is the seed planted, and faith is the action that generates the surprise -- the unpredictable
outcome of hope.
       Hope is the energy of creation stored in the seed, waiting to be released by our faith.
Hope needs no object. We don’t hope in -- not in an outcome apart from us we want to bring into
our reality -- we simply hope. We enter the state of mind of hope, and we act from there.
       Hope unadorned with outcome is complete; it needs no outcome to make it whole.
As soon as we say we hope in this, the possibility that it won’t be this also comes into existence.
This and not-this struggle to exist together, and our false hope is baffled and blocked.
But let go of outcome, and hope opens to life, ushers eternity into the moment, awakens us to the
boundless possibilities of creation. From there, our faith is ready to create our incarnation afresh.
       Faith is what we practice, and what we practice we embody.
                          Why All the Adjustments Don’t Work


We keep trying to get faith right. We think our adjustments and fine-tuning will get us closer to
an ideal -- that perfect frequency we know is being broadcast from out there somewhere that, if
we can dial into it, it will bring us what we could be, how we could live.
We spin the dials in vain.
What we think are those minute twists honing in on the new frequency of the ideal us are each a
new creation -- us once more inventing a new self, trying it out, seeing how it is for the energy of
self to vibrate at a new frequency.
Each time we are a new creation.
Creation is inexhaustible. There is no end to the adjustments because there is no end to creation,
our ability to create, to keep creating ourselves.
We don’t arrive where we intended because there was no place to arrive to -- only a projection of
what we thought we were creating, only an exercise of our imagination. Only the way we
thought the new creation would go.
We thought we could control the creation of ourselves and our lives with the tweak of the faith
dial. Creation cannot be controlled. We don’t stand outside of it, we move within it --
experiencing, participating, acting and reacting, along with a myriad flow of other energies we
can’t possibly catalogue or control.
Creation is its own energy. We set it in motion with our decisions and actions. We shape it with
our aspirations, intentions, and goals. Our beliefs turn the flow on or off, lessen or increase it.
We do all of that. We are powerful in our ability to create. But control where our creations take
us, how they play out? No, not that.
There is no final place where our creations take us. There is no outcome out there somewhere,
waiting for us to get there. There is only us, experiencing our own creation of ourselves and our
lives, and when we get there it won’t be as imagined. What will be there is us, having done the
creating to get ourselves there, and creating still in the new moment.
This is the life of faith. There is no getting it right, and no getting anywhere or getting anything.
Only creating life, living by faith, faithful in our creating.
We are the world, and ourselves in it, every time we incarnate.
The world. Ourselves. Our creation. All we are. All there is.
                    Some Other Things Ethos Practice Is And Isn’t


                                           Isolating? No.

Ethos practice isn’t about the lone wolf, the stoic holdout.
Or separation. Or loneliness. Or isolation. Or anything like that.
Just the opposite. It’s about full connection. Including connection with the divine, however we
conceive of it.
That’s what immanence is about. That’s what coming fully-loaded means.
                                            Heroic? No.
We’re not gallant, or trying to save anyone or anything.
We’re just living, from the inside out.
If we’re saving anything, it’s ourselves and our own lives. That’s enough. It will spread from
here.
Not that ethos doesn’t take courage. It does. Lots. But not because we’re taking on a foe; only
because living fully always takes courage. Always.
                                          Deprivation? No.
Hardly.
Ethos practice is opening the sluice gate all the way.
It’s life to the max, because it’s ourselves to the max.
And immanence is always to the max. Always. That’s what it means.
                                          Serendipity? Yes.
And wonder, synchronicity, luck, coincidence, and all those other fun ways of experiencing life.
And intuition, instinct, hunches, insight, clairvoyance, mystery, and all those other fun ways of
knowing what we need to know when we need to know it.
And being in the right place at the right time and having things work out, and all those other fun
ways of living.
All those fun things are all there -- incarnated and immanent; in us and in our lives.
Ethos opens us to them.
                                   Trust? Yes. Definitely Trust.
When we’ve tried everything else.
When the research is conclusive, but doesn’t satisfy.
When cause and effect has been established, but the stuff we’re doing doesn’t seem to be causing
or affecting.
When correlation does not open the Mystery.
When we’ve tried everything else except this one last thing: to trust ourselves.
We have practiced the no’s and denied and negated the yes’s, favoring the idealized and
judgmental out there, the separate and disembodied.
We can learn again. We can return to ourselves, embody and inhabit ourselves, and in so doing
learn to trust the trustworthy: the life flowing through us, ourselves incarnate, the immanence of
our world, the ethos of us.
                                        And There’s More.
Ethos practice is not about willpower or hardening. Or about striving and wanting. Or about law
and judgment. Or dying for the cause.
Or motivation and inspiration -- although it’s both, but not like we usually think.
Or drive and determination -- although it’s both of those, too, but not like we usually think.
It’s not about nihilism or hedonism, fatalism or materialism, or other kinds of isms like that. Or
any kind of ism, for that matter.
It is about grace and freedom, struggle and achievement, and about all the opposites, paradoxes,
contradictions, and conundrums you can think of, all coming peacefully together.
And more.
Ethos practice can’t be any of those things, because ethos isn’t any of those things.

                                         The Haunting


Impossible needs a beginning without something coming before it. Most beginnings aren’t that
way: they come at the end of other beginnings; they’re salvaged, recycled, repurposed as one era
of our lives morphs into another. Not so for impossible. Impossible requires a fresh beginning,
not a recycled one. That’s why impossible is… well, impossible: there’s no connection to what
went before.
But things don’t just come out of nothing, do they? It seems there needs to be at least an idea
ahead of time about what the new beginning will be. Kind of like this:
                   In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.
        The earth was without form and void, and darkness was over the face of the deep.
                 And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters.
                            Genesis 1:1-2, English Standard Version
Okay, the earth was formless and void, but at least there was darkness, and the face of the deep,
and the Spirit of God hovering. That’s not nothing; it’s mysterious, yes, but it’s something to
work with. The Big Bang hadn’t happened yet, but everything was staged, set up, ready to go --
the entire universe compressed into an immeasurably compact form, poised, ready to explode,
waiting for the signal…
                               And God said, “Let there be light,”
KA-BOOM!!
You hear different versions -- contemporary and ancient -- of a similar creation myth about our
personal beginnings. The gist is that there was more going on than the gleam in our father’s eye,
there was a cosmic staging area where we waited to make an entrance. The idea of us preceded
our entrance; there was a cosmic vision for who we might become, for our calling and mission in
life. We came endowed with a destiny and purpose.
The myth goes on to say we lose track of our transcendent origins. “Real life” becomes a slow
forgetting that accounts for a lot of our bumbling around, making mistakes, getting off track. If
we kept better touch with our ultimate origin, we would do a better job of getting through life.
The 19th Century poet William Wordsworth captured all of this in his “Intimations of
Immortality”:
                             Our birth is but a sleep and a forgetting
                            The Soul that rises with us, our life’s Star,
                                 Hath had elsewhere its setting
                                      And cometh from afar
                                   Not in entire forgetfulness,
                                    And not in utter nakedness,
                             But trailing clouds of glory do we come
                                   From God, who is our home:
                               Heaven lies about us in our infancy!
                            Shades of the prison-house begin to close
                                      Upon the growing Boy,
                           But he beholds the light, and whence it flows,
                                        He sees it in his joy;
                            The Youth, who daily farther from the east
                                Must travel, still is Nature’s priest,
                                    And by the vision splendid
                                      Is on his way attended;
                             At length the Man perceives it die away;
                              And fade into the light of common day.
Sigh.
We began so well. God was our true source, we came with the aura of Heaven upon us, trailing
clouds of glory… only to have it all fade away in mundanity.
Sigh again.
The pattern is familiar: an inspired beginning, a strong start, a slow, tiresome deflation. I’ve
done it often. You?
Endings also seem to need something coming after them. We want over to not be over. Life just
doesn’t seem to fit into finite and temporal, it needs infinite and eternal waiting on the other side.
I remember as a kid, lying in bed trying to imagine an infinite universe. (Honest!) I visualized
the star-studded blackness of deep space whizzing by. (Star Wars gave that a name:
hyperspace.) As soon as I tried to stop the trip I’d immediately think something had to be on the
other side, just past the end. Either that, or the universe just kept going on and on forever.
(Which now we think it does.) Then I’d start it up again, unsure.
Rewind, repeat.
Eventually I gave up. I couldn’t just lie there imagining all night; I needed to go to sleep like a
good boy, it was a school night.
And thus the shades of the prison-house closed upon the growing boy.
A more grownup version is the idea of an afterlife. Our religious doctrines leave us with the
sobering notion that our temporal lives might have eternal consequences.
                                         To die, to sleep,
                       To sleep, perchance to Dream; Aye, there's the rub,
                       For in that sleep of death, what dreams may come,
                           When we have shuffled off this mortal coil,
                                       Must give us pause.
If our souls continue on to another stage, then we can’t do just any old thing in this life, we
might pay for it later. The afterlife is out there waiting for us like the Ultimate Badass Santa
Claus: it knows when we’ve been naughty or nice, and we better watch out.
Thus we have beforelife and afterlife, and in between we’re haunted by them both.
The Haunting… That wasn’t my term. Someone else used it in something I read as I was writing.
I liked it. I am borrowing it.
We’re fascinated with The Haunting. We perceive the source of it as Other -- not us, not here,
not on our side of a curtain circumscribing our world. Usually the curtain is opaque and
impermeable, but not always: there are moments when we catch a hint of something happening
over there. We hear music and voices speaking in foreign tongues, we see forms and shadows
that are alive but not mortal.
The Other is also where the gods are, and what goes on for them affects what goes on for us. Or
so it seems.
We conceptualize the Other using language and practices according to our bent: religious,
spiritual, psychological, neurological, biological, philosophical, sociological, technological, and
all the other ologies, each with its own vocabulary, explanations, recommendations, and
demands.
Our beliefs about the Other ignite our passions, fire our imaginations, sear our consciences. We
fill libraries and graveyards with them. None of that deters The Haunting: our ologies are
convincing in the light of day, but in the nighttime of our souls they rarely rid us of our phantom
lives.
Sometimes The Haunting draws us into those strangely affirming and comforting times of
melancholy when we feel the longing and mystery and emptiness, and know we’re not alone.
These feelings are deep and visceral, unsettling and disturbing, and oddly comforting. We have
them, we move on. Or if we’re Wordsworth or Shakespeare, we write poetry about them.
Other times, we find ourselves fresh out of explanations and stuck with The Haunting’s
inexplicability and inescapability. We get banged up by life, or someone we love does, and
suddenly we’re the atheist in the foxhole, praying and contemplating with a whole new urgency
our beginnings, endings, and in-betweens. But then there’s school the next day, so we settle on
some kind of belief system that mostly keeps us feeling unhaunted most of the time.
We’re all free to contemplate The Haunting all we like. It’s part of life, part of our
consciousness. It might be a human necessity. Thinking about The Haunting is just something we
do. Great -- we can give whatever energy to it we like.
But just know that the inner game of ethos doesn’t do that. It doesn’t take a position on The
Haunting or the Other, and won’t be lured into debate about them. There is no ology of The
Haunting in ethos practice. It doesn’t need one, for this reason:
                To practice ethos is to experience The Haunting, not to explain it.
We experience The Haunting when we experience incarnation and immanence. That’s the whole
story of us, in ethos.
Got that?
Thank you for getting that.
                                    We Create Cosmologies


I am here. You are there. Other people here, and there and there. God here, angels there. The
mysterious and transcendent, the known and unknown, seen and unseen, near and far, imminent
and remote… everything has its place, and its own rules that govern its place.
Its place in a model we’ve built.
And because it’s a model and because we’ve built it, it’s already distant and removed from us.
We’re creating ghosts, our own haunting, God in our own image, the projection of our beliefs.
We can’t help our idolatry, we have learned so well this creating God in our image in the name
of faith, in the service of the Strobe.
Ethos is not a cosmology. It is not distant. Ethos is where we begin, and continue. It is the
inescapable us, the creation of ourselves and our lives, the ever-expanding universe of ourselves,
shaping and organizing chaos into our known reality, the cosmos of ourselves, the life surging
through us.
                                The Mystery Is In the Moment


A lot of life happens where we can’t see, beyond the reach of perception and articulation. It’s
not sensory, but we feel it. We know it’s there. But usually the best we can do is notice what it
leaves behind after it’s acted, made its presence known by shaping some reality in our lives.
And then we go running to the point where it occurred, where it happened to us, where it messed
with us or something in our lives, and try to figure out how to make it happen again, make it not
mysterious anymore.
We make the Other and its alternate reality our focus, so that our lives become an outer game
instead of an inner game. We make the Other our Strobe. We practice faith by reference to it. We
look to the gods who live there to tell us what’s possible and what isn’t, what’s normal and
what’s miraculous, and whether we’re fulfilling our destiny, purpose, calling, mission, being and
doing the best we can be. We look to it as the source of the ideal of ourselves and the ideal lives
we might and could and should be living. And we use it to console ourselves that our suffering
will be recompensed when we find ourselves on the other side of the curtain.
The Other begins as unpredictable, with a life of its own, but in time, and through our ologies,
we become convinced that we can live with it as an active agent in our lives. We create
structures -- rituals, practices, sacraments, institutions -- in our lives to invite it, entice it, bring
its presence more often and dependably, make it less chancy and situational. Some of these we
form into mass -- altars and buildings and temples -- places we build and point to and say that’s
where the Other is, or at least where it once was, and maybe it will visit again.
And we call that “faith.”
What if none of that?
What if the Other is on this side of the curtain? Still Other, still not entirely us, still unpredictable
and exasperating, still hard to conceive and talk about, but still also on this side, part of us, part
of what it means to be ourselves, in our minds and bodies, expressing and experiencing and
embodying the Other in the cells and systems of us, in our thoughts and feelings, reactions and
initiatives, and all the other tools we have to somehow get ourselves up onto the crest of the
wave of life flowing through us -- life that we didn’t cause or create -- and then ride it -- the
whole wave, the full wave, all the way to its breaking.
What if all that?
What if the Other is in the moment?
The Other is in the moment. Like everything else, it has to be: the moment is the only place we
can experience anything, and that goes for the Other, too.
No, it’s not entirely us. Yes, there’s a sense of the Other about it. But we’re okay with that. It
gets to be the Other. We don’t need to own or explain it, teach or learn it.
But one thing will be different, one really big thing: it won’t be slipping in and out of the curtain
anymore. It will be here with us all the time. We won’t need to invoke it and hope it hears us,
hope it’s not busy with somebody or something else, that it’s in a mood to be appeased and to
listen… none of that anymore.
Instead, we’ll live in the presence of the invisible, comfortable with The Haunting. We’ll notice
it, cock an ear in its direction, sense it making our lives rich and full and complete and whole like
we haven’t experienced them before.
There won’t be the curtain and the separation. The Other will take up residence in us.
Us… that’s what it will be: it will be us, the way we live, who we are -- in the Other, the
invisible and multidimensional fabric of this great big thing called Life, not perceiving of the
Other as apart from us but fully abiding in it, in its energy, its meaning, its substance and the
endless impersonal and personal web of it.
                                                God


Resign to it -- the concept of God. Or challenge it. Love it. Hate it. Argue about it. Bow to it.
Reach for it when we’re in trouble and forget about it as soon as we’re not.
And a million other responses.
Or how about this: access it, work with it, create with it. Enjoy, value, expect it, wonder about it.
But mostly embody it, act from it, be guided by it, create within it -- let it give us ideas, guide us,
shape us and what we do.
That’s what happens when we create. We get things. Things come to us. Ideas. Suggestions.
Insights. They pop in, come from we don’t know where. Creating becomes not a solo flight, but
a conversation, a collaboration.
Know that it’s there, and not just there but there for you. In fact, it might BE you.
Really.
But that’s the beauty of it. We don’t have to know. It’s a gift, but more than that it’s the giving,
there when we need it. Always there, never failing to be there -- always one more edge, one more
horizon, one more idea, one more thought.
There’s this sense that “oh that came from somewhere else.” Maybe it did, maybe not. It works
whether we know or not.
Get all the way to the center of us, and it’s always there, never goes away, never turns off, the
connection never breaks, the signal is never lost. The Other is always there -- in us, with us, for
us, the center of us. We’re never lost, disconnected, left without the guidance and everything else
we need to do what we do: create ourselves and be created, moment by moment by moment.
The heartbeat is given. We are given. God is given -- even if not known, articulated, or named,
but simply experienced. That’s good enough.
And then the gratitude: life is this way, really.
                                          Finding God


Or the Divine. Or the Other, the Transcendent, the Higher Power, name it what you like.
To find it, we need look no further than ourselves -- the incarnation of us. We’re the whole
package, God included.
Dress it up with names, create religions to hold it, with all their vocabulary and doctrine and
ritual and everything else, but ultimately there’s no other place to experience it than here, in this
life, in and through ourselves.
If we’re experiencing it, it can’t be out there, it has to be here, where we can embrace, embody,
and experience it. We can’t experience anything except within ourselves. Whatever transcendent
connection and experience we have has to come through us, be experienced and realized in us, in
our bodies, ourselves, our present reality. It can’t come any other way. God has to be incarnate in
us, otherwise we can’t experience it.
If our aspirations seem to us to be removed, distant, apart, separate… in God, in another
transcendent realm… that cannot be. They have to be in us, touching us, making themselves
known in us. We cannot experience them any other way. There is no experience of God -- and
therefore no God -- except the one revealed and known to us, inside us, in the incarnation of us.
                               There is No Sadder But Wiser


No too soon old, too late wise.
And all those other words we fling around -- easily, because there’s no weight to them.
We were not better then. We will not be better then.
We can’t be better than we are now.
We can only be what we are experiencing in each moment -- including this moment.
There is nothing lacking or missing or failing about us. Not now.
We owe nothing to the moment that we’re not already giving it.
We are simply here.
Where mercy isn’t a decision, it’s a state of grace.
                                                    Grace


Grace is the law breaker. It is where we break the law we have become unto ourselves. And not
just break it, but be as free as if it never was.
Grace is the bold dethroning of the gods we have created to rule over our lives.
Grace is living without the deception of the strobe and the bonds of how we have practiced faith.
Grace is knowing that our debt to our laws and our gods have been canceled in a fraudulent
transaction, and accepting it, welcoming it, not stepping up to pay it anyway, trying to make it
right.
Grace is wrong, just plain wrong. We accept its wrongness and illogic, and we are grateful.
Grace is knowing we won’t experience the repercussions of what has gone before.
Grace is being given a new identity in the moment -- our past identity vanished, untraceable, our
future identity irrelevant.
Grace finds us untouched by all the wrongs we have pronounced upon ourselves, and leaves us
there, free of them.
Grace violates our moral and ethical and legal sense of obligation. And irrefutably satisfies them
all.
Grace is the freedom we did not deserve, a permanent excuse from the standard we could never
satisfy. We did not deserve to live under it: it was a lie, a deception, a vapor, a ghost.
Grace -- the lawbreaker, the giver of freedom, the source of life.

                                            The Crux


The crux is the melted cored of immanence.
It is the center of our own cosmology, the center of all things us. Not ego asserting control, but
us experiencing, embodying, embracing, incarnating our perfect selves.
We can’t help but notice. What was that bit about “perfect”?
The short definition of perfect: not here, not us, not now.
The expanded definition of perfect: perfect in every imperfection.
Perfect is not a status quo to which we aspire, to which we might arrive or that we might slip out
of.
Perfect is the constant shifting tension of life at the crux: where all the contradictions are present
but don’t matter. Where our cosmologies break down, and with them, our urge for orderliness
and law, control and predictability.
All the distinctions we’ve relied upon in our lives of separateness -- good/bad, right/wrong,
sacred/profane, us/them, fair/not fair, just/unjust, seen/unseen, known/unknown, then/now,
heaven/earth, and all the rest -- vanish at the dynamic point of perfection, where paradox and
conflict, contradiction and inconsistency, negation and opposite, possible and impossible all exist
together, without distinction or judgment.
Where none of that matters.
And in the center of the impossible tension of them all, we become possible.

                                Building the Tower (Again)


       Once upon a time, long ago, there was a powerful impulse in the people of the
       world that made them separate from each other. They roamed as tribes of
       nomads, scattered across the face of the Earth, seeking their own places of
       protection and provision.
       Although their habit was to separate from each other, they all spoke the same
       language. They could communicate if their paths crossed.
       One day, some of them realized that speaking the same language gave them
       power. They spoke of doing something great together. They would cease their
       roaming and settle together and become a great city.
       At that time, the people of Earth had learned to build with bricks instead of native
       stone, using tar for mortar. This made it possible for them to build their new city.
       The city founders taught each other the skill, using their common language, and
       began to build. In the middle of their city they began to build a great tower,
       reaching to the heavens -- a monument to their skill, their unity, their common
       language, their power.
       The gods who ruled the Earth saw what the people were doing, and came down
       from Heaven to see the city and the tower. They spoke among themselves and
       said, “If the people of Earth can do this just by speaking the same language, then
       nothing will be impossible for them.”
       The gods destroyed the city and its tower. They put an end to the world’s single
       language and scattered the people, made them roam again over the Earth as they
       had before, but now they spoke separate languages.
       They gods named the ruins “Babel,” because now, when the people tried to speak
       to each other as they had done before, it was as if they were just babbling. Their
       conversations, once clear, became clamor, racket, hubbub.

Before they were scattered, the city builders had a unified ethos, including a unified language.
They built a city where they could dwell, settle into and live their lives on Earth. Plus, the tower
in the center of it would reach to the heavens, where the gods live -- the ultimate source of
meaning, the ultimate why of their lives. They built their tower ultimately not with the new
technology of bricks and mortar, but with their universal language -- the same language,
understood worldwide, able to carry truth and convey meaning they could reckon with on
common terms. Their words delivered the full power of the truth they wished to convey. Nothing
was lost in the translation.
Knowledge is power, and the builders knew it. They built to know what the gods know in
Heaven. They built one word brick upon another, building communal truth that could reach to
Heaven, displace the gods, change world governance. They would rule their own lives, make a
name for themselves. Nothing would be impossible for them. Unified language is that powerful,
that threatening.
The gods had to put a stop to it -- they could, and did. They had power the builders didn’t know.
They descended from Heaven to Earth and destroyed the city, smashed the tower, scattered the
people, confused their language. Never again, they said. Never again this power, invested in
humankind.
     And thus the myth explains metaphorically why we have collective ethos -- separate
cultures, eras, and communities, each with their own characteristic spirit, manifesting their own
beliefs and aspirations. One World Government? No, says the myth. Never. Never again.
The myth speaks to us individually, too, about our drive to build a place in which we can dwell,
and from there a tower, reaching into Heaven itself. We could build as they did in the myth, too,
if our internal language was as unified and pure as theirs. It’s not. Our internal language is
confused -- part of us speaks one language, asserting one truth, while another speaks another
language and another truth, and the result is babble -- unfocused, unproductive noise. We’re
scattered, we wander. We don’t make a name for ourselves. We don’t build the tower. We don’t
do the impossible.
The myth offers consolation, in the form of a warning. Sometimes we reach too high and pay the
price, it says. It’s best to leave the gods’ business to the gods. They’re more powerful. They can
trump our aspirations, bring them crashing down around us. They’re in charge of Judgment Day,
not us, and we do well to remember, bow our heads, and accept our place, grateful for as much
as we’ve got.
We hear, and heed. We have learned not to dismiss myths lightly, that we ignore them at our
own peril. There’s a reason they endure: they’re embedded in our cells, they course through our
veins. They have power to shape collective and individual destinies. If we dare to build, we will
fall under the myth’s judgment, bring the wrath of our own gods down upon us.

                                      The Game Of One


In the dream, I’m standing in a locker room, I’ve reached some kind of milestone, and a voice is
describing a tradition: when you get this far, you play in a hockey game, or you can opt out.
If you opt out, a voice says, “You need to go to Lilly House and reflect on this question: Who is
the world serving by your choice?”
I don’t know how to play hockey. I used to skate when I was a kid, but now? No certainly not
now, or ever, really. And if I could skate, am I well-conditioned enough to play three periods of
hockey? No. Certainly not. The idea is so ludicrous, it’s not even laughable.
I get a brief flash of me, suited up in hockey gear and uniform, skating well, handling the puck,
but then I remember no, there’s no way. I guess I’ll opt out. Playing just isn’t an option. It’s a
silly tradition anyway: if gives me a choice, but really it’s no choice at all.
The coach comes up behind me -- I sense him over my right shoulder -- and slowly and carefully
he repeats the deal: either play in the game or go to Lilly House and reflect on this question:
“Who is the world serving by your choice?”
Such an odd question. Shouldn’t it be, “How am I serving the world by my choice?” He affirms
no, that’s not it, it’s what he said.
The corridor out of the locker room to the west/left is to the hockey game. The one to east/right
is the opt out. I figure I’ll opt out. Like I said….
I wake up. I need to remember this dream. I find my way to the living room in the dark, switch
on the light over my desk, sit down and take notes. It was the Oracle I know as Phineas, giving
me a riddle. I roll my eyes and say out loud, “Thanks a lot!” I go back to bed, wondering.
I wrote about oracles and riddles in my book Life Beyond Reason. Here’s part of that:
       Inspiration asks us to do the impossible, but the impossible waits to be discovered. It’s
       there all the time, with us and within us, in the darkness and the cloud of unknowing,
       until eventually Phineas – or whatever his equivalent for you – steps out from behind the
       veil and begins to unfold the mystery to us.
       We learn that creation starts in chaos, but chaos doesn’t stay chaotic forever. Eventually
       it organizes itself, becomes cosmos, takes on shape and order and structure.
       And we learn that creation doesn’t fire off commands and impose rules and formulas,
       doesn’t promise that if we just follow these 10 steps we’ll get it right.
       Instead, we learn that inspiration is an invitation. “Here – solve this riddle,” it says. “Put
       this puzzle together.”
I lie in bed, wondering what my answer will be to the dream’s riddle. Then I think, it was just a
dream, I’ll go back to it and try out the two choices. I settle into a deep meditative, self-hypnotic
state. I go first to the left.
I skate out onto a brightly lit hockey rink in an empty, darkened arena. I’m in full uniform, the
color of deep twilight. I’m the only player. I see the coach skating to my right. We share a
conspiratorial spark of understanding: there was no game to join, there was only a game if
anyone chose to play it.
The expectation that there was something there -- a game in progress, with other players -- was
an illusion. All that exasperation, thinking it’s a strange tradition, being frustrated with it, all that
trying to picture myself in a game I don’t know how to play, all that thinking I don’t know how
to play so I’ll opt out… it was just an illusion. There was no there there. If anyone shows up,
there’s game; if no one shows up, there isn’t. Simple, but we don’t make it so. We reach for all
the drama and illusion. We believe there’s a reality out there waiting for us to join it. There’s
only us, and the choice we’ve made.
I’m surprised when another guy shows up, late. He’s much smaller than me. His uniform is
white, sharply contrasted with my midnight colors. He looks uncomfortable to be there, unhappy
to see me. I wonder if we’re supposed to play a game, me against him, then I think that’s stupid,
I’m not doing that, and he’s not interested anyway.
I skate off to my left, toward a goal at the far end -- way off in the distance, far longer than an
ordinary hockey rink. I’m unskilled, but I keep the puck moving along on my stick. I look over
my shoulder for the other guy, his end of the rink is now a long ways off. He’s kind of messing
around at his end. I don’t care.
I get to the goal, knock the puck in. The red light on top of the goal cage twirls, the alarm
sounds. I’m surprised it’s live. I scored!
I stop that vision, go back to the locker room, take the opt-out way to the right. I walk into a vast
interior space that reminds me of walking into Denver International Airport on the mezzanine
level, looking down into the atrium below, where the security lines are. The place is packed.
People are jammed shoulder to shoulder, on every level -- all around the mezzanine level I came
in on and in the atrium below.
I realize something and hear a voice tell it to me at the same time: these are all the people who
opted out. They went through the same thought process I did: they didn’t know how to play, it
was a dumb traditional anyway, I guess I’ll opt out…. There were two of us at the rink; there are
thousands here.
Then the dream’s question comes to me: “Who is the world serving by your choice?” I
immediately know the answer: the world is serving itself. It’s keeping everything within its
control, keeping everything neat and in order, maintaining status quo. That’s what draws the
crowd -- we opt into the world’s game because we believe the alternative is a game already
going on that’s out of our reach and ability. We don’t have the skill, the stamina. We can’t play.
Why bother. It’s stupid to try. And so we make the choice that empowers the world to serve
itself, just the way it is.
Meanwhile, the hockey rink is where a new game gets played. Whoever shows up gets to play --
it will be a game of one if only one shows up. Whoever you are, whatever you can do… that’s
enough. You are enough. Just show up, skate out, score the goal, be the hero. Nothing can stop
you, not even your own clumsiness.
Take the left route, put on the twilight-colored uniform: symbolically, the way of the night, of
intuition, of things hidden. Put the uniform on and go behind the veil, into the Mystery. Confront
the Oracle with its own riddle, create the meaning you want.
The rink in the empty auditorium is where the impossible happens. Opt to play the game, and we
get to do the impossible: skate when we can’t; score the apparently meaningless, uncontested
goal that turns out to mean everything.
Either that or make the choice that allows the world to serve itself, where we’re all packed in
together, doing the possible, the expected, the ordinary.
As I leave the dream and the meditation, I wonder how many in that vast crowd did what was
directed: take time to reflect in Lilly House about who’s being served by the crowded, normal,
possible choice.
I sleep heavily the rest of the night. When I get up in the morning, I look up the meaning of the
name “Lilly”: purity, innocence, beauty. I think that, if we could get into that state, we could
find the answer to the question before we make the choice in the locker room. But we don’t solve
the riddle that way; it never occurs to us.
I didn’t solve it that way; I didn’t make the choice in the dream. I solved the riddle another way,
my way, by detaching from the choice long enough to watch it play out before I made it. That
option wasn’t given in the dream, but I created it upon awakening. My mindful meditation gave
me a second chance to make the choice once I saw how both options would play out. It did so
without judgment.
I feel grateful. I came this close to joining the crowd. Look at what I would have missed.

we’ll need to remove MS not just from individual bodies but from the collective ethos as well.

Plus, if I engage in the inner game of running marathons at the ethos level, and succeed in
eliminating MS as a reality in my life and win a marathon to prove it, then suddenly the human
race is capable of way more than we used to think. Turns out it wasn’t just me doing something
for myself, it was for the rest of us, too.
We can’t play the ethos game small, because it’s not.
If we play ethos at all, we play big.
Ethos isn’t just a new inner game, it’s The Game Changer.

                                       Apocalypse Now


Theoretically, we could change our inner game because we want to, but usually it takes an
Apocalypse. No, not the end of the world, just the end of the world as we have known it -- which
is pretty much the same thing.
The essence of post-apocalyptic life is its impossibility. What lies on the Other Side of Over is a
world beyond our comprehension.
Post-apocalyptic life requires decisions and commitments, actions and outcomes in an setting
where all bets are off, all rules, judgments, and beliefs are canceled. On some evolutionary level
so primal we’re not in touch with it, we need to adapt or die, crawl and scramble our way out of
the ooze yet one more time.
That’s why post-apocalyptic life needs a new inner game, and needs it NOW.
Apocalypse offers staggering opportunities -- such as doing things we used to think were
impossible. Doing impossible things must be part of the package: to live in post-apocalyptic
times, we need the ability to be and do what we haven’t been and done before, under conditions
we’ve never faced before.
Apocalyptic times are messy, chaotic, threatening, confusing, unsafe. We need something
powerful to keep us steady, help us find our way when there is no way. Aspiring to do
impossible things gives us that kind of powerful grounding. It’s a lightning rod for all the
destructive energy flying around us: it attracts, collects, compresses, and directs it, sends it
shooting not harmlessly in the ground, but into our hearts, splitting the atom at the core of us,
releasing the power we need to make our individual response to the collective invitation to join
the movement. That atom at the core of us is our personal ethos -- the ultimate beginning of
ourselves in this new life we must learn to inhabit.
I plan to write a book about this next year. It’s working title is Apocalypse: Life on the Other
Side of Over.
Just felt like telling you.
                             Three Phases Of The Inner Game


To get all the way to ethos, we pass through three phases of the inner game of being human. At
least, that’s how it worked for me, and from what I can tell, my experience is pretty typical.
The inner game begins in ego. Here’s how I introduced ego in Life Beyond Reason:
       [Ego] makes us who we are: the dynamic organizing principle that gives our lives
       psychic shape and physical expression, that creates and sustains who we are, what we do,
       and what we have.
       We formed an ego around ourselves when we were young, to make us feel safe in a scary
       world. Ego gives us our sense of self, creates boundaries that differentiate us from others.
       It’s the summation of belief and behavior that shapes our habitual experience of life. It
       accounts for how we make decisions, our likes and dislikes, our areas of competence and
       ignorance, and a whole lot more. Ego draws our experience of reality into orbit around
       itself, defines for us what’s normal and what’s not, what’s safe and possible and
       predictable and what isn’t.
Ego gets a lot of bashing, but we need to give it some credit. It’s fully formed in our first four
years of life, and its job is to ensure our survival in a scary world. As we differentiate into our
separate identities, ego is responsible for our drive and development, accomplishments and
achievement, our approach to life.
The second phase of the inner game moves us beyond ego. We could do that for other reasons,
but we usually it takes a personal Apocalypse: some kind of trauma. accident or illness, financial
or business crash, relationship breakup, job loss….
Whatever the cause, moving beyond ego is still ego-centric. Ego has been our center of gravity a
long time, we’re used to its hold on us, and we feel it even as we’re breaking free. It’s like we
blasted into space, orbited Earth awhile, fired some rockets to break free of its gravity, and now
we’re headed to Mars and past the point of no return, but it still takes awhile to get ourselves
fully around all the idea of a non-Earth centric life. We’re still emotionally attached to what we
left behind.
But one day we get it. It finally sinks in that we really are past the point of no return, there really
is no turning back. We’re now at the Ultimate Impossibility: the need to create an entirely new
sense of identity and life for ourselves in a world we thought we could imagine but it turns out
we were wrong. We can’t imagine. The whole thing is impossible, and we can’t imagine
impossible.
We’re now entering the third phase: life beyond ego.
Moving beyond ego must take us to the threshold of impossibility, because once we cross it ego
will be finally dismantled. Ego has long defined what’s possible or not. But press on further, and
there’s no way to tell. Ego is suddenly hopelessly out of its league, forever off its game.
This is when Apocalypse asserts itself. There is no longer any reality but the end of reality as we
have always known it. Meaning, purpose, value… all those things ego has given us are now a
ruined currency we can’t trade with anymore.
Ego gave us order, predictability, stability to hold our world together. But now, anarchy is the
post-apocalypse default setting (which is why dystopian stories always begin after an
apocalypse, and why authoritarian rule rushes to fill the void). Anarchy is where our beliefs and
aspirations come unglued, lose their grip on both our internal sense of place and purpose and the
external relationships, practices, and institutions that hold our lives together. The world falls
apart as we watch in dismay, helpless and hopeless.
We’re talking about the soul’s journey here, and apocalypse and anarchy happen on the inside.
Our souls watch the destruction of our ego-based beliefs and aspirations. “This was never
supposed to happen” becomes the rule of the day. All judgment is suspended; everything is fair
game for destruction. There is no impossible because all is suddenly possible, in the scariest way.
This is the phase in which that urge to do something about it is given the controls, and the
opportunity and responsibility of what it might create with its new power is overwhelming, to the
point where our gravest threat is self-annihilation -- the suddenly very real possibility that we
might destroy ourselves and our world.
If we survive, we find a new way of framing reality that seems to be forged out of runaway
individualism and anarchy, but in truth is characterized by the simultaneous presence of all the
contradictions that were there all along but ego wasn’t equipped to handle.
The transition can be too much. Too much apocalypse too fast can take us out. Sometimes it
does; our souls can’t hold it; they separate from our bodies; our incarnation is over.
I make entering this third phase sound dire because it is, and knowing that might help us when
we go there, or if we’re there already. If we can last -- which often is what it means to survive --
we’ll eventually discover that we’re no longer driven by trauma (the violation of belief) or
inspiration (taking on new aspirations), but by an energy which transcends and informs them
both: the energy of ethos, which now throbs full force as we reach the core of ourselves. We’ve
been shielding our ethos with our ego-based practices for a long time. Now, the shield is off, the
life force in us is unleashed in its rawness.
My personal Apocalypse came in waves: crisis, followed by time to recover and assimilate,
followed by new crisis. That also seems to be typical, and that, too, is a mercy.

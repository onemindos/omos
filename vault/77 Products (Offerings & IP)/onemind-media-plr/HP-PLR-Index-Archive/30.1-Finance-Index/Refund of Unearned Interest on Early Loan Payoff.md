Refund of Unearned Interest on Early Loan Payoff
Under U.S. law, if a borrower pays off a consumer loan early, the lender generally must return any
“unearned” interest – that is, interest for the remaining period that the borrower will no longer use.
This principle is embodied in federal law (Truth in Lending Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1615), which states that when a
consumer prepays a loan in full, the creditor “shall promptly refund any unearned portion of the
interest charge” 1 . The only exception is if the amount to be refunded is less than $1, in which case no
refund is required 2 . Below we break down how this requirement applies to different loan types, and note
any specific rules or exceptions (including the prohibition of the “Rule of 78’s” interest calculation for long-
term loans 3 ).


Mortgage Loans
Most mortgage loans in the U.S. are structured with simple interest accruing daily, meaning interest is
calculated on the outstanding balance up to the payoff date. If you pay off a mortgage before the end of its
term, you simply stop incurring interest from that day forward. Federal law requires that lenders cannot
keep interest for periods beyond the payoff – any unearned interest must be credited back or not
charged at all 1 . In practice, this means when you request a payoff on a mortgage, the payoff amount is
calculated through the date of payment, and no extra interest after that date is owed by the borrower.


Importantly, charging interest beyond the payoff date is treated as a prepayment penalty, which is
largely disallowed on most consumer mortgages. For instance, FHA (Federal Housing Administration)
loans historically charged interest for the entire month in which the loan was paid off (even if you paid early
in the month). A HUD rule effective January 21, 2015 ended that practice, prohibiting lenders from
charging interest past the actual payoff date 4 . After that change, FHA loans (and most other home
loans) only accrue interest up to the day the loan is paid in full, aligning with the requirement to refund or
forgo any unearned interest. Conventional mortgages (loans from banks or lenders not insured by FHA)
also generally do not have interest past payoff; if a conventional loan did include such charges, it would
likely violate the Truth in Lending Act’s mandate to refund unearned interest 1 .


Prepayment penalties on mortgages are a separate issue from unearned interest. While prepayment fees
(explicit penalties) are allowed on some mortgages (typically only in the first 2–3 years and with clear
disclosure, and prohibited entirely on many types of loans by Dodd-Frank rules), those are contractual fees
and not “unearned interest”. Unearned interest refers to interest that would have been paid in the future
but is no longer earned because the loan ended early. In summary, for mortgage loans any interest for
the remaining term is not owed once you pay off the loan, and lenders must ensure they don’t retain
such interest. The $1 de minimis rule applies here as well – if the amount of leftover interest is negligible
(under one dollar), the lender isn’t required to issue a refund 2 (often this is a non-issue because payoff
figures are calculated precisely).

Auto Loans
Auto loans (often structured as retail installment contracts) are also covered by the federal rule requiring
refund of unearned interest on early payoff 1 . In the past, many auto loans were made with
precomputed interest – the total interest for the full term was calculated upfront and built into fixed
installment payments. If such a loan is paid off ahead of schedule, there will be a portion of that
precomputed finance charge that was not actually “earned” yet. By law, the lender must rebate that
unearned portion to the borrower (usually by reducing the payoff balance or issuing a refund) 1 . For
example, if you took a 5-year car loan but paid it off in 3 years, you should not have to pay the interest for
the remaining 2 years; any interest beyond the payoff date should be removed from what you owe.


Federal law also restricts how lenders calculate the interest rebate, particularly for longer auto loans.
Lenders historically used the “Rule of 78’s” (also known as the sum-of-the-digits method) to calculate
refunds on precomputed loans. The Rule of 78’s front-loads the interest in the early months of the loan,
resulting in a smaller refund to the borrower if the loan is paid early (because under that formula, a larger
share of interest is considered “earned” in the beginning) 5 6 . This method is now illegal for long-term
loans: since 1992, federal law has prohibited using the Rule of 78’s for any consumer loan with a term
over 61 months 3 . In other words, for auto loans longer than five years, the refund of unearned
interest must be calculated by a method at least as favorable to the consumer as the actuarial
(simple interest) method 3 . Practically, this means an auto loan of, say, 72 months must use a pro-rata
method to refund interest, rather than the old Rule of 78’s which is less generous to the borrower. (Many
states even ban the Rule of 78’s for shorter loans or altogether, as it’s widely viewed as unfair to
consumers paying off early   7   .)


Today, most auto loans are originated as simple interest loans rather than precomputed interest loans 8
  9 . In a simple-interest auto loan, interest accrues on the outstanding balance daily, and if you pay off

early, you simply pay less total interest (there isn’t a “prepaid” interest to refund; you just stop accruing
further interest). But if an auto loan was a precomputed contract, the lender is obligated to credit you
for the unearned portion of the finance charge when you payoff ahead of schedule. State laws reinforce
this: virtually all states require an interest rebate on precomputed vehicle loans paid off early, often using
either the Rule of 78’s or a statutory actuarial formula. For instance, New York law specifies that if a bank
takes interest in advance on an installment loan and the debt is paid off prior to maturity, the bank “shall
refund to the obligor ... the unearned interest” that was previously collected 10 . In short, for auto loans,
the borrower is entitled to a refund or credit for any interest for time they didn’t borrow the money.
The only exception would be trivial amounts under $1, per the federal de minimis rule (many state laws also
have a $1 threshold for refunds, similarly to the federal rule).


Personal Loans
Personal loans (such as unsecured installment loans from a bank or finance company) follow the same
basic principle: when you prepay the loan in full, any unearned interest must be returned. Personal
loans are consumer credit transactions, so 15 U.S.C. § 1615’s refund requirement applies to them just as
it does to auto loans or mortgages 1 . If the personal loan is a simple interest loan, paying it off early
will mean you stop accruing interest and your final payoff will include interest only up to the payoff date. If
the loan was made with precomputed interest (add-on interest), then at payoff the lender must calculate
the interest you’ve saved by paying early and refund it to you (often by deducting it from the

outstanding balance when you request a payoff quote). In all cases, you shouldn’t have to pay interest for
time beyond when you actually repaid the principal.


State laws also address early payoff of personal installment loans. Many states have adopted provisions
from the Uniform Consumer Credit Code (UCCC) or similar laws, which explicitly give consumers the right
to prepay at any time and receive a refund of unearned charges. As an example, New York’s banking law
(for licensed lenders and banks) says that if interest is taken in advance (discounted or precomputed) and
the loan is paid off or accelerated early, the lender must refund the unearned portion of interest to the
borrower 10 . Typically, the refund is calculated by a method set in statute or regulation. (Before federal
preemption, some state laws allowed using the Rule of 78’s for these refunds, especially for shorter-term
loans, while others required the actuarial method. Now, however, any state law that would permit a less
favorable method on loans over 61 months is overridden by the federal standard requiring the actuarial
method 7 3 .)


In practice, many personal loan lenders today (including banks, credit unions, and online lenders) charge
interest on a simple interest basis, meaning your interest is calculated periodically on the remaining
balance. These lenders often advertise “no prepayment penalty,” which indicates not only that there’s no fee
for early payoff but also that you will not be charged any remaining interest once you pay the loan off. If
you happen to overpay or if an automatic payment overshoots the payoff amount, the excess (which could
include a bit of unearned interest) is typically refunded to you. The bottom line is that for personal loans,
consumers are entitled to avoid or get back any interest for the unused portion of the loan term – by
law you only pay for the time you actually had the loan. The $1 minimum rule applies here as well, so
extremely small amounts might not be refunded 2 , but otherwise lenders must be prompt in returning
unearned finance charges.


Student Loans
Student loans come in two broad categories – federal student loans and private student loans – and both
categories allow early payoff without the borrower paying extra interest. In fact, prepayment penalties are
outright prohibited on all student loans by law. Federal student loans have, since the inception of the
program, allowed borrowers to “accelerate repayment of the whole or any part of the loan” without
penalty (language from the Higher Education Act of 1965) 11 . This means you can pay a federal loan off
early and you will only pay the interest that has accrued up to that point – there is no charge for the
remaining term’s interest. Private student loans became subject to a similar rule in 2008, when the Higher
Education Opportunity Act amended TILA to ban prepayment penalties on private education loans 12 .
Thus, no student loan lender (federal or private) can impose a fee or forfeiture of interest for paying
off early 13 .


With federal student loans, interest accrues daily but is typically capitalized only at specific times (e.g. after
deferment periods or for unsubsidized loans). If you pay off the loan in full, the servicer will calculate the
interest up to the payoff date and that’s all you owe. If you somehow paid more than the payoff amount
(e.g. if an autopayment overshot), the excess, including any unearned interest, would be refunded to you.
Federal regulations explicitly forbid any unearned interest beyond the payoff; in fact, federal loans don’t
even allow precomputed interest – they operate on a simple interest accrual system by design. Private
student loans generally work the same way: interest accrues over time on the balance. When you request a
payoff quote, it usually includes interest up to a certain good-through date. Paying by that date means no
further interest is charged. Because of the federal laws mentioned, private lenders must also credit any

interest that was factored in beyond the payoff date (in practice, most don’t factor in beyond that date
anyway, aside from perhaps a day or two of interest “cushion”). The Truth in Lending Act’s general rule on
unearned interest refunds applies to private student loans as a type of consumer credit transaction, but
since these loans cannot have precomputed, front-loaded interest or penalties, the main effect is simply
that interest stops when the loan is paid.


It’s worth noting that with student loans (especially federal loans), borrowers are often encouraged to pay
more than the minimum or to pay off early to save on interest. Doing so indeed saves you all the future
interest that would have accrued, and there is no catch – the lender doesn’t get to keep that foregone
interest. In summary, student loan borrowers are always entitled to avoid paying unaccrued interest if
they accelerate payment, and any interest payment that was made but not actually earned (for example, if
you paid a month’s payment in advance and then paid the loan off before that month ended) would be
refunded or applied to principal. Both federal statutes and regulations ensure no interest beyond the
payoff date can be collected on student debt 13 .


Conclusion – When Are You Entitled to a Refund of Interest? In all of the above loan types, the common
rule is that you only owe interest for the time you had the loan, and if you end the loan early, any
interest for the remaining term is either not charged or must be returned. Thanks to 15 U.S.C. § 1615 and
various state laws, consumers have the right to prepay without losing money on unearned interest. This
generally applies to all consumer loans – mortgages, car loans, personal loans, and private student loans
– with the only caveats being: (1) if the calculated refund is tiny (less than $1) the law doesn’t require the
refund 2 ; and (2) lenders can charge bona fide prepayment fees on certain loans if allowed by law and
contract, but they still cannot keep unearned interest as an additional gain. The “Rule of 78’s” once
allowed lenders to keep more interest on early payoff of some installment loans, but its use is now heavily
restricted or banned (federally prohibited for loans over 61 months) 3 7 . Consumers who pay off their
loans early should therefore either see a reduced final payoff amount reflecting the interest saved or
receive a refund for any interest that ended up being paid but not earned. All relevant laws and regulatory
guidance make it clear that unearned interest belongs to the borrower once the debt is discharged.
